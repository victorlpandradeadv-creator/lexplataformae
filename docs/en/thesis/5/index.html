5 On the legal regime of the Immaterial Object of Consumption (IOC) under the Lex Plataformae

Epigraph

K/DA, Villain (2020), in dialogue with Scarlet Envy, RuPaul’s Drag Race: All Stars 6 Promo (2021). What it announces · Read the epigraph in the PDF.

p. 391

The legal problem of continuous digital consumption calls for parameters of qualification, proof and attribution that neither depend on material analogies nor stop at the contractual text. The dogmatic task is to make consumer protection operational in an environment in which enjoyment is modulated by private architecture, rules and enforcement, and in which unilateral changes can degrade value and delivery without formally suppressing access.

In this register, the reconstruction of the immaterial object of consumption is consolidated as a concretization of the “immaterial product” provided for in Article 3, paragraph 1, of the Consumer Protection Code. The decisive point is that, in platform ecosystems, the object does not present itself as a stabilized thing, but as a bundle of functional and symbolic enjoyment that depends on regimes of access, visibility, updating and governance. For this reason, adequate protection requires recognizing two complementary dimensions. Functional integrity, linked to performance, to the conditions of use and to the utility actually delivered. And symbolic integrity, linked to belonging, reputation, distinction and promises of identity mediated by signs and by the environment's internal equivalences, when these elements form part of the performance as offered and perceived.

The consumer-law reconstruction of the IOC also requires a check on the nominalism that separates “product” from “service” by label, without reaching the substance of governed enjoyment. To this end, Rizzatto Nunes402 is drawn on to support a sufficiently broad reading of the concept of product in the CDC, capable of encompassing borderline zones in continuous and mediated arrangements, without dissolving criteria. This broadening has no rhetorical function. It serves to preserve the application of the consumer regime when delivery takes place as an enjoyment bundle dependent on architecture, and not as an isolable material object.

Conceptual discipline and the intersubjective controllability of attributions are treated as a condition of validity of the proposed regime. Thus, Karl Larenz403, Tércio Sampaio Ferraz p. 392 Jr.404 and Humberto Ávila405 are drawn on as methodological checks against elastic categories that explain everything and prove nothing, above all in matters of evidentiary difficulty and of objectively confronting the technical opacity of platforms.

Along the same axis, Claudia Lima Marques406 establishes vulnerability as a structural feature of the consumer relationship and as a guide for protection in long-term relationships; Ricardo Morishita Wada407 dispels the illusion that volume of information restores autonomy, requiring qualified intelligibility, comparability and practical usefulness; Miguel Reale408 offers the key to integrating facts, values and norms, making it possible to bring regularities of the technical environment back to legal categories without voluntarism; and Roberto Mangabeira Unger409 comes in at specific points to provide grounds for resisting the institutional fatalism that mistakes contingent arrangements for technical imperatives and to reinforce bringing governance by architecture back into the field of what can be protected by Law.

The Lex Plataformae is treated here as the contemporary form of practical normativity exercised by protocols, flows, interfaces, sanctions and private adjudication procedures that organize consumption within an environment. The consumer bond comes to be p. 393 governed not only by clauses but by design and continuous execution, with structural asymmetries of contestation and proof. This shifts the problem of abusiveness beyond the textual content of the contract and calls for criteria capable of attributing liability for architectural decisions that downgrade enjoyment, alter equivalences and reorganize the consumer's experience even when there is no traditionally identifiable “defect” in a product or service.

In this sense, abusive teleology is stabilized as a controllable unlawful act of architecture. This is not an effort to prohibit changes, technical evolution or the necessary internal rebalancing of digital systems. The issue is recognizing the need to distinguish legitimate change, compatible with good faith, transparency and social function, from change that diverts the purpose of the performance and turns governance into a mechanism for the extraction and unilateral recomposition of value, with a relevant downgrading of the enjoyment bundle. Abusive teleology appears when a technical, contractual or enforcement mutation reorganizes delivery so as to preserve the nominal existence of the object while degrading its functional or symbolic substance, exploiting the asymmetry of controllability and the consumer's dependence on the environment.

To allow institutional control and minimally intersubjective proof, two operational parameters are then set. First, open auditability, characterized by the requirement that changes relevant to enjoyment be traceable and intelligible, including access rules, modulations of delivery and sanction mechanisms, so that what opaque systems produce becomes contestable. And, immediately after, functional value equivalence, as a criterion for assessing whether the change preserves the content delivered in terms of the promised utility and experience, preventing formal substitutions from being used to cover up substantial losses. These parameters work as comparison grids to distinguish legitimate evolution from abusive practice, without requiring technical freezing or ignoring the dynamic nature of the object.

The dialogue with the legislative framework of intellectual property rights and with the Brazilian constitutional-economic order enters as a structural element of the regime, since exclusivities, interfaces and access regimes act as an infrastructure of power and must therefore be read within the limits of good faith, transparency, social function and controllability. When enjoyment depends on proprietary grammars and on unilaterally administered access conditions, exclusivity is not merely a legal title. It becomes a condition of governance, with a direct impact on the content delivered and on the consumer's position in the ecosystem. Legal control, therefore, must reach the way this infrastructure is used to modulate value, impose frictions, degrade equivalences and administer permanence.

p. 394 The ultimate objective is to deliver an applicable regime: criteria for qualifying the IOC, identifying the relevant injury in unilateral mutations and private sanctions, structuring proof of the degradation of the enjoyment bundle and attributing abusiveness in a controllable way, with merely illustrative and referenced use of examples from the empirical laboratory where indispensable to establishing intelligibility, without any claim to autonomous demonstration. This set prepares the conclusions and the guidelines for institutional protection, shifting the debate from “inevitable change” to verifiable standards of legitimacy in the governance of continuous consumption.

Notes

  1. Doctor of Philosophy of Law and livre-docente in Consumer Law from the Pontifical Catholic University of São Paulo (PUC-SP), Luiz Antonio Rizzatto Nunes is a Brazilian jurist and professor with a distinguished career in Consumer Law and in the São Paulo judiciary. ↩
  2. Doctor of Law with habilitation from the Georg-August-Universität zu Göttingen, Karl Larenz was a German jurist of great influence on Civil Law dogmatics and on twentieth-century legal methodology. He had a long teaching career, with notable periods at Kiel and, from 1960, at München, becoming a reference for systematically organizing problems of interpretation, dogmatic construction and the structuring of obligations in private law. ↩
  3. Doctor of Philosophy from the Johannes Gutenberg-Universität Mainz and Doctor of Law from the Faculty of Law of the University of São Paulo, under the supervision of Miguel Reale, Tércio Sampaio Ferraz Jr. is a Brazilian jurist and legal philosopher recognized for his contribution to legal theory and the philosophy of law, in close dialogue with language, pragmatics and legal decision-making. He was a full professor in the Department of Philosophy of Law at FDUSP and remains active academically and institutionally in legal theory, dogmatics and legal methodology. ↩
  4. Doctor of Law from the Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München, Master of Public Law from UFRGS and livre-docente in Tax Law from the University of São Paulo, Humberto Bergmann Ávila is a Brazilian jurist recognized for his contribution to legal theory and public law, with an emphasis on the theory of norms, argumentation and tax law. ↩
  5. Doctor of Law (summa cum laude) from the Ruprecht-Karls-Universität Heidelberg, with a master's in Law from the Eberhard-Karls-Universität Tübingen and specialization at the Universität des Saarlandes, Claudia Lima Marques is a Brazilian jurist and professor recognized as one of the leading authorities on Consumer Law in the country, in close dialogue with European and German Law. She is a full professor at the UFRGS Faculty of Law and works on consumer protection agendas at the national and transnational levels, including as chair of the Committee on International Protection of Consumers of the International Law Association (ILA). ↩
  6. Doctor of Law from PUC-SP and Master of Civil Law from USP, Ricardo Morishita Wada is a Brazilian jurist with a distinguished career in Consumer Law, combining an academic path with significant service in public administration. He was director of the Department of Consumer Protection and Defense (DPDC) of the Ministry of Justice from 2003 to 2010 and later worked as a professor and research coordinator in Consumer Law at the Center for Justice and Society of FGV Direito Rio (2010-2014). ↩
  7. Doctor of Law from the Faculty of Law of the University of São Paulo, Miguel Reale was a Brazilian jurist, legal philosopher and public intellectual whose work had a direct impact on the general theory of law and on the shaping of legal dogmatics in the country. He held the chair of Philosophy of Law at USP and twice served as Rector of the University of São Paulo (1949-1950 and 1969-1973). ↩
  8. Roberto Mangabeira Unger is a Brazilian social philosopher, jurist and public intellectual, with a law degree from the Federal University of Rio de Janeiro and graduate degrees in Law from Harvard (LL.M. and S.J.D.), where he became a professor and went on to hold the chair of Roscoe Pound Professor of Law at Harvard Law School. Associated with the Critical Legal Studies movement in the United States, his work combines legal theory, social theory and political economy, focusing on the critique of “false necessity” and on the idea that institutions and social arrangements are historically contingent artifacts, open to revision and reconstruction. In Brazilian institutional life, he served in government as Minister-Chief of the Secretariat of Strategic Affairs of the Presidency (2007- 2009 and 2015). ↩
