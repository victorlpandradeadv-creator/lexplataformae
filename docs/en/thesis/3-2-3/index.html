3.2.3 Value extraction, artificial scarcity and the consumer as incorporated exterior

p. 208

The discussion of technofeudalism and digital rentierism then moves to the operational level, prompted by a central question: how can platforms extract value from ontologically non-scarce goods and, at the same time, stabilize positions of power that are analogous to feudal lordship?

Under the IOC regime, the business model tends to depend on deliberately engineered scarcity and risk, turning enjoyment into a toll circuit and permanence into a practical obligation. The platform does not profit merely by “selling”, but by governing access, modulating the experience and making exit costly.

When technical reproduction is cheap and availability could be expanded, income is sustained not by natural rarity but by produced rarefaction, which shifts the center of gravity of economic power. Instead of competing chiefly on productive efficiency or on the intrinsic quality of a good, what comes to matter is control over gateways, protocols, p. 209 interfaces, catalogs and authorization regimes, that is, control over the very symbolic and technical infrastructure that organizes consumption.

In the immaterial domain, technical reproducibility lowers marginal cost and shifts the conflict to control of access. For this reason, when persistent unavailability, exclusivity and “last chance” are observed, the decisive fact is not natural rarity but rarefaction by design, sustained by authorization, catalog and visibility regimes that turn abundance into a toll.

In the domain of immaterial goods, subjection intensifies because technical reproduction dissolves the limit that traditionally contained economic power, namely physical rarity. With digital objects, the marginal cost of reproduction tends to fall drastically and, in theory, availability could expand without requiring productive capacity comparable to that of the factory, logistics and the extraction of inputs. For this reason, when a persistent regime of unavailability, exclusivity and last chance is observed in these markets, what one faces is not a natural condition of the object but a deliberate institutional choice, so that scarcity appears not as a fact but as a project.

As a consequence, the platform no longer monetizes only the delivery of a good and comes to monetize control over access and time. It does not sell a “thing”; it sells passage, permanence and predictability in an environment that it governs itself. And for this government to generate income, friction must be reintroduced where technology would produce abundance. At this point, artificial scarcity and rentierism cease to be mere marketing and productivity tactics and become structural operators of the business model itself.

The platform creates windows, rotations, tiers, seasonal events, passes, countdowns, limited bundles and conditional acquisition mechanisms. It then turns this arrangement into a narrative, speaking of “rarity”, “exclusive”, “last chance”, “collectible” and “special edition”. This narrative is in turn operationalized by interfaces and rules that make the user's experience dependent on continuous attention. The non-scarce is made scarce by design and, in the process, the foundation of a rent economy is consolidated.

This regime of rarefaction by design finds its most direct economic translation in the diagnosis of informational rentierism. What is monetized is the authorization to enter, to remain, to accelerate, to stand out and not to lose. The platform, therefore, does not operate merely as a supplier of goods, since it operates as the turnstile of an environment in which consumers retain value only if they accept cycles of repeated charges and shifting terms. It is p. 210 at this point that artificial scarcity ceases to be ornament and becomes an engine, since it continuously manufactures the practical conditions for the toll to seem natural.

At the microarchitectural level, this shows up clearly in continuous digital services, especially in the games-as-a-service ecosystem. Acquisition no longer means the stabilization of a good beyond the supplier's reach and comes to mean enjoyment conditioned by governance. The consumer does not buy a “thing” and take it home; they enter an access regime in which the object remains functional only within an infrastructure they do not control, cut across by updates, balancing changes, the closing of events, catalog rotation, reconfigurations of in-game currency and policy changes. The platform monetizes access in layers: it charges for entry, for acceleration, for convenience, for symbolic differentiation, for return and, above all, for mitigating the risk that it manufactures itself.

Thus, the informational rentierism described by Dowbor (2024) finds its microarchitectural counterpart in the everyday life of platforms. The toll does not present itself only as an entry price. It reappears as a repeated charge for permanence, organized around time, attention and the predictability of cycles. In the industrial economy, the consumer pays to acquire a good whose existence, once purchased, tends to stabilize beyond the supplier's reach, at least within reasonable expectations of durability and of the rules on defects.

The critical point is that this model needs its own grammar of risk. In classical markets, the relevant risk is the risk of defect, malfunction, lack of safety or objective breach of the offer. Here, the efficient risk is a different one: the risk of missing the timing, of missing the window, of losing status, of “falling behind”, of watching symbolic value evaporate and, of course, the risk of losing functional value within the ecosystem. It is an engineering of opportunity and belonging, and its consumer is compelled not by the lack of the good itself, but by the threat that their position within the environment will be discontinued.

This alters the legally relevant object of the relationship. Coercion appears neither as violence, nor as direct prohibition, nor as an explicit imposition to buy; it appears as functional and semiotic dependence, built by design. The platform governs scarcity over time, and by governing time it governs the economic meaning of the object. The value of what was “acquired” comes to depend on cycles, calendars, rotations and unilateral rules. Consumption, in this register, becomes the management of anxiety. Its experience is organized so that continuous presence seems a rational requirement rather than a capture.

p. 211 Once rentierism is understood as a logic of recurring extraction, its operational mechanism in continuous enjoyment remains to be spelled out. Under the proposed hypothesis and empirical laboratory, extraction depends not primarily on raising “upfront” prices but on managing risk and scarcity as an architecture of conduct, producing an economy in which the consumer decides under time pressure, under threat of symbolic loss and under structural opacity as to the future of the catalog and the rules. The economic diagnosis then becomes a legal axis, since the platform does not merely offer, it governs, and governs by design.

The manufacture of risk is central to understanding why artificial scarcity is not a marketing detail but a technique of power. In environments of continuous enjoyment, the most effective risk is not the risk that the product “does not work”, which would belong to a classical grammar of quality defect through inadequacy. The decisive risk is the risk of missing an opportunity, of falling behind, of not belonging, of losing status, of seeing the desired object go out of circulation and of having the symbolic identity built in the ecosystem depreciate.

This risk is not natural. The platform calibrates it through a temporal architecture, with seasons, splits, metas and missions. It is also calibrated through an offer architecture, with rotation, scheduled exclusions and variable catalogs. To these are added a reward architecture, with progression systems and grind, and a sanctioning architecture, with bans, restrictions, automated penalties and opaque policies. Users find themselves compelled to follow, to return and to keep up with the ecosystem, on pain of symbolic and material-digital loss.

The temporal architecture that manufactures urgency does not operate only through seasons and rotation, since it turns into discipline when the bond itself is formatted as a circuit of tasks. Missions are the operational name of this conversion. They push consumption up against labor by making permanence a requirement to “earn” what will later be presented as a reward or as a right to unlock. The command appears not as an authoritarian order, but as an infantilized phrase of social contract: do something, get something. In terms of political economy, it is the typical gesture of turning time into currency without ever calling it labor, and of turning engagement into value without ever calling it production Riot Games(Riot Games, 2017d)227.

p. 212 The contract does not leave this mechanism at the level of the rhetoric of “events” and “fun”. It builds it into the very grammar of acquiring virtual goods by stating that access to Game Currency and Virtual Content may come through purchase, receipt or earning, and that such “earning” may result from completing missions or tasks. The platform therefore formally recognizes a path of acquisition that runs not through direct payment but through the fulfillment of internal objectives, objectives defined by the supplier itself, with criteria and rewards defined by the supplier itself, and whose effectiveness depends on remaining in the ecosystem. This is no minor detail. It is the moment at which the incorporated exterior, which seemed to be merely a “user”, is reconfigured as an operator who works to make enjoyment itself possible, stable and monetizable Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e)228.

When the mission becomes the unit by which time is organized, what is bought and what is “earned” begin to merge in a single circuit. Currency and items no longer appear as simple deliveries, but as prizes conditional on following an agenda. This agenda in turn produces the central effect that Marx describes in diffuse labor, since the consumer is induced to internalize as a need what is ultimately a requirement for the reproduction of command. The game becomes a metagame, and the metagame becomes a mechanism of extraction. Rather than freeing the user, the reward ties them to the cycle, since it presents itself as “progress” and “accumulation” that make sense only while the platform keeps its regimes of equivalence, visibility and distinction alive Riot Games(Riot Games, 2017f)229.

To keep this description from becoming an abstraction, it is enough to note that the ecosystem's own interface dictionary records missions as objectives with criteria, progress tracking and rewards, varying with events and seasons. The consumer, then, does not merely play. They carry out a schedule for producing numbers, presence and compliance, whose social form is that of an obligation disguised as choice. Diffuse productivity becomes routine. And routine becomes, without scandal, a method of monetization League of Legends Wiki(League of Legends Wiki, [202-?]f).

When this control over the existence and duration of the object is exercised systematically to intensify urgency, dependence and extraction, the problem ceases to be merely p. 213 “contractual freedom” or a “legitimate business model” and becomes one of purpose. The rationality of the system reveals itself in the pattern, not in the episode. The dividing line does not lie in the fact that artificial scarcity exists, nor in the fact that the offer exploits rarity as a sales technique, but in the way scarcity and temporality are mobilized to produce, predictably, frustration of utility, lowering of adequacy or precarization of the functional and positional value that grounded the consumer's consent. If scarcity is produced as an instrument of recurring capture and if access is designed as precarious authorization, the architecture comes to operate against the legitimately expected functional minimum, straining good faith, material transparency and balance. In this framing, the discussion about clauses and policies is not peripheral. It is the visible surface of a teleology embedded in the design of the service. This prepares the dogmatic qualification of the deviation of purpose when the arrangement itself is calibrated to exploit the consumer's inability to audit the equivalence between promise and delivery.

This distinction can be illustrated by contrasting positional scarcity with corrosive scarcity. Selling a commemorative edition or a rare cosmetic item during a short window may manufacture urgency and capture value through opportunity, provided that it leaves the delivery owed intact and gives the consumer clear parameters on duration, form of access, limits of the offer and any future repetition. This is additive rarity, whose effect falls on the consumer's choice without lowering the integrity of what has already been acquired or degrading the experience of those who choose not to buy. Quite different is a design in which the object or the set of advantages already obtained deliberately loses status, power, practical relevance or use value if the consumer does not pay for new layers, does not complete retention routines or does not submit to imposed update cycles, manufacturing default by architecture. In this second scenario, scarcity ceases to be a simple commercial strategy and comes to operate as a mechanism for frustrating adequacy, because maintaining the functional value of the object is turned into a permanent toll, paid in money or in compulsory presence, which reconfigures the contract as a dynamic of unstable permission and reinforces, through the very structure of the design, the supplier's governance asymmetry.

There is, however, an even more commonplace and equally abusive mode, which depends neither on loss of access nor on extreme degradation of functionality, and which manifests itself as positional deception by omission. When the platform sells an item at a high price on the promise of exclusivity, or presents exclusivity as a relevant attribute to justify price and spending, it incorporates this quality into the content of the offer and into the perceived utility of the object. In such cases, the consumer pays not only for aesthetics or immediate p. 214 availability, but for the promised rarity, for the status and for the social distinction that the ecosystem's own symbolic architecture enforces. If, after a period, the same item reappears in an alternative store, for a special currency, in an aggressive promotion or at a significantly lower price, and if the time limit on the exclusivity was not disclosed adequately and understandably at the time of acquisition, the value delivered is retroactively reconfigured, frustrating legitimate expectations and violating transparency. Scarcity here may operate as a device for capturing consent under inadequate information, potentially qualifying as misleading advertising by omission and breach of the offer, in addition to breaking the equivalence between spending and utility delivered as legitimately expected. The legal consequence is not exhausted in subjective discomfort, since the subsequent change to the attribute that justified the price may call for material restoration through the typical remedies of consumer law, by refund, price reduction or equivalent functional compensation, and may also open the way to damages when additional harm arising from the positional deception is shown.

Artificial scarcity thus functions both as an economic operator and as a legal operator. Economically, it turns replicable goods into assets capable of sustaining recurring monetization. Legally, it is frequently anchored in exclusivity rights and in contractual arrangements that authorize unilateral control over the catalog, the rules and the very ontological status of the object, such as revocable licenses, non-transferability and reserved rights of modification, as well as access and interface architectures that make enjoyment an unstable permission. The result is a form of market in which asymmetry is not limited to the supplier's “superior knowledge”. It materializes in the supplier's very capacity to define what exists, for how long, under what conditions and under which narratives, including with respect to positional attributes offered as components of the price. For this reason, artificial scarcity must be understood as part of an architectural teleology. It belongs to a set of design choices aimed at extraction rather than at the simple efficient organization of a naturally limited resource, and it is at this threshold that it ceases to be mere commercial strategy and becomes a legally relevant category for qualifying deviation of purpose when the equivalence between promise and delivery is made opaque or deliberately frustrated.

Architectural teleology manifests itself not only in clauses and policies, but in the way the interface organizes visibility, calculation and risk. It is at this level that the toll becomes material, even when it does not take the form of an explicit charge. Three features tend to recur. The first is the coercive time window, which narrows the space for decision and turns not acting p. 215 into predictable loss. The second is the sanction for absence, in which the mere interruption of use triggers a downgrade of status, access or opportunity, turning permanence into a practical obligation. The third is the opacity of price and equivalence, achieved through intermediate currencies, bundles and conversions that prevent the consumer from comparing costs, measuring spending and auditing, in functional terms, the promise and the delivery. When these features combine, the toll ceases to be a simple marketing strategy and becomes coercion built into the mode of enjoyment.

A recurring example is the battle pass, in which the consumer pays for the chance to unlock items, but actual enjoyment depends on a race against the clock and an imposed volume of engagement that, if not met, turns the initial payment into precarious access and projected loss. The urgency is not incidental. It is manufactured to push the consumer to spend disproportionate screen time, or to make a new purchase to make up for lost pace, so that non-use becomes a designed form of default. Another example is the scheduled downgrading of ranking and visibility for inactivity, which turns absence into punishment and makes anxiety a cost of holding one's position, even when there is no new functional delivery to justify the degradation. A third example is the system of layered virtual currencies, in which purchases are made not in legal tender but in intermediate credits and successive conversions that fragment perception, mask the real price and hinder comparison, reinforcing the inability to audit the equivalence between what is promised and what is delivered. These mechanisms are not mere design embellishments. They are the normal way the toll is collected in continuous immaterial consumption.

The management of this relationship, however, tends to take an even more characteristic form, which helps explain why these mechanisms are so effective. The platform treats the consumer relationship itself as a game in its own right, a metagame running parallel to the main product. The user is there for the matches, for the interaction with others and for the competitive experience of the game, but the interface induces them to take part in a continuous contest to keep access, opportunity and status, now not against other players but against the very structure that governs the environment. It is a game rigged by design, because the supplier is at once referee, rule-maker, scorekeeper and beneficiary of the informational asymmetry. By turning permanence tasks, time windows and progression systems into the language of merit, the platform shifts to the consumer the burden of avoiding predictable losses and normalizes the idea that enjoyment depends on “making an effort” not to lose what has already been acquired or to preserve symbolic value within p. 216 the ecosystem. The house always wins, not out of rhetorical fatalism, but because the governance regime controls the conditions of victory, reconfigures goals, alters equivalences and manages scarcity, time and visibility as instruments of recurring extraction.

These mechanisms are legally relevant because they reconfigure the consumer's decision-making autonomy and the very adequacy of the object, shifting the center of value to conditions of enjoyment that are unstable, unilaterally managed and far from transparent. Scarcity, in this context, is not only the rarity of the item, but the governed rarefaction of time, opportunity and recognition. When the platform designs the experience to produce predictable loss, impossible calculation and dependence on permanence, it turns supply into a dynamic of unstable permission and reinforces the deviation of purpose already noted, because the architecture comes to operate against the legitimately expected utility and against the trust on which the contract rested.

It is from this point on that the IOC category becomes absolutely necessary to ensure intelligibility for the consumer user. In continuous digital services, the immaterial object of consumption is not an isolated good. It is a bundle of functions and signs whose integrity depends on coherence among promise, delivery and the preservation of value over time. When the platform organizes artificial scarcity and risk as pillars of its business model, it shifts consumption into a regime of enjoyment in which the consumer's autonomy is systematically strained by urgency, opacity and identity dependence. The consumer decides under produced conditions. This is not merely informational vulnerability. It is factual semiotic and temporal vulnerability, since the field of choice is pre-formatted and the economic meaning of the object depends on the very environment the supplier governs.

Within this framework, the IOC functions as an operator for reconstructing supply and for scrutinizing the supplier's justificatory discourse. It requires treating as legally relevant variables that, on platforms, tend to be precisely the variables of extraction. These include the durability of functional value, the minimum stability of the conditions of enjoyment, catalog governance, the reasonable predictability of changes and the possibility of auditing what was promised as utility.

If the ecosystem is structured to produce risk, scarcity and dependence as engines of consumption, legal analysis cannot be confined to an episodic reading of a single term or to a snapshot of a patch. What is being qualified is a regime of governed enjoyment. It is the persistence of the mechanism that makes it possible to move from economic description to the identification of p. 217 an operative purpose and, where applicable, to the characterization of the unlawful act of abusive teleology.

Once the decisive risk becomes the risk of losing opportunity and position, the platform gains an instrument of command that does not depend on the isolated quality of a product. The axis shifts from “delivering something” to “retaining someone”. The discipline is not “buy this”; it is “stay available”, “be present”, “follow the cycle”, “don't miss the window”, on pain of devaluing what has been accumulated so far. The coercion is soft but structuring, since it appears as a rationality of permanence.

It is here that the metaphor of technofeudalism leaves the aesthetic plane and becomes a functional description. Power is stabilized not only by price, but by the cost of exit. Leaving means abandoning history, reputation, library, items, progression, emotional investments, social networks and, in some cases, the very ability to take part in communities organized within that ecosystem. The more the object of consumption is governed enjoyment rather than a delivered thing, the more the dependence is on infrastructure, and the more “competition” becomes a contest over lock-in by design rather than over the excellence of the good.

Continuous extraction is not sustained by recurring charges alone, since it depends on a return of value that comes not as price but as activity. Every session, click, match, search, comment, purchase, abandonment, return and comparison keeps the ecosystem alive and feeds metrics, network effects, reputations, hierarchies and engagement cycles. This is more than measurement, because this activity is converted into data and metrics, processed by telemetry and by machine learning models that refine monetization strategies, calibrate reward probabilities and modulate matchmaking and recommendation algorithms, adjusting the very functional architecture of the Immaterial Object of Consumption.

The platform is not merely selling an experience, since it turns presence into an asset, valuable both economically, in revenue, valuation and market power, and politically, in the capacity to influence conduct and structure meanings. The user continuously tests, legitimizes and improves the system, supplying behavioral feedback on a massive scale, without any legal recognition of this diffuse labor.

The institutionalization of the Public Beta Environment (Public Beta Environment, PBE) makes this diffuse labor visible in its most literal form. The ecosystem creates a “downstairs” for experimentation, where the community is called on to test future versions, to put up with instability, to report bugs and to expose, at scale, behaviors that serve as a dress rehearsal for what will be launched in the main environment. What is presented p. 218 as an opportunity to “help” and “try new features” is, in practice, the material incorporation of the consumer into the production process, not as end recipient but as a calibration component. Here the user is not only measured; they are mobilized as a distributed workforce that lowers the cost of quality control, widens the surface for bug detection and feeds the supplier's organizational intelligence, generating a productivity that appears not as labor but as participation Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024j)230.

This incorporation can be read more precisely by observing how the contract treats the informational core of this same circuit. In the Unsolicited Idea Submission Policy, the supplier states that it “values” feedback, but at the same time structures a regime of appropriation, reserving for itself the free use of ideas, suggestions and creative materials submitted by the user, without any economic consideration, compensation or duty of acknowledgment. The effect, on the Marxian plane of the incorporated exterior, is plain. The user's contribution is useful, is absorbed and becomes part of the ecosystem's metabolism, but it is legally “dehydrated” so that it never becomes a credit, remuneration or bond. The PBE laboratory, then, is not merely a testing space, since it serves as an institutional symbol of what the contract does in silence. The platform acknowledges and manages diffuse productivity, but preserves the asymmetry of command, turning collaboration into an appropriable input Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e)231.

To keep this description from becoming an innocuous metaphor, it should be noted that the PBE is not described as a “parallel” server for ordinary enjoyment. It is presented as a testing environment geared to future features and, therefore, as a quality-adjustment stage prior to going “live”. The very existence of a stable, continuous public testing infrastructure indicates that the ecosystem needs this incorporated exterior to sustain the update cycle without paying the corresponding cost in internal labor. What appears to the consumer as the privilege of early access appears, in the social form of the arrangement, as a machine for extracting time, attention and technical reporting in exchange for a promise of belonging League of Legends Wiki(League of Legends Wiki, [202-?]g)

This productivity is diffuse but organizable, because the platform captures data on use and attention and also captures social proof, diffusion and symbolic validation. This includes p. 219 user-generated content, such as posts, comments, streams, memes, guides and reviews, which becomes an engine of visibility and recommendation and functions, in practice, as invisible labor in the production and marketing of the IOC. What circulates in this environment is not only money but also status, belonging, visibility, comparison and distinction, so that the value of the IOC lies not only in the object but in the social and semiotic environment that surrounds it.

Among the most conspicuous forms of this invisible labor is the work of content creators, streamers and influencers who orbit the ecosystem as if they were independent “microentrepreneurs”, when in practice they function as an intensely public face of the incorporated exterior. The platform benefits from a mass of communicative labor that not only talks about the game but translates it, explains it, makes the catalog intelligible and sustains the collective desire that keeps symbolic rarefaction working. This is the production of meaning as infrastructure. In the platform rent economy, producing meaning is producing value, because the consumer pays for distinction only when distinction is recognized and when that recognition is socially produced.

This ambiguity is reinforced by the contractual design itself. On the one hand, the contract insists that use is “personal” and “non-commercial”, pushing creative work into a zone of conditioned tolerance, dependent on authorization, exceptions or a specific program. On the other hand, the company maintains institutional support channels for creators, with partnership programs and benefits, formalizing a functional integration without converting this integration into an employment bond nor into duties equivalent to those of an employer Riot Games(Riot Games, 2025m)232. In this combination, the platform captures the creator's diffuse productivity as advertising and as a living informational manual of the ecosystem, but maintains the asymmetry of command, because the creator's permanence, access to benefits and the very possibility of monetizing content depend on private, revocable and selective rules.

The capture becomes even clearer when one observes the problem of intelligibility of the cosmetic “product”. As of the date of conclusion of this research, the client233 does not offer, in the purchase flow itself, a robust and generalized preview mechanism that p. 220 allows the user to audit the experience of a skin before acquiring it, so that the decision depends, to a relevant extent, on external mediations that display, compare and describe the content. Riot's own representatives publicly acknowledge this discomfort when announcing experiments with mechanisms for offering previews of skins, making explicit that the user often does not know precisely what they are acquiring at the time of purchase RIOTMIKOUZ(RiotMikouz, 2025)234. In this void, channels such as SkinSpotlights come to function, in practice, as prostheses of the ecosystem's duty to inform, because they display what the consumer cannot audit within the normal purchase circuit SKINSPOTLIGHTS(SkinSpotlights, 2026)235. The result is that a formally external agent is incorporated as a necessary piece of the circulation apparatus, delivering intelligibility, reducing the risk of regret and sustaining the consumer's practical trust in the purchase, without receiving any legal status corresponding to the economic function it performs within the regime236.

In this picture, the user contributes to manufacturing the very rarity that governs them, since, in the immaterial, rarity is not substance, but grammar. Whoever defines this grammar, that is, what counts as legendary, exclusive, meta or essential, controls the circuit of value. Artificial scarcity produces urgency of purchase and also urgency of presence, because, for the narrative to be sustained, the crowd needs to see, dispute, comment and desire. Without an active community, rarity loses effectiveness. Thus, the consumer of the IOC is not only a consumer, since they are, structurally, also an input.

With this, the notion of the incorporated exterior237 gains a decisive layer of intelligibility. The user not only pays and produces, but also legitimizes and polices, p. 221 sustaining the value of the environment through presence and its governability through collective performance and defense of the fief. Recurring extraction therefore depends on a design that simultaneously organizes cheap symbolic rewards, material costs borne by the consumer and visibility mechanisms capable of improvising an audience and suppressing friction. Artificial scarcity, in this picture, is not only a rarefaction of supply. It is a rarefaction of recognition, opportunity and belonging, managed so as to turn community into a cog, engagement into value and loyalty into a barrier to exit. This architecture confirms the thesis that capture is not a marketing accident but architectural teleology, because the mechanism reproduces itself precisely by turning mediated life into a requirement for the rent regime to function.

At this point, with a strictly functional scope and with no claim to exegetical reconstruction, it is justified to draw, by strict structural homology, on the figure of the lumpenproletariat as presented by Karl Marx238. The aim is not to transpose categories of the employment relationship to digital consumption, nor to shift the framing of the consumer microsystem to a general theory of class struggle. The term is used as a key to understanding an analogous power structure, in which subjects located outside regular formal production are functionally incorporated into a regime of valorization and can be mobilized as a mass that sustains, legitimizes and stabilizes a command, including under the guise of spontaneity, leisure and “community participation”.

In the platform environment, this homology describes a specific mechanism of functional incorporation of the consumer. The user's presence, permanence, performance, sociability and collective recognition operate as infrastructure of network value and symbolic value. Positional goods and regimes of administered scarcity depend on this social base to convert rarity into prestige and prestige into price. It is not a matter of asserting an employment bond, but of identifying an unpaid social co-production of value, captured and converted into revenue through unilateral governance of the rules, of visibility, of the catalog and of the conditions of enjoyment. In dogmatic terms, this datum does not change the nature p. 222 of the relationship as consumption. It qualifies the governance asymmetry and reinforces the centrality of duties of material transparency, loyalty and protection of trust in continuous adhesion contracts, precisely because the supplier administers, without real auditability by the consumer, the parameters that define what is worth, what appears and what is considered “rare”.

It is also in this light that one can understand a decisive and recurring practical element of digital consumption, which the language of the lumpenproletariat helps to illuminate without reducing the phenomenon to a sociological identity. The platform does not merely capture the user's participation as an economic condition of the ecosystem. It can mobilize it as a technology for stabilizing command. Consumers become activist defenders of the very regime that captures them, policing discourse, enforcing community norms, harassing critics and naturalizing tolls and reconfigurations of equivalence as if they were merit, “deservingness” or simple individual choice. This mobilization effect is not incidental, because it functions as a layer of legitimation and social enforcement of the design, increasing the system's practical opacity and hindering collective reaction, which deepens and exacerbates the position of vulnerability and makes it all the more important to delimit dogmatically when the architecture frustrates adequacy, transparency and trust, turning participation into an instrument of extraction.

Historically, this is a layer situated at the margins of regular productive labor, unstable and heterogeneous239, whose relevance, in the Marxian analysis, does not lie in being a “producing class” in the industrial sense, but in occupying a social position of exteriority to the classical circuit of labor that, even so, can be functional to certain arrangements of power. The parallel that matters here is neither identitarian nor historical, but structural.

p. 223 The consumer-user of platforms stands formally outside the wage relationship and outside the classical categories of labor that underpinned industrial legal dogmatics, yet is materially incorporated into the regime of valorization as a constant source of diffuse productivity and as the base that sustains the toll.

From this point of view, the consumer approaches a digital lumpenproletariat: they participate intensely in the production of value, but do so on an opaque plane, outside the traditional categories of labor, employment contract or provision of services. Their exploitation occurs not only through the extraction of direct income, but through the structural capture of their subjectivity (of their symbolic identity and of their personal integrity) as raw material for the mode of production. When progress, status, reputation and the very continuity of access to the Immaterial Object of Consumption become dependent on unilateral decisions of the platform, the consumer finds themselves in a position analogous to that of the worker subjected to living conditions defined by others, but without the law recognizing them as such.

This is an external agent who, far from being irrelevant, is turned into an economic cog precisely because what it provides presents itself not as labor but as mediated life, attention, circulation and conduct. This homology helps clarify a decisive feature of digital rentierism. Platform extraction does not depend only on selling goods or services. It depends on governing a set of social behaviors turned into economic material. The platform captures the user's time, but it also captures their place in the collective, with visibility, comparison, distinction, belonging and reputation. This capture is at once economic and semiotic.

The platform needs the user to be there, returning, circulating and feeding the ecosystem because it is from this return that the data, the network effects and the very valuation of the environment are manufactured. The “cloud serf” produces without a contract, without a wage and without recognition as labor. And it is precisely there that the effectiveness of the regime lies. Extraction is naturalized as entertainment, as habit, as sociability and as a consumer micro-decision.

The technofeudal specificity of the arrangement appears even more forcefully when one observes the inversion of infrastructure costs. In industrial capitalism, the holder of capital provides machines, facilities and means of production, subordinating labor through material control of the productive apparatus. In the regime of IOCs, the consumer comes to finance, with their own resources, the minimum infrastructure of their own economic and semiotic incorporation, paying for hardware, electricity and connectivity in order to remain in an environment that converts their presence into someone else's asset. This is a functional mutation of p. 224 command, since the formally external subject not only feeds the system with data and attention, but also internalizes the material cost of maintaining the access that enables the capture. The result is a model in which extraction rests on a prior investment by the exploited themselves, naturalized as a technical condition of participation and masked as a consumer choice.

This financing of the infrastructure combines with a logic of reward that, by contrast, has a marginal cost tending toward zero. If, in classical arrangements of political and social mobilization, loyalty can be bought with biological sustenance, here loyalty is captured through symbolic sustenance. Insignia, skins, emblems, passes, prestige levels and signs of distinction function as payment, but a payment that does not remunerate, it only regulates. The supplier exchanges lines of code and calibrated scarcity for hours of life, attention and behavioral predictability of the consumer, producing the cheapest form of bribery240 yet observable in markets, since what is granted to the user has no intrinsic value independent of the ecosystem. On the contrary, its value depends on the very informational fief that issues it, which reinforces the circuit of permanence, since the reward received only “exists” as a recognizable difference within the controller's infrastructure.

Here, the Marxian rereading engages directly with the notion of semiotic vulnerability developed earlier. Exploitation operates not only at the economic level, but at the semiotic level. The platform controls the meanings attached to certain goods (a “legendary” skin, an “exclusive” title), defines which symbols confer status and belonging and determines what counts as legitimate progress within its ecosystem, which in turn encourages the user to invest so much lived experience in it that it can influence even how those meanings are understood outside its sphere of action and control. At the same time, it controls the mechanisms by which the consumer can lose these symbols, including resets, meta shifts, discontinued items and changes in the relative rarity of rewards. The fear of losing what has been accumulated, of seeing one's digital identity devalued or erased, becomes an instrument of discipline, consolidating a form of existential captivity that adds to the economic captivity already described.

p. 225 This effect can be named, with operational precision, as identity captivity, understood as a modality of dependence in which the exit cost is not reduced to the patrimonial plane, but involves loss of recognition, of reputation and of narrative continuity objectified in public performance records, collections, histories and signs of distinction produced within the ecosystem. Dependence, in this register, is not psychological in the individualizing sense, but institutional and semiotic, because it stems from a regime of visibility and sanction that defines what counts as value, belonging and legitimate progress and, correlatively, defines how this can be downgraded, erased or disauthorized. In consumer-law terms, the relevance of the concept is not to create a new good, but to make controllable a specific form of loss in the fact of consumption, in which integrity of agency and symbolic identity are corroded by instability, opacity and manufactured risk, affecting the adequacy of the enjoyment regime and the legitimate trust that sustains continuous bonds governed by a platform.

In this key, the categories of digitally based businesses need to be treated as arrangements that organize the exploitation of this consumption “lumpenproletariat” and, at the same time, as structures that capture social coproduction and convert it into recurrent extraction through unilateral governance. A monetization model based on microtransactions for obtaining rare cosmetic items, for example, is not merely a commercial choice, but a device of value extraction that relies on the user's internalization of the logic of symbolic distinction produced by the platform and on the administration of the conditions of visibility and recognition that give economic meaning to rarity. The consumer pays to preserve or raise a position that exists only within the fief, in an economy whose currency is, at the same time, money and recognition, and in which the functional value equivalence of the Immaterial Object of Consumption is continually reconfigured to keep extraction ongoing.

Moreover, the incorporation of what is “outside” operates not only as economic fuel for the ecosystem, but also as an instrument for imposing order in practice. In regimes of platform governance, everyday discipline is not exercised only through written rules and formal sanctions, since it is sustained by the functional mobilization of collectives that simulate consensus, amplify official narratives, isolate dissent and, in certain circumstances, engage in reputational hostility and symbolic expulsion as a social extension of moderation decisions. The circuit closes when the infrastructure itself encourages and directs this mobilization (through visibility, ranking, recommendation, reporting and engagement mechanisms), turning the mass of users into an operational force capable of containing resistance, imposing exit costs and consolidating the regime of p. 226 permanence241. It is in this sense that the “incorporated exterior” can operate as a political and policing tool in a structural sense, even without police, since coercion appears mediated by collective performance and the asymmetric protection of the apparatus242.

Besides capturing time and conduct as economic input, the platform mobilizes the incorporated exterior to perform a specific function in the reproduction of command, the capacity to improvise an audience. In structural terms, this is not merely spontaneous participation around a product, but a diffuse labor of staging adherence that manufactures visibility, normality and collective desire, turning a launch into an event and an event into social proof. Hashtags, engagement drives, event attendance, clips, reactions and micro-performances of enthusiasm operate as a symbolic infrastructure of valorization, since they turn an act of consumption into a public display of belonging and draw new users into the circuit of dependence. Productivity here is not only informational. It is scenographic, because it produces an artificial atmosphere of celebration that raises the perceived value of the offer and reduces reputational friction against the very architecture of extraction.

The political function of the incorporated exterior is not limited to the manufacture of a public and the production of social proof. It extends to community policing as an informal extension of governance. When criticisms of the model, of moderation decisions or of p. 227 monetization practices emerge, the consumer themselves, whose identity is in captivity243 in the ecosystem, may act as a force of reputational intimidation and containment of dissent, not by explicit order, but by functional alignment between belonging and defense of the environment. Thus a device of diffuse private security is formed, in which fan “armies” operate to delegitimize critics, impose costs on speech, produce coordinated harassment and reinforce the official narrative, preserving the regime that exploits them in order to protect the symbolic coherence of their own position. Coercion, once again, appears without police, but with asymmetric protection of the apparatus, since the infrastructure of visibility, ranking and engagement amplifies informal policing and converts conflict into a mechanism of discipline.

The same logic deepens when one observes that the platform not only captures the user's behavior, but reabsorbs it as an internal function of the governance apparatus itself, that is, as a part of an organizational machinery that becomes autonomous and starts to operate as a practical condition of command244. The user comes to make up the machinery of valorization by producing social proof, by functioning as diffuse advertising, by maintaining routines of presence that stabilize metrics, by performing microtasks of curation and recommendation, by p. 228 mass-testing changes of architecture (through behavioral “telemetry”) and, in certain segments, by supplying creative and communicational labor that is converted into an asset of the ecosystem. Thus, the boundary between public and operator becomes blurred, since the collective that should remain outside the center of command is, in practice, installed as a part of the governance circuit, and its participation comes to be exploited as a requirement for the functioning of the extraction regime. The cloud serf is not merely a captive consumer, because they are also an integrated component of the symbolic and economic administration of the fief, insofar as the apparatus feeds on their presence to regulate itself and to expand its own capacity for command.

The Marxian reading of the lumpenproletariat, in this framing, helps to set aside two inadequate simplifications. The first is to confuse exteriority with insignificance. The second is to treat the political usefulness of this layer as a merely episodic detail. Marx (2011) describes an unstable social mass that can be organized and activated as a practical device of power, operating in functions that cannot be reduced to regular productive labor, but that are decisive for the stabilization of a command and for the manufacture of public effects of legitimacy and order. It is this element (an instrumental functionalization of the “outside”) that directly interests the argument of the “incorporated exterior”245, because it allows exteriority to be treated not as an absence of link with valorization, but as a p. 229 reconfigured link. The framing remains one of consumer law, since the homology operates as a tool for describing governance and mobilization in consumption, and not as a displacement of the object toward categories of labor.

It is precisely at this point that the structural parallelism with the platform economy gains density. The platform not only administers prices and catalog but also administers conduct, rhythms and presence. To do so, it needs to produce, continuously, public signs of adherence, normality and enthusiasm246, while neutralizing frictions and dissent that threaten retention and monetization. In functional terms, the user is integrated as part of this device, since, by engaging, reacting, ranking, recommending, reporting, moderating informally and reproducing patterns, they participate in the maintenance of the environment and its governability. The material incorporation of everyday life as input is not limited to data as information, since it includes behavior as an organizing force of the platform's social space, that is, as an operative component of government.

In this register, the analogy operates as a key to intelligibility. Marx (2011) describes the organization of the lumpenproletariat as a force capable of improvising a public, simulating enthusiasm, intimidating opponents and acting with institutional tolerance/protection, functioning as a gear for the performance of power and the manufacture of consensus in act247. The p. 230 usefulness of the passage lies not in importing nineteenth-century categories as if they were the sociological identity of the contemporary user, but in recognizing the recurrent structure: layers socially “external” to regular productive labor can be mobilized as instruments of government and as a technology for stabilizing command, including when their activity appears under the guise of spontaneity, leisure, sociability or “community participation”.

Transposed to the environment of IOCs, this structure helps to explain why rentier extraction requires more than charging: it requires an architecture of permanence. The platform calibrates what counts as visibility, what counts as reputation, what belonging is and what represents normality in the ecosystem, and it does so by converting the very circulation of users into a mechanism of ordering. Thus, the cloud serf is kept simultaneously as a recurrent payer and as a diffuse producer of stability for the fief, since they sustain the valuation of the environment through engagement and sustains the governance of the environment through the everyday production of signs, rankings and informal discipline. The legal-structural consequence is direct, because, when the platform comes to depend on the continuous mobilization of the user to govern and to valorize the ecosystem, it gains systemic incentives to design scarcity, risk and sanction so as to produce presence, and not merely purchase.

The Marxian tradition describes capitalism as a social form capable of incorporating collective and organizational conditions into the logic of valorization248, converting direction, p. 231 coordination and reproduction into internal functions of the process249. Applied to the environment of IOCs, this means that extraction is not limited to price. It depends on keeping enjoyment under governance and on transforming the presence of users into a condition of stability of the ecosystem250. The consequence is that the economic purpose of the model materializes as p. 232 institutional design, that is, as architectural teleology oriented toward retention and continuous capture.

It is in this sense that “direction” and “command” do not appear as an element external to the process, but as a constitutive dimension of the capitalist mode of production. “All immediately social or collective labor on a large scale requires, to a greater or lesser extent, a direction that establishes harmony among individual activities and performs the general functions that result from the movement of the total productive body as distinct from the movement of its autonomous organs. A single violinist directs himself, but an orchestra requires a conductor. This function of direction, supervision and mediation becomes a function of capital as soon as the labor subordinated to it becomes cooperative. As a specific function of capital, direction assumes specific characteristics. First, the motive that drives and the purpose that determines the capitalist production process is the greatest possible self-valorization of capital, that is, the greatest possible production of surplus value and, therefore, the maximum possible exploitation of labor power by the capitalist. As the mass of simultaneously employed workers increases, so does their resistance and, with it, the pressure of capital to overcome it. The command of the capitalist is not only a specific function, arising from the nature of the social labor process and, therefore, peculiar to that process, but, at the same time, a function of exploitation of a social labor process, and is, for that reason, determined by the inevitable antagonism between the exploiter and the raw material of his exploitation.” Marx(Marx, 2013, p. 504). ↩
  24. The idea of “incorporated exterior” gains precision when one considers that, in the Marxian tradition, activities that seem situated outside the immediate core of production, such as individual consumption and the very everyday reproduction of labor power, can be treated as internal moments of the reproduction of capital. This is not a metaphor, since the consumption that keeps the worker fit to work is described as a functional element of the process, even though it takes place outside the factory. With this, Marx provides a formal scheme especially useful for this research, since capitalism does not depend only on what happens “within” the productive act, but on capturing and organizing external social conditions as requirements for the continuity of valorization. On the plane of IOCs, the structural analogy is not to assert that the user “is an industrial worker”, but rather to recognize that the platform converts presence, routine and consumption into conditions for the reproduction of its own economic circuit. “The worker's consumption has a double nature. In production itself, he consumes, by means of his labor, means of production, transforming them into products of greater value than that of the capital advanced. This is his productive consumption. At the same time, it is consumption of his labor power by the capitalist who bought it. On the other hand, the worker spends on means of subsistence the money paid for the purchase of labor power: this is his individual consumption. […] The worker is often forced to convert his individual consumption into a mere incident of the production process. In that case, he supplies himself with means of subsistence to keep his labor power running, in the same way as the steam engine is supplied with coal and water and the wheel with oil. […] When the capitalist converts part of his capital into labor power, he thereby valorizes his total capital and kills two birds with one stone. He profits not only from what he receives from the worker, but also from what he gives him. The capital alienated in exchange for labor power is converted into means of subsistence, whose consumption serves to reproduce the muscles, nerves, bones and brain of existing workers and to produce new workers. […] Such consumption is production and reproduction of the means of production most indispensable to the capitalist: the worker himself. The individual consumption of the worker thus remains a moment of the production and reproduction of capital, whether it is carried out inside or outside the workshop, the factory etc.” Marx(Marx, 2013, p. 787-788). ↩
