6.4.1 On the unlawful act of abusive teleology: design defect and abusive practice

See this section in the PDF

Concepts in this section

  • Unlawful act of architecture
  • Abusive teleology

p. 665

Abusive teleology designates, under the CDC regime, a qualified form of unlawfulness in which the exploitation of vulnerability appears not as an episodic deviation in the functioning of the service, but as a purpose built into its very program of operation. In continuous digital services, the legal problem is no longer limited to identifying a one-off error, an isolated communication failure or a singularly excessive clause. What must be controlled is the functional design of supply when it comes to organize, in a stable way, defective understanding, hindered reversibility, degradable enjoyment and structurally weakened challenge. Architecture, in this context, does not remain outside the performance. It is part of the very way in which utility is produced, maintained, restricted and made intelligible to the consumer.

This is precisely why the false dilemma between a quality defect in the product and a governance failure must be rejected. In the IOC regime, immaterial product and governed environment are not opposed as independent layers. Enjoyment becomes legally consumable only through the governance that organizes it. When this governance is designed to stabilize asymmetries p. 666 of understanding, of reversal, of proof and of tolerance to loss, the problem may be described, at the same time, as a defect in the supply regime and as a relevant degradation of the enjoyment of the object consumed. There is no competition between the two readings. There is complementarity. The same dysfunction appears, on one side, as a design defect in the mode of performance and, on the other, as a functional or symbolic downgrading of the utility promised to the consumer.

Seen this way, abusive teleology is not to be confused with a merely harsh business choice, with debatable design or with architecture that is simply efficient for the supplier. The category is justified only when the exploitative effect derives from the very design of the flow and of governance, when the mechanism presents itself as a stable and replicable pattern of functioning and when this pattern relevantly affects the consumer's practical autonomy and the integrity of enjoyment. The unlawful act therefore does not arise from a singular episode that can be treated as an accident. It arises when defaults, frictions, manufactured urgencies, hostile cancellation routes, elastic equivalences, sanction regimes or evidentiary arrangements function together as a method of exploitation. Abusiveness ceases to be a mere accident of execution and comes to reside in the program that organizes execution.

This formulation is not a license for judicial impressionism or abstract moralization of the digital environment. On the contrary, it functions as a technique of dogmatic containment. The category only makes sense because it allows repeated functional patterns to be treated as legally attributable units, instead of dissolving them into a collection of disconnected micro-events. Dogmatics does not operate by infinite reproduction of the empirical, but by bringing functionally similar occurrences back to a unitary normative frame, capable of guiding decision and legal consequence Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 99; p. 312-313])756. When the service repeats, with functional coherence, mechanisms for capturing choice, for making loss opaque and for p. 667 demobilizing challenge, attributing the program is no longer rhetorical exaggeration. It is the technically adequate way of apprehending the unlawful act.

The same logic applies to the stability of the standard. Abusive teleology does not derive from an artificial aggregation of heterogeneous facts, but from the functional similarity between occurrences that reveal a common rationale of exploitation. Legal reasoning does not need to reproduce the case in its full empirical extent to treat it adequately; it abstracts, synthesizes and formulates a decision rule from a recognizable pattern Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 52; p. 337])757. That is exactly what the category does here. It prevents the system from treating stable architectural exploitation as a mere sum of small isolated annoyances, each apparently trivial, but all functionally articulated for the benefit of the same program.

This framing also requires recognizing that abusiveness cannot remain confined to the atomized reading of isolated clauses. In platform-governed consumer relationships, legal control needs to reach the global logic of organization of the relationship, the distribution of frictions, facilities, barriers, opacities and asymmetries within supply. Abusiveness, therefore, is not measured only by the contractual sentence taken alone, but by the design of the contract and of the performance seen as a dynamic totality. Good faith and balance operate precisely on this plane, making it possible to identify abuse when contractual or procedural engineering concentrates excessive unilaterality and significant imbalance to the detriment of the consumer Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 163; p. 291)758. In continuous p. 668 digital services, this means that decision flow, reversal architecture, distribution of noise, zones of opacity and the design of challenge already belong to the field of the consumer-law unlawful act.

For this reason, abusive teleology may operate, depending on the structure of the case, as a design defect in supply and as an abusive practice. As a design defect, it appears when the service is born already architected to produce asymmetry as a method, downgrading in advance intelligibility, reversibility and the minimum stability of enjoyment. As an abusive practice, it manifests itself when the everyday governance of the ecosystem repeatedly and predictably exploits the consumer's factual and semiotic vulnerability, administering urgency, scarcity, exit cost, informational noise or difficulty of proof for its own benefit. In borderline cases, the same rationale may further approach the figure of the safety defect of the service, when the program of functioning produces relevant risk or actual harm to reference legal interests, including those linked to personal integrity, biographical continuity and symbolic identity.

This qualification does not depend on proving the supplier's intimate psychological intention, as if the unlawful act required a subjective confession of malice. The focus falls on the functional purpose of the arrangement. What matters is how the program operates, what asymmetries it stabilizes and in what way it organizes the expected behavior of the consumer. Exploitation may be incorporated into the design even when business discourse presents it as simple technical neutrality, operational inevitability or a natural consequence of the service. In dogmatic terms, this suffices. The rationale of the program is legally more important than the benevolent self-description the platform gives of itself.

This is where the notion of the barrier clause gains special value. Abusiveness manifests itself not only when the supplier frontally restricts a right, but also when it organizes the relationship to hinder, delay or discourage the exercise of that right through multiple steps, unnecessary requirements, artificial frictions and procedural increases in the cost of the consumer's reaction. When performance or reversal becomes possible only after excessively costly paths, without plausible justification, the very way the relationship is organized already functions as a device for containing the consumer's claim. The CDC does not need to wait for a scandalous text to react. It is enough that the contractual or procedural structure operates as a mechanism of substantial imbalance, p. 669 unjustifiably hindering the consumer's access to what would ordinarily be owed to them Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 1236)759.

With this, the unlawful act of abusive teleology gains more precise contours. It does not name every bad experience, every consumer frustration or every unfavorable alteration made on a platform. It names cases in which the architecture of the service was conceived or came to be administered so as to structurally exploit the consumer's vulnerability, turning opacity, friction, difficulty of reversal, capture of expectation and concentration of proof into ordinary elements of the performance. Its object is not the interface taken as aesthetics, but the functional organization of enjoyment. Its point of application is not the isolated anecdote, but the pattern. Its criterion is not cultural indignation at design, but the verifiability that the program operates against real understanding of the essential, against practicable reversibility, against intelligible challenge and against minimally loyal stability of the acquired enjoyment.

When the program of the service comes to operate in these terms, the CDC is not facing disconnected micro-incidents, but a mode of supply that is legally controllable in itself. The qualification of the unlawful act may then open, depending on the case, the door of the design defect, of the abusive practice, of the abusive clause that shields this program or, in aggravated cases, of the safety defect of the service. From there, the discussion ceases to revolve around the impoverished question of whether the platform “can or cannot change” and comes to bear on what really matters: whether the design of supply was organized in a way compatible with good faith, balance, auditability, functional equivalence and a real possibility of reaction by the consumer. When the answer is negative, abusive teleology ceases to be a theoretical hypothesis and becomes a technical category for attributing the exploitative program itself.

Notes

  1. The formulation of the unlawful act of abusive teleology depends on recognizing that dogmatics can treat repeated functional patterns as legally attributable units, instead of dissolving them into disconnected micro-events. It is not a matter of moralizing the design of the service or of producing a sociological denunciation of the environment, but of verifying whether there is a program of functioning stable enough to receive unitary qualification. Ferraz Jr. offers a basis for this operation by showing that dogmatics apprehends social phenomena as "normed sets", that is, as units endowed with organization and structure, and not as dispersed empirical facts Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 99]). It is in this key that abusive teleology can be treated as a technical category of attribution: legal relevance does not derive from the simple sum of isolated episodes, but from the possibility of bringing them back to a coherent functional pattern of exploitation. In addition, the author observes that, from the legal angle, the decisive question is not the brute causality of the event, but the normative attribution of consequences to legally qualified conduct Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 312-313]). The problem, here, is not only to demonstrate the fragmentary existence of acts of inducement, capture or opacity, but to know whether, legally, they compose a unitary program of abusive supply. ↩
  2. The stabilization of abusive teleology as a dogmatic category also rests on the distinctive form of legal decision-making, which does not merely collect empirical occurrences, but reconstructs the case by synthesis, abstraction and controlled generalization. Ferraz Jr. observes that the jurist's work does not consist in reproducing cases "in their full extent", but in abstracting them, contrasting reasons and broadening the problem "in such a way that we can obtain from it a general rule that decides the case and serves for others" Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 52]). This dispels the objection that abusive teleology would depend on an artificial aggregation of heterogeneous facts. On the contrary, when multiple occurrences reveal sufficient functional similarity, bringing them back to a unitary frame is simply the normal way dogmatics operates. The same reasoning sustains the formation of legal standards by similarity, including with support in decisions and examples, as the author records when he mentions the constitution of "legal standards" by the "principle of similarity" Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 337]). Abusive teleology thus appears not as a loose metaphor, but as a technical framing aimed at giving unitary treatment to a repeated pattern of architectural exploitation. ↩
  3. Within the CDC, the assessment of abusiveness cannot remain confined to the atomized reading of isolated clauses, as if the contract were merely a mosaic of legally autonomous sentences. The mode of organization of the contractual relationship — its global design, its internal logic of functioning, the distribution of frictions, facilities, reversals, informational burdens and points of opacity — is part of the very object of legal control. Marques offers a decisive basis for this reading by asserting that the interpreter's activity in identifying abusiveness must concentrate on a “dynamic and total view of contracts”, so that abusiveness is not observed in the isolated reading of the clause, but “in the reading of the whole of the contract, in the function of the clause in the contract as drafted, in the repercussion of the clause in that kind of contract” Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 163). The author reinforces that, under the influence of good faith, abuse can also be identified in the “excessive unilaterality or unreasonable imbalance of the contractual engineering”, the “significant imbalance of rights and duties, to the detriment of the consumer, in the contractual relationship seen as a whole” being an indication of abuse Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 291). This authorizes treating architecture, decision flow and opacity as legally relevant elements of the performance itself. ↩
  4. Objective good faith and contractual balance also operate as direct limits on the creation of contractual or procedural structures designed to unilaterally favor the supplier. In consumer matters, it is not only the declaratory content of the clause that may be abusive, but also the way the relationship is organized to hinder, delay or discourage the exercise of the consumer's rights. Marques is precise in stating that the attempt to prevent performance may be directed at the supplier's own performance, when the contract makes it enforceable only after “certain and multiple authorizations, papers, proofs, without plausible justification, merely to hinder and discourage the consumer from asserting their own (and main) claim.” Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 1236). Claudia Lima Marques shows that the “barrier clause” is contrary to Article 51, item IV, of the CDC, precisely because it substantially unbalances the contractual relationship Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 1236). Applied to the unlawful act of abusive teleology, this reasoning makes it possible to control entire procedural arrangements conceived to produce asymmetric friction, increase the cost of reversal and demobilize challenge. ↩
