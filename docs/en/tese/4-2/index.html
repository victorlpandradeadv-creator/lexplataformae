Section 4.2 · pp. 330 to 334

4.2 Informational domination, private power and subjectivity

View this section in the PDF

This section in the layers of the site

  • Foundations · Sections of chapter 4

p. 330

So far, enjoyment has appeared as a position sustained by signs, visibility and equivalences that only exist while the environment keeps them operative. In this condition, acquisition depends on an ecosystem that provides intelligibility, commensurability and the possibility of sanction, while at the same time defining, in practice, access, permanence and hierarchies. For this reason, the description of the IOC, when it seeks to sustain legal control, cannot remain confined to the vocabulary of price nor to the psychological repertoire of preference. The core is institutional, because the platform administers the conditions of enjoyment and, by this route, stabilizes value as a recognizable position, including when the relevant experience is reconfigured over time.

The aim is not to build a general constitutional theory of platforms nor to automatically transpose categories of public power to private relations, but to delimit a minimal analytical framework in which the platform is read as an instance of practical governance, with sufficient economic and informational power to organize access, permanence and participation at scale.

When the offer materializes as ecosystem-dependent enjoyment and when value is anchored in hierarchies, criteria and sanctions produced by the environment itself, private power ceases to be merely a competitive datum and comes to act as a structural component of vulnerability, so that consumer protection needs to engage with limits and duties p. 331 proportional to the intensity of the control exercised, without dissolving the problem into abstractions and without reducing the platform to the classic figure of the supplier indifferent to the mode of operation of the very market it administers.

The investigation was opened with the hypothesis that, in the digital environment, the platform tends to occupy, by technical and contractual means, functions that were previously distributed socially, replacing public criteria of circulation and recognition with private rules of access, visibility and permanence. The formula according to which the platform “occupies the place of the collectivity without authorization” is treated as a descriptive problem to be stabilized in verifiable institutional terms, lest it remain an intuition without legal consequence.

This hypothesis needs to be stabilized in controllable institutional language. It matters to identify which operations make up this occupation, which effects it produces on the enjoyment position and which asymmetries it installs between consumer and administrator of the ecosystem, so as to allow assessment of conduct, duties and restoration without depending on impressionistic readings of the conflict. To this end, it is necessary to recognize that, in the IOC regime, the platform is not limited to intermediating a bilateral relationship between supplier and acquirer. It organizes, simultaneously, the normative content of the environment and the mechanisms of its enforcement.

In the first place, it defines and updates internal rules that structure access and permanence, establishing permissions, incentives and punishments through moderation, functional restrictions or exclusions. In the second place, it administers visibility and reputation as economically relevant dimensions, through metrics, rankings, signals of prestige and instruments of public comparison that produce hierarchies and modulate the experience. In the third place, it exercises continuous curation of what appears, what disappears and what returns, controlling the horizon of expectation and reorganizing the temporality of consumption. Finally, it applies sanctions and restrictions with effects that go beyond the loss of one-off utility, degrading symbolic position, interrupting continuity of enjoyment and reconfiguring the economic meaning of the IOC, in a key close to functional and identity dispossession.

The description of these powers dispenses with the assumption of subjective intent, because what is at stake is a mode of operation in which control is exercised through architecture and through information. In digital environments, the relevant normativity does not manifest itself only as an explicit command, but as a configuration of choices, rhythms, frictions and incentives that makes certain conduct more likely and other conduct more costly, while it defines the criteria by which the environment itself will recognize, reward or sanction the user. This governance is carried out through design decisions, moderation parameters, recommendation patterns and p. 332 access rules that operate as silent infrastructure, so that coercion need not present itself as frontal prohibition to produce obedience, predictability and conformity.

The result is that the consumer faces not merely a contract, but an integrated set of devices that guide the experience and stabilize hierarchies, while keeping the effective operating criteria opaque, creating a structural asymmetry between those who live the ecosystem and those who control the variables that define its conditions of legibility, permanence and value.

It is in this strict sense that the expression “without authorization” should be understood, not as a generic political judgment, but as the finding that the platform exercises functions of social ordering without holding a public mandate and without submitting, to an equivalent extent, to demands of institutional reciprocity. The relevant absence is not that of formal delegation, but of minimum guarantees that function as counterweights of legitimacy, such as intelligible publicity of criteria, possibility of verification, decisional coherence, accessible reasoning and effective avenues of contestation.

When the environment is governed by mutable rules, by selective curation and by technical enforcement, the user is subjected to decisions that can alter their enjoyment position and degrade the value of the IOC without the grounds of the act being transparent, without proportionality being examinable and without review being practicable in terms compatible with the economic and identity relevance of what is at stake. The consequence is that private power consolidates itself as a power to administer symbolic and economic destinies at scale, while preserving a functional opacity that prevents the consumer from turning the conflict into an object of rational control, shifting the experience of enjoyment into a regime of institutional dependence not recognized as such.

Once this structure is recognized, the consequence for consumer law cannot be formulated as a simple rhetorical expansion of protection, but as an adjustment of lens capable of reaching the way in which the offer materializes in practice. If the IOC exists as a governed enjoyment position and if value depends on internal criteria of recognition, protection cannot be content with reading the contract as an exhaustive declaration of content, because the effective shaping of the object is produced by rules, architecture and continuous operations that exceed the documentary form and that can alter, unilaterally, the continuity of supply.

The question, therefore, is not merely to verify isolated abusive clauses, but to understand that the environment itself functions as a mechanism for producing vulnerability, which makes duties proportional to the power exercised relevant. It is, p. 333 thus, a matter of preparing a conceptual basis that makes it possible to demand transparency, stability and restoration in terms compatible with the regime of permanence and with the institutional density of the platform, without yet anticipating the dogmatic reconstruction that will be developed later, but establishing the need for protection to reach the architectural plane in which enjoyment becomes possible and in which risk is effectively administered.

To fulfill this task without generalizations, the development ahead will mobilize references with delimited functions, as instruments of description and conceptual locking. With Julie E. Cohen (2012; 2019), the specific form of power that stabilizes when information and architecture become means of practical governance is delimited, making it possible to treat choices, patterns and infrastructures as normative mechanisms that organize conduct and distribute possibilities. Warren and Brandeis ([201-?]) are mobilized as a methodological matrix of legal reconstruction in the face of technical changes that reconfigure exposure and vulnerability, indicating that alterations in the material conditions of social life require reinterpretation of categories without resorting to moralization of the phenomenon. Byung-Chul Han (2015; 2017) is employed as a lens to describe adherence and self-adjustment through performance, comparison and expectation, qualifying subjection as a structural effect of incentives and of symbolic risk management, instead of attributing it to individual frailties. Eros Grau (2008) provides the conceptual lock to avoid both the liberal neutralization of private normativity and the hasty transposition into the category of State, making it possible to describe it as presupposed law endowed with collective effects and, therefore, demanding control criteria compatible with its density.

The expected result is to fix a conceptual key according to which the platform, in the IOC regime, operates as private power with practical normativity, because it administers not only conditions of exchange, but conditions of enjoyment and recognition in a governed environment. This key shifts the debate from the simplifying opposition between contractual freedom and state intervention to a plane in which internal rules, design, curation and enforcement constitute the economic object itself and distribute risks of loss, exclusion and degradation of value.

The transition is thus prepared toward operational criteria that will make possible the discussion of proportional duties, transparency standards and restoration mechanisms without depending on merely formal categories, because the relevant materiality of the IOC will be understood as functional and ecosystemic materiality, and not as simple abstract ownership or one-off performance.

Governance by architecture is taken as the starting point to show how the platform constitutes a normative environment in which choices are configured and in which p. 334 informational domination is carried out through the administration of the variables that make enjoyment legible, comparable and sanctionable. This form of power is not exhausted in the economic dimension, since the technical reorganization of the conditions of exposure, participation and permanence calls for a standard of legal reconstruction capable of dealing with new forms of vulnerability at scale, without this implying converting the platform into a State.

In this same key, the consumer's subjection is treated as a structural effect of mechanisms of performance, comparison and self-adjustment, when the ecosystem turns recognition into a condition of value and converts the management of symbolic risk into an instrument for shaping conduct, so that identity captivity emerges as an institutional result and not as an individual pathology.

The analysis then reaches the framing of private rules and enforcement as practical normativity, making explicit how the Lex Plataformae operates as the effective law of the environment and how it administers access, reputation, curation and sanctions with effects of exclusion and functional dispossession. This path closes with the figure of the consumer-citizen370 subjected to this private normativity, making visible that consumer protection, when dealing with IOC, needs to engage with the institutional impact of platform governance and prepare the passage to structuring concepts that turn the description of power into an object controllable by criteria of transparency, stability and restoration.

Notes

  1. The expression “consumer-citizen” is employed in a strictly analytical and functional sense. It designates the subject holding an IOC, an immaterial product whose utility is realized as permanence in an ecosystem, and not as an isolated delivery. The “citizen” component does not refer to the classic political-constitutional status or to nationality, but describes the practical conditions of participation, belonging and continuity within an environment governed by private rules and technical sanctions (Lex Plataformae). It is a matter of recognizing that, in continuous digital consumption, the subject is subjected to a private normative order that administers their visibility, reputation and capacity to act, making the enjoyment of the product inseparable from their position as a governed member of the ecosystem. ↩
