7.1.3 On the guidelines for control of architecture and governance

See this section in the PDF

This section in the layers of the site

  • In practice · The ten control guidelines

p. 748

The legal control of Immaterial Objects of Consumption requires a methodological and dogmatic shift that has not always been carried out with the necessary clarity. In platform-governed ecosystems, the analysis cannot remain centered on isolated clauses, terminal acts detached from context or one-off events artificially separated from the environment that produced them. The center of gravity of the examination must be the regime of p. 749 supply, that is, the operating program of the ecosystem, the mechanisms by which enjoyment is administered and the legitimacy filters that make this program institutionally controllable. The shift is necessary because, in relations of this nature, what the consumer faces is not merely an episodic manifestation of private power, but a continuous arrangement for organizing experience, access, intelligibility, visibility, contestation and evidence.

The first guideline, therefore, consists in treating architecture and governance not as a neutral backdrop of the bond, but as constitutive elements of supply itself. The environment does not merely host the consumer relationship. It materially shapes it. It is through architecture that the platform defines paths, intensifies or attenuates frictions, selects saliences, organizes degrees of reversibility, distributes costs of understanding and establishes the conditions under which loss will be perceived, expectation will be frustrated and reaction will be possible or unfeasible. It is through governance that the supplier administers permanence, sanction, updating, priority, visibility and mechanisms of contestation. When this structure is rendered legally invisible, control is impoverished. One comes to discuss only final acts detached from an ecosystem whose design, in practice, has already predetermined much of the disputed experience.

From this follows a second guideline. Relevant changes to the service, rule changes, ordinary reconfigurations of the environment and modulations with material impact on enjoyment should not be judged on the basis of a generic discourse of updating, innovation or technical convenience. They must be examined in light of open auditability and functional value equivalence. The relevant question is not only whether the platform could make the change. The question is whether the change was made institutionally intelligible and whether it preserved, in substantial terms, the function that justified the acquisition and structured the consumer's legitimate expectation. The mere appeal to the dynamism of the digital cannot work as an abstract authorization to degrade the experience, obscure the modulation of the environment or turn mutability into an ordinary technology of loss.

The third guideline bears on private sanctions with material impact. In platform ecosystems, sanctions do not operate merely as a one-off disciplinary response to conduct attributed to the user. They may affect access, permanence, visibility, reputation, relational insertion and continuity of enjoyment. For this reason, they must be controlled by denser criteria than the mere formal existence of sanctioning power. They require verifiable reasoning, decisional coherence and intelligible avenues of contestation. Where the platform exercises power of inclusion, exclusion, suspension, visibility downgrading or functional restriction, it is not enough p. 750 that the final act be formally communicable. The procedure must be institutionally reconstructible and contestation must not be a mere empty ritual. The examination, in such cases, must reach not only the challenged act, but the procedural conditions designed by the supplier itself for adhesion, enjoyment, evidence and resistance.

This requirement leads to the fourth guideline. The dispute should not be read only from the terminal moment at which it becomes outwardly manifest. It must be reconstructed from the environment in which it arose, was perceived and can be reconstituted. This means that judges and protection bodies need to consider the way in which the platform organized, from the outset, the conditions of adhesion, understanding, circulation of information, access to records and possibility of contestation. The litigious experience, in continuous digital services, already reaches the proceedings traversed by private filters of perception and evidence. Ignoring this fact means accepting that the supplier controls not only enjoyment, but also the way in which the conflict will be narrated institutionally. The case, therefore, needs to be seen in its trajectory and not only in its outcome.

The fifth guideline concerns structural opacity. It should not be treated as an inevitable and legally neutral characteristic of digital complexity. It must be recognized as a factor that intensifies vulnerability and evidentiary asymmetry. Where the platform concentrates logs, histories, change records, interface trails, curation criteria and decision data, there is a correlated concentration of power over the institutional conditions of evidence. This fact justifies orders for preservation and disclosure of minimum records, delimitation of expert examinations and dynamic redistribution of the burden of proof whenever the relevant evidence arises and remains under the supplier's technical control. Opacity, in other words, cannot operate as a procedural privilege self-generated by the governance model itself.

A sixth guideline consists in avoiding both the fragmentation of the problem into individual lawsuits incapable of reaching the source of the injury and the opposite temptation of turning each case into a manifesto against technology or against the economic model as such. Control must be specific enough to respond to the concrete conflict and attentive enough to structure to identify when this conflict is a localized manifestation of a repeated pattern. Neither atomization nor grandiloquence. The institutional challenge lies in recognizing when the case reveals an individual inadequacy correctable without further expansion and when, on the contrary, it exposes a regime defect, repeatable and incorporated into the ordinary mode of operation of the ecosystem. This distinction is essential to calibrate the legal response and to prevent programmatic harms from being treated as a mere sum of private annoyances disconnected from one another.

p. 751 Hence the seventh guideline, concerning remedies. In cases of architectural injury, the legal response must favor functional restoration and procedural correction, instead of automatically resorting to monetization. When what has been affected is the continuity of enjoyment, the integrity of the experience, the permanence of access, the intelligibility of the bond or the consumer's relational position in the ecosystem, a mere monetary award tends to operate as a poor and structurally ineffective remedy. What is required, whenever possible, is restoration of access, reversal or review of sanctions, restoration of functionality, preservation of history, equivalent substitution and adjustment of governance procedures. This priority is even clearer when the injury stems not from an isolated episode, but from the very design of the supply regime and its ordinary mode of operation.

The eighth guideline emerges precisely from this point. When the pattern of injury proves to be repeated, replicable and incorporated into the structure of the environment, protection should not be limited to the single case. Room opens for injunctive relief and for regime correction measures. This includes procedural duties, architecture adjustments, reinforced auditability requirements, review of sanction flows, imposition of intelligible contestation channels and other measures capable of reaching the continuous source of the problem. The system cannot treat programmatic harms as a casual accumulation of autonomous conflicts. When the injury is programmatic, the response must also be structurally sensitive to the program that produces it.

A ninth guideline consists in preserving institutional sobriety. Control of architecture and governance does not require the judge to become the system's engineer nor the protection body to hold full technical mastery of the ecosystem. What is required is something at once more modest and more serious: the capacity to bring the environment back to verifiable filters of legitimacy. Open auditability, functional equivalence, verifiable reasoning, procedural coherence, minimum intelligibility of contestation, preservation of essential records and functional reconciliation between exclusivity and consumer-law protection are sufficient criteria to make the conflict decidable without any pretension to technical omniscience. The complexity of the digital does not eliminate Law. It only requires Law to choose more rigorously what it needs to understand in order to control.

Finally, a tenth guideline requires that institutional examination preserve unity among supply, evidence and remedy. In IOCs, these planes cannot be rigidly compartmentalized. The way the object is offered interferes with the way the loss will be perceived. The way enjoyment is governed conditions the way the conflict can be proven. The way architecture distributes access and opacity influences the adequacy of the remedy. Artificially separating these planes produces decisions that are formally organized but p. 752 materially blind. Effective control depends on seeing them as parts of one and the same legal problem. That is what makes it possible to treat architecture and governance as elements of supply and not as a technically interesting but legally irrelevant backdrop.

These guidelines, taken together, offer an application criterion that rejects both atomized examination and inflamed generalization. What is proposed is more useful. A way of reading continuous digital conflicts from the regime that produces them, subjecting architecture and governance to the legitimacy filters that make private power institutionally controllable. It is at this point that the IOC ceases to be merely a descriptive category of new environments and asserts itself as a decision instrument able to organize evidence, qualification and legal response without the technical complexity of the environment operating as a practical obstacle to control.
