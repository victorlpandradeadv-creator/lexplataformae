7.2.6 On deepened finalism and the consumer by equivalence in platform ecosystems

See this section in the PDF

This section in the layers of the site

  • Dogmatics · Provisions cited
  • Platforms · Extension of the typology

p. 777

The incidence of the CDC in platform ecosystems requires a functional reading of the relationship, capable of going beyond contractual self-descriptions that, although formally elegant, are materially insufficient to explain the real supply regime. In environments governed by private architecture, the dispute is not adequately resolved by the simple question of who acquires what at the end of the chain, nor by the isolated verification of professionalism, economic instrumentality or peripheral insertion of the subject in the ecosystem. The decisive fact lies in the way the environment concentrates power, organizes asymmetry of meaning, produces evidentiary asymmetry and administers the very conditions of access, permanence, visibility, verification and contestation. Whenever this functional subjection proves legally relevant, the neutralization of consumer-law protection by civil appearances of parity must be rejected.

This point is especially important because platforms frequently operate through strategies of formal mischaracterization of the relationship. In some cases, they present themselves as mere neutral infrastructure for the encounter of autonomous parties. In others, they qualify the relationship as business collaboration, technological partnership, pure intermediation or simple licensing of access to a tool. In all these arrangements, contractual vocabulary tends to shift attention from the effective governance regime to the symmetrical appearance of the contractual frame. The dogmatic problem, however, lies not in the drafting sophistication of the contract, but in the materiality of the subjection produced by the ecosystem. When the platform concentrates the power to modulate enjoyment, to redistribute informational risks, to govern evidence and to condition the p. 778 continuity of the subject's insertion in the environment, formal parity comes to have limited explanatory value.

It is in this context that deepened finalism appears here as a reading criterion and not as an autonomous object of doctrinal exposition. Its usefulness lies in preventing the figure of the consumer from being compressed by an excessively narrow reading of the final recipient, incapable of capturing relationships in which vulnerability stems less from the classic position in the economic chain and more from submission to a private environment of concentrated governance. The focus, therefore, is not on indiscriminately inflating the concept of consumer, but on preventing the dogmatics of continuous supply from being neutralized by formal classifications that ignore who actually bears the architecture of risk, opacity and functional dependence. In ecosystems of this kind, vulnerability does not dissolve because the subject earns income, uses the platform to make their activity viable or presents themselves, to some extent, as an economically active agent. What matters is whether they remain subject, in asymmetric terms, to the private regime that organizes meaning, visibility, permanence and evidence.

This reading is particularly relevant for reaching situations in which sellers, partners, productive users of infrastructure and other participants economically inserted in the environment remain exposed to the platform's unilateral governance to a degree materially incompatible with a simple presumption of parity. The seller who depends on ranking, reputation, visibility and opaque prioritization rules does not become immune to consumer-law protection merely because they sell. The partner who uses the infrastructure to produce income does not cease to be vulnerable merely because they earn revenue. The peripheral professionalized user does not automatically become the strong party to the relationship just because their activity has an economic purpose. In all these cases, the legally relevant question lies in the density of the platform's governance over their insertion in the ecosystem. If permanence, the intelligibility of the environment, the reconstruction of the conflict and the contestation of relevant decisions remain concentrated under the platform's private control, the a priori exclusion of the CDC by formal label loses traction.

That is why functional exposure to governance practices and semiotic vulnerability must be treated here as central dimensions of factual vulnerability. The subject is not vulnerable only because they know less or because they have lower abstract economic capacity. They are vulnerable because they are inserted in an environment that produces the very legibility of the relationship, defines the criteria of internal relevance, administers the signals of trust, controls the records of what occurred and concentrates the power to change the conditions of enjoyment. On platforms, asymmetry is not limited to the content of the contract. It projects itself onto the way p. 779 in which the reality of the bond can be perceived, narrated and proven. This form of vulnerability reaches not only the classic consumer, but also other subjects functionally exposed to the concentrated governance of the ecosystem.

The usefulness of Article 29 of the CDC817 then appears with special force. Read functionally, it makes it possible to reach cases in which the subject, although not fitting comfortably into the traditional figure of the final acquirer as imagined in linear and simple relationships, is exposed to the commercial practices, supply methods and private governance mechanisms of the environment in a condition of legally relevant vulnerability. The point is not to dissolve every distinction between consumer and economic agent. It is to recognize that, in governed ecosystems, functional exposure to platform practices may produce situations in which consumer-law protection remains justified even when the relationship does not fit effortlessly into the classic picture of private domestic consumption. Dogmatics must be able to see the structure before deciding on exclusion.

This reading also prevents peripheral professionalization from being used as a universal screen against the CDC. In many platform environments, the subject may carry out their own economic activity, minimally organize their routine, earn income and still remain dependent on a private infrastructure they do not control, whose criteria of visibility, sanction, prioritization and evidence remain opaque and unilaterally modulable. There is no contradiction in this. The existence of the user's economic interest does not, by itself, eliminate the vulnerability arising from the governance of the ecosystem. The error would lie in presuming that any productive insertion automatically transforms structural asymmetry into a relationship between equals. On platforms, this presumption is frequently misleading precisely because the appearance of autonomy coexists with severe concentration of private power over the real conditions of operation.

The dogmatic consequence of this framing is important. The CDC may apply not only where there is a classic final acquisition, but also where the platform produces relevant functional subjection to a private governance environment that administers, in concentrated form, p. 780 access, permanence, visibility, verifiability and contestation. In these cases, the consumer by equivalence does not arise as a figure artificially expanded by theoretical generosity. It arises as a necessary response to supply models that have shifted the center of vulnerability into the architecture of the environment. The question then ceases to be whether the subject is “too much of a businessperson” to deserve protection and becomes whether the relationship, as structured, remains marked by material asymmetry of power and by submission to legally controllable private governance.

This does not authorize indiscriminate inflation of the category. The criterion remains demanding. Not every participant in a platform ecosystem will be a consumer by equivalence. Not every relationship economically mediated by technology will justify the incidence of the CDC. What is asserted is something more delimited and more serious. When the platform organizes the very intelligibility of the bond, concentrates the evidence of what it does, opaquely distributes opportunities and risks and conditions the continuity of the subject's insertion in the environment, the automatic exclusion of consumer-law protection by reference to nominal appearances of professionalism or partnership becomes methodologically poor and normatively dangerous. What decides is the real structure of subjection, not the contractual appearance of horizontality.

Read in this way, coherence is preserved with the constitutional matrix and with the functional reading of Article 29 of the CDC. Protection does not move beyond the system. On the contrary, it emerges from the very need to prevent new regimes of continuous and governed supply from escaping control by means of private vocabularies that simulate parity where there is dependence, simulate neutrality where there is concentrated management and simulate simple intermediation where there is actual unilateral organization of the experience. Deepened finalism, here, does not enter as a lecture. It enters as a tool to prevent dogmatic blindness before ecosystems in which vulnerability no longer presents itself in the classic garb of consumption, but remains fully alive under the logic of the platform.

Notes

  1. “Art. 29. For the purposes of this Chapter and the following one, all persons, whether determinable or not, exposed to the practices provided for therein are treated as equivalent to consumers” Brasil(Brasil, [2022]). In the case law of the STJ, the provision works as one of the bases of the so-called deepened finalist theory, because it allows, in exceptional cases, the strict reading of the final recipient to be mitigated and the application of the CDC to be recognized for natural or legal persons who, although not final consumers in the classical sense, find themselves in a situation of vulnerability or exposed to abusive commercial and contractual practices; the Court describes Art. 29 as bearing its own and very broad concept of consumer by equivalence, especially directed at the chapters on commercial practices and contractual protection, and draws from it the possibility of treating the vulnerable legal person as equivalent, above all when it acts outside its core of specialty, as a way of preventing formal parity from neutralizing the material protection of the CDC. ↩
