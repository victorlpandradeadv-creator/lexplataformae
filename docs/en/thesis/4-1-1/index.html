4.1.1 Sign systems and the production of meaning on digital platforms

p. 266

The extraction typical of IOCs depends on a regime of signification that makes the good intelligible, desirable and comparable in a governed ecosystem. The object only acquires economic form when it can be read as a position of enjoyment endowed with meaning, rarity, belonging and prestige. For this reason, the offer is not reduced to technical delivery nor exhausted in formal declarations, since it operates through signs that organize expectation and guide conduct. The semiotics of Umberto Eco290 enters as a descriptive instrument to establish the sign as a legally material component of the immaterial good, a practical condition of its social existence and of its techno-economic valorization.

The sign relevant to IOCs is not a label coupled to neutral content, because the meaning of the good depends on conventional correlations governed by code. In Eco, the sign p. 267 ceases to be treated as a static entity and comes to be understood as function, that is, as an operation of signification that occurs when elements of a plane of expression are correlated, by convention, with elements of a plane of content within a system, which is why the analysis must focus less on signs as things and more on the semiotic functions that constitute them Eco(Eco, 2000, p. 83-84). This formulation shifts the focus to the rules that allow something to count as something, which makes visible that the intelligibility of the object is produced by structural relations and, therefore, can be administered, stabilized and reconfigured by regimes of signification.

An operational analogy helps make the question under debate more evident. As a sign of sustenance, the “sausage”291, in material economies, refers to a referent that nourishes and is used up. The “digital sausage”, by contrast, in the form of a skin, a badge, a frame or a title, operates as an almost pure sign: it is not consumed, does not wear out, is not rival, and yet it organizes desire, conduct and hierarchy in the ecosystem. What is bought is not an object endowed with use in the material sense, but a semiotic function carefully positioned in the network of differences that makes the item legible as “rare”, “event”, “prestigious” or “of belonging”. In this regime, the sign does not accompany the offer. It is a constitutive part of what is recognized as delivery.

This structure reveals a decisive economic shift, since the marginal cost of producing the sign may tend to zero, but its capacity to capture value is maximized when the platform controls the grammar that defines what counts as distinction and what must be recognized as superior. Exchange, then, ceases to occur between things and comes to occur between time of life and symbolic positions. In practical terms, real hours of attention, permanence and performance are converted into markers whose existence depends only on the maintenance of the code and the circulation of recognition. The supplier's gain does not lie in delivering a “good” proportional to the consumer's effort, but in administering a regime of signification in which effort is socially converted into sign, and the sign, in turn, is reconverted into permanence and recurring monetization.

If the sign is function and not “thing”, the decisive point is how the platform organizes the correlations that make the good legible and desirable. In IOCs, the offer is a position of enjoyment, not mere technical access: its reading informs what the item “is”, what it is “worth” and why it matters. The correlation between expression and content, in this environment, does not occur as a neutral given, but as p. 268 directed production of meaning. The platform decides which marks, categories, narratives and signals will be correlated with the item to make it exist socially as rarity, belonging or prestige. For this reason, the semiotic regime is not an external layer of consumption, but a practical condition of existence of the economic object, because it transforms the immaterial good into a socially recognizable expectation.

The next step is to set aside any naturalistic reading of meaning and to establish that the content operated by the sign is a product of rules and shared repertoires. In Eco, signification is not anchored in a thing that would carry meaning by itself, since the very meaning of the term is treated as a cultural construction, in the sense that the meaning of a term is a cultural unit Eco(Eco, 2000, p. 111). This allows the value of IOCs to be described as something that stabilizes through codes and through distinctions internal to the system, and not through intrinsic properties of the item. The encyclopedia, in this framework, functions as a public repertoire that makes such correlations socially accessible, because semiotic processes circumscribe meanings without ever touching them directly, but make them operable through other cultural units Eco(Eco, 1991, p. 112-113). Thus, the meaning that sustains the promise of the IOC appears as a structured effect of rules and of a shared interpretive network, the condition by which the platform can produce, reinforce and also reconfigure the intelligibility of the good.

A direct way of observing what Eco describes as a cultural unit, operating under a regime of code, is to look at signs that “add” nothing to the technical performance of the game and, even so, reorder the social reading of the subject within the ecosystem. In League of Legends, a loading screen border associated with the user's ranked tier, or a progression icon, does not increase damage, does not alter range, does not improve the win rate. Even so, it functions as a public reading instruction, since it anticipates, in a few pixels, a set of inferences about seniority, effort, reliability, threat, prestige, belonging and hierarchical place, all of this even before any gameplay action. This economy of meaning is “clean” enough to show the almost pure sign, because it shifts delivery to the plane of legibility, comparison and recognition.

The shift does not occur by spontaneous generation. The platform administers the correlation between effort and sign, by producing and maintaining a stable set of marks that turns time of life into recognizable distinctions. The public support documentation itself describes “ranked regalia” as a visual representation of the player's rank, distributed among emblems and borders, and states that loading screen borders display the p. 269 current rank of the mode being played Riot Games(Riot Games, 2025g)292. The rewards policy reinforces this same mechanism by repeatedly linking visual rewards to tiers reached and to the highest tier obtained in time windows, converting a performance trajectory into signs that come to circulate as condensed reputation Riot Games(Riot Games, 2026b)293. If, in Eco's vocabulary, the encyclopedia is the public repertoire that makes certain correlations operable, here it is also an apparatus of design and support that keeps these correlations “up to date”, stabilizing what can be read as prestige and as relevant difference.

The result is a system of differences whose value is positional. A “Diamond” border is valuable precisely because it exists in contrast to “Iron”, “Bronze”, “Silver”, “Gold”, “Platinum” and “Emerald”. It is precisely because the ecosystem repeats the same visual idiom in every match, every lobby and every season, naturalizing the hierarchy as grammar, that the ranking system takes on such symbolic weight over players. In this sense, the public table of tiers and divisions makes explicit that distinction is not an intrinsic property of the item, but an effect of relational architecture and of criteria of promotion and demotion League of Legends Wiki(League of Legends Wiki, [202-?]j)294. The sign, thus, is not an ornament glued to neutral content. It is a constitutive component of what circulates as delivery, since the position of enjoyment is realized as a socially legible, comparable and recognized position.

This reading gains even more force when one observes that the supplier's own contract refuses to treat virtual content as an appropriable object in the classical sense and blocks its conversion outside the ecosystem. By stating that the user holds no title over the “Virtual Content”, that it cannot be redeemed for “real world” money and that the company denies monetary value to data and content residing on its servers, the contract makes clear that the economic substance of this kind of item is realized, primarily, p. 270 within the circuit of signs that the platform itself institutes and maintains Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e)295. In this key, the “digital sausage” is less a metaphor and more an operational description of the bond. Delivery consists of a recognizable place within the game's encyclopedia, in which borders and icons function as cultural units of prestige and as markers of social legibility, organizing desire and disciplining conduct through visibility.

The same logic appears even more transparently in the Challenges system, because there the platform assumes, without embarrassment, that part of the “delivery” is realized as a language of identity. Titles and tokens are not performance accessories. They are public labels that become companions of the player's name and come to circulate in the environment as a small vocabulary of reputation, in which the person chooses how he wants to be read. Riot describes this in institutional walkthrough material by stating that titles are earned through challenge progression, and that tokens can be equipped “to display”, with contextual reading by the system itself Riot Games(Riot Games, 2022d).296 In this design, the sign need not even disguise itself as a “cosmetic” tied to an avatar. It attaches to the subject himself as a short phrase, and, in doing so, reinforces the semiotic function as a cultural unit in an almost pure state, since what circulates there is a standardized and recognizable reading instruction, integrated into the protocol of the social encounter in the game.

The effect is that the regime of signs ceases to operate only at specific moments, such as the loading screen, and comes to form a continuous layer of presentation of the consumer within the ecosystem. The official support documentation does not treat titles and tokens as curiosities, but as elements of “identity customization”, with a defined place of equipping p. 271 and with the assumption that this choice has display value Riot Games(Riot Games, 2022b).297 If the encyclopedia, in Eco, organizes a shareable field of inferences, this kind of feature makes the encyclopedia “walk” on the user's body, since it converts feats and routines into marks ready for reading, standardizing the way the community recognizes “what someone is” in that world.

This standardization is, again, relational and governed. The institutional material on Challenges itself states that titles are “earned” by progression through tiers and that the player may freely choose among the unlocked titles, which reveals a double layer of administration: first the platform defines the tracks and criteria by which the sign is released, and then it offers the consumer a repertoire of self-presentation, which functions as a selection within a previously produced encyclopedia Riot Games(Riot Games, 2022d). In semiotic terms, the gesture is elegant and dangerous at the same time. It reduces subjectivity to a set of short and comparable markers, and intensifies the internal commensurability of the ecosystem, because it multiplies the ways in which the player can be “read” in real time, even when nothing is happening in the gameplay.

Finally, this second micro-example speaks directly to the contractual framing of Virtual Content. The platform may admit, as a baseline rule, that the user has no title, no exclusive right and no monetary conversion outside the ecosystem, and, even so, offer an abundance of identity signs as part of the consumption experience, precisely because the central value of these items is not outside, but inside the circuit of recognition. The contract, by denying cash value and blocking transfer and redemption, makes even more visible that this kind of delivery rests on the social encyclopedia and on the reading protocols instituted by the company itself Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e).298

If meaning is a cultural unit and if the encyclopedia delimits what can be recognized as meaning, the analysis of the IOC must treat rarity, belonging, prestige and “edition” as effects of a social grammar operated by the platform. These attributes do not emerge from the item as if they were natural properties, because they depend on codes that define relevant distinctions and on repertoires that make such distinctions interpretable in community. p. 272 The immaterial good comes to exist economically insofar as it can be situated within this network of differences, scripts and shared expectations, which explains why the platform governs not only access, but also the symbolic conditions of intelligibility that sustain the promise. When this administration reorganizes categories, reorders signals, reconfigures narratives or alters the mode of circulation of the item, the very form of social existence of the object is also altered, with direct impact on what the consumer understands he is acquiring and on how this acquisition can be recognized as valuable.

The reading of the offer, in these markets, does not operate by individual intuition, but upon a prior social grammar that organizes inferences and stabilizes expectations. Eco describes this basis as an encyclopedia in operation, in which lexical meanings enter as instructions for contextual insertion and are articulated with scripts, allowing the addressee to elaborate chains of inferences capable of making contextual meaning grow Eco(Eco, 1991, p. 79-80). Interpretation, therefore, takes the form of cooperation regulated by competences and by scripts that guide what may be expected of a situation and of a sign.

At the same time, this framework does not authorize interpretive relativism, because the encyclopedia acts as a limit. Eco describes it as a regulative hypothesis from which the addressee builds a local portion of repertoire and, with this, recognizes a series of semantic competences attributable to the text or to the sender Eco(Eco, 1991, p. 114-115). On platforms, this dynamic means that signals of rarity, belonging, exclusivity or prestige function as coded instructions that project conducts and expectations of enjoyment, while the consumer remains dependent on the encyclopedia administered and activated by the techno-media environment itself.

If interpretation depends on scripts, instructions and regulative hypotheses, the promise of the IOC operates as a continuous organization of conduct, not as information prior to purchase. The platform provides signals and contexts that guide the reading of what the item “is” and of what it publicly “puts at stake”. Thus, desire, urgency, comparison and belonging cease to be lateral psychological effects and become predictable results of an engineering of signification that stabilizes expectations in real time. With this, enjoyment tends to coincide with performance, and the value of the good comes to depend on public recognition of its position within the ecosystem, which makes permanence part of the product itself and prepares the ground for understanding why visibility and social proof will be treated, further on, as the economic infrastructure of consumption.

The dynamic by which these signals produce and sustain meaning is not organized as a linear sequence, but as a network in continuous circulation, in which connections recombine and p. 273 start again even after ruptures. Eco describes this functioning through the model of the rhizome299, in which “each point of the rhizome can be joined and must be joined with any other point” and in which there are no fixed positions, but “lines of connection”, capable of breaking and “starting again following its own line” Eco(Eco, 1991, p. 115-116).

In this framework, meaning does not depend on an isolated act of reading, since it is guaranteed by the chaining of the system itself, which provides successive interpretants and maintains the validity of the sign beyond the occasional presence of an interpreter. Eco formulates this idea by maintaining that the interpretant is what guarantees the validity of the sign even in the absence of the interpreter and that signification is established through successive referrals in a process of unlimited semiosis Eco(Eco, 2000, p. 114). On platforms, this makes it possible to describe the promise of the IOC as the effect of a network of signs that reinforces itself through circulation and recombination, making value something reiterated and stabilized in the environment itself, while preserving openness to reconfigurations that need not be announced as an explicit change of content.

When meaning stabilizes through successive referrals, the platform comes to govern value without directly touching the technical “content”. The promise can be reinforced, displaced or emptied through curation of connections and through alterations of visibility, relevance and reading scripts. Thus, permanence is sustained not only by an exit barrier, but by continuous semiotic reiteration, in which the consumer is kept in a circuit of recognition, comparison and updating of meaning. This mode of functioning makes it plausible that relevant reconfigurations occur as diffuse adjustments of the ecosystem, that is, as changes in the network that sustains interpretants, and not necessarily as a formal breakage of an item, which helps explain why the verifiability of the link between promise and enjoyment becomes a structural problem.

The networked openness of semiosis does not imply chaotic drift, since social life itself imposes cuts of stability so that communication and the coordination of expectations are possible. Eco makes explicit that, in each context, certain properties are held as peacefully accepted, not by ontological necessity, but because it is pragmatically necessary to take something for granted Eco(Eco, 1991, p. 133). At the same time, this stabilization does not freeze meaning into a fixed essence, since the network of referrals continues to operate and semiosis circumscribes cultural units by making them, in fact, accessible Eco(Eco, 2000, p. 117-118). In this framework, the platform can administer the promise of the IOC through local cuts p. 274 of consensus and through reconfigurations in the chaining of signs, maintaining the public intelligibility of the good while gradually shifting the conditions that sustain its reading and its value.

If the stability of meaning depends on pragmatic cuts of consensus, then the promise of the IOC functions as an interpretive pact continually renewed within the ecosystem. The platform preserves “accepted” traits to maintain public recognition of the item and, at the same time, shifts connections, narratives and relevances without explicitly breaking delivery. This helps explain why semiotic governance is especially effective: it manages to maintain the appearance of public continuity of the object while altering, from within, the conditions of interpretation that sustain its value. The asymmetry is not limited to access to contractual information, but reaches the capacity to perceive when the cut of consensus was altered, by which criteria and with what effects on enjoyment and on the symbolic position acquired.

The semiotic shift needed here consists in separating the intelligibility of the good from any reading that treats the sign as a label glued to a thing. In Eco, content does not coincide with a raw entity, since a given civilization organizes content into fields, axes and subsystems and cuts the continuum in such a way that these segments correspond not only to physically recognizable entities, but also to culturally instituted relations and directions Eco(Eco, 1991, p. 61). This distinction gains an even more direct formulation when one considers the functioning of the code, because, in mentioning states of the world, the expression does not primarily transmit an object, insofar as, in principle, an expression does not designate an object, but transmits a cultural content Eco(Eco, 2000, p. 102). For IOCs, this makes it possible to describe the offer as an organization of intelligibility and expectation, in which the platform does not add an image to a neutral good, but institutes the cultural forms through which the object exists socially, is recognized as valuable and can be enjoyed as a position within the ecosystem.

If the offer transmits cultural content and if civilization cuts the world into fields of intelligibility, the immaterial good cannot be reduced to “file” or “access”, because what is acquired includes the social form through which the item is perceived, compared and recognized. On platforms, this form is produced by classifications, narratives, categories, signals and scripts that define what counts as rarity, what counts as belonging and what counts as prestige.

From this point on, the description of the IOC can definitively abandon the image of the “product” as a closed unit and treat its economic form as an effect of semiotic organization. The p. 275 acquisition does not deliver merely an isolated element, but a package of intelligibility that includes criteria of pertinence, comparability and public recognition, that is, a socially stabilized mode of reading the item. For this reason, the sign does not enter as adornment, but as part of the material regime of the offer, because it is the sign that fixes what the good is taken to be, in what terms it is desirable and under what parameters it becomes measurable within the ecosystem. This key also delimits the type of legal control that becomes necessary. If value depends on administered semiotic conditions, the problem is not solved merely by examining clauses or by verifying technical execution. It requires criteria capable of making the link between promise and enjoyment verifiable in an environment in which the very intelligibility of the good can be reconfigured by continuous governance.

The possibility of treating the production and circulation of signs as a governable dimension depends on recognizing that communication does not operate by spontaneity, but through prior systemic conditions. Eco makes explicit that any process of communication between human beings presupposes a system of signification as a necessary condition300 Eco(Eco, 2000, p. 25). In the same sense, the production of signs, especially in the production of contexts, only becomes possible thanks to the foreseen rules provided by the code, which is not reduced to correlations, but includes combinatory rules and can be understood as competence301 Eco(Eco, 2000, p. 144). This competence is not a private attribute, since the code, understood as “language”, constitutes a sum of notions that form the speaker's total competence and, at the same time, arises as a collective convention composed of the sum of individual competences302 Eco(Eco, 2000, p. 198). On these bases, controlling rules, contextual instructions and criteria of competence that precede the concrete occurrence of the sign means controlling the conditions of intelligibility and acceptability through which the promise of the IOC becomes operative, recognizable and economically valuable in the ecosystem.

The sign system can be treated as an operative mechanism because it organizes meaning through internal rules of pertinence and through positional values, and not through some essence of the object. Eco formulates this by maintaining that, in the functioning of the system, neither psychic p. 276 ideas nor objects or referents enter, but values that emanate from the system itself, definable in terms of differences and by the position they occupy in the network of oppositions Eco(Eco, 2000, p. 122). This framing prevents reducing the system to a rigid list of equivalences, since the code, understood in its complexity, functions as a machine that provides instructions for maneuvering systems of equivalences in various contexts and circumstances, approaching the model of the encyclopedia as socially stabilized competence Eco(Eco, 1991, p. 286).

On platforms, this double key explains why the intelligibility of the IOC is administrable. Value is sustained by recognizable differences and reading instructions, which can be recalibrated by the governance of context without formal rupture of the item.

What the platform governs when it governs the IOC now comes into sharper focus. It is not merely a matter of controlling access, catalog or purchase frictions, but of controlling the pertinent differences that make an item count as rare, prestigious, “event” or “of belonging”, as well as controlling the contextual instructions that tell the consumer how this difference should be read and performed. Since value emanates from the system and is defined by position, changes in visibility, categorization, interpretation scripts or connections with other signs reconfigure the very economic form of the good, even if the file, the model or the minimum functionality remain technically “intact”. For this reason, the promise of the IOC cannot be audited merely by inspecting the isolated item, since it depends on the systemic conditions that stabilize its meaning and on the contextual maneuvers the platform can carry out over time, reordering the network without announcing an explicit substitution.

At this point, it becomes easier to perceive why position is not a psychological metaphor, but a structure of value. Eco indicates that, when a good enters a system of exchange, its value ceases to be expressed only as use and comes to take on a relational character, that is, it becomes exchange value within a system of equivalences, in which the commodity functions as the form of expression of this value Eco(Eco, 2000, p. 48-49). This logic helps describe the IOC as a unit whose relevance depends on the position it occupies in the ecosystem of signs administered by the platform.

Moreover, Eco makes explicit that cultural systems can restructure themselves, such that a semantic field begins to dissolve to give way to another organized differently, a process that produces confusions and disputes and that often accompanies cultural divisions that are also class divisions Eco(Eco, 2000, p. 130-131). In platform markets, this means that reordering categories, criteria and axes of comparison can reconfigure the social value of the good without any need to alter the object itself, because what changes is the field in which it has value and the grammar that makes it commensurable.

p. 277 If value is positional and if fields can be dissolved and reorganized, then the semiotic governance of the platform appears as a concrete form of economic power. The platform decides which axes of opposition matter, which distinctions will be visible and which criteria will make an item jump from “common” to “rare”, from “old” to “vintage”, from “event” to “collectible”, or from “relevant” to “forgotten”. Thus, what seems mere aesthetic curation or interface adjustment functions as a restructuring of the field, with direct effects on the measurement, recognition and circulation of value.

In this environment, the consumer does not negotiate price alone, because what the consumer buys is insertion into a system of equivalences that can be recalibrated over time, shifting value without needing to touch the minimum materiality of the item. Semiotic vulnerability then becomes even sharper, given that the asymmetry lies not only in ignorance of contractual terms, but in the practical inability to audit when the system has reconfigured the axes and, with this, reconfigured the value of the position of enjoyment acquired.

From this fit between exchange value and the restructuring of fields, the sign ceases to be only a condition of intelligibility and comes to appear as infrastructure of social competition. If the position of the good in the system is what makes it commensurable and desirable, then visibility, comparison and social proof are not “side effects” of consumption, but means by which value is produced and kept in circulation. The platform environment functions as a device that exposes these positions, accelerates oppositions and makes hierarchy legible in real time, which turns enjoyment into display and turns acquisition into a gesture that needs to be recognized in order to fulfill its economic function. It is from this point that the analysis can advance to the dynamics of spectacle, not as cultural ornament, but as a form of governance that stabilizes desire and permanence through continuous mediation of visibility and reputation.

In social environments, utility does not circulate in pure form, since practices and objects come to operate as signs of the position they occupy and of the value they carry before others. Eco formulates this by stating that, from the moment society exists, every function is automatically transformed into a sign of that function Eco(Eco, 2000, p. 47). This means that use already carries a public and comparable dimension, which opens space for recognition and social differentiation. In the same sense, the object becomes a semantic unit of distinction when it indicates a certain social position and acquires symbolic value, being able to signify wealth or other attributes even when it presents itself as an object Eco(Eco, 2000, p. 52). For IOCs, this structure describes why value is not stabilized only by the technical attributes of the item, but by its capacity to function as a recognizable sign of belonging and prestige within p. 278 the ecosystem, which is why it becomes central to understand how such differences are produced, maintained and made legible in circulation.

When function becomes sign and when objects indicate social position, the platform gains a specific kind of power, since it controls the mechanisms through which reputation, belonging and distinction become legible and comparable. The promise of the IOC comes to depend not only on having the item, but on being able to make it count publicly as what it signifies within that ecosystem. This makes visibility a condition of enjoyment. In this logic, permanence is not only technical permanence, but social permanence, that is, remaining inserted in the circuit in which difference is perceived, hierarchy is updated and status is validated in real time. This allows the next move to introduce spectacle as a mechanism of continuous mediation of comparison, by explaining how the platform converts visibility into discipline of conduct and into recurrent renewal of desire.

Signs of narrative promise operate as a privileged way of converting code into desire, because they offer the consumer a script of meaning ready to be inhabited. Trailers, splash arts, event texts, cinematics, “seasons” and collection milestones do not merely describe an item, but organize anticipation, belonging and prestige as if they were natural and spontaneous attributes of the good. Narrative promise functions as a frame that decides what should be seen as rare, memorable, “occasional”, “community” or “identity”, and makes this decision look like evidence of the object itself, when in reality it is a construction of the ecosystem. Thus, the IOC becomes desirable not only for what it allows one to do, but for the role it enacts in the shared imagination and for the position it promises within a continually reactivated collective story. For the central argument at hand, this requires treating narrative as an operative component of the offer, since it projects expectations, guides reading and provides the grammar through which enjoyment comes to be recognized as “delivery” and as value.

Narrative promise does not operate as rhetorical embellishment, because it functions as a mechanism of instruction that selects reading paths and, with this, produces expectation as part of the offer. In Eco, these contextual selections303 do not merely accompany meaning, but distinguish and direct reading paths, determining denotations and connotations and performing the function of a switch, in the sense of a railway switch, which p. 279 routes interpretation along tracks foreseen by the system itself Eco(Eco, 2000, p. 169). The consequence is that discourse provides reading rules even before any isolated content, since language itself, far from being a mere association between signifiers and signifieds, provides discursive rules that are part of the pragmatic functioning of signification Eco(Eco, 2000, p. 146). For IOCs, this makes it possible to describe trailers, events and collection narratives as devices that channel inferences, filter what counts as relevant and stabilize anticipations of what the item “promises”, making expectation a coded product of the ecosystem and preparing the ground for understanding how this codification also organizes comparability and internal measurement of value.

This interpretive steering is not limited to producing desire, because it also provides a grammar of economic comparability that makes the promise measurable within the ecosystem itself. Bundles, collections, availability windows, progression tracks and value labels are not merely sales formats, since they function as signals that translate qualitative differences into differences with the appearance of measure, creating internal proportions, scales and operational equivalences. Thus, the consumer comes to compare not only items, but positions of enjoyment, assessing cost, rarity and opportunity according to parameters that the platform itself institutes and recalibrates over time. The consequence is that the value of the IOC is stabilized less by an intrinsic property of the item and more by the possibility of situating it within a symbolically guided system of exchanges and substitutions, in which “expensive”, “advantageous”, “exclusive” or “unmissable” are effects of codification. This requires, in the next step, grounding how sign systems produce rules of measure and comparison even when the material basis of the good remains volatile and reconfigurable.

Economic comparability in IOCs is sustained by semiotic rules that function as cultural measures of pertinence, selecting which equivalences should hold in each context and which readings should be treated as implausible. Eco makes explicit that theory should not list all possible occurrences, but only those culturally and conventionally recognized as statistically most probable Eco(Eco, 2000, p. 176), which indicates that the system organizes a hierarchy of acceptable interpretations by stabilized criteria of cultural probability, and not by the essence of a referent. In platform terms, this helps describe why labels, bundles and value windows not only inform price, but also instruct the consumer to compare and substitute according to scales internalized by the ecosystem, filtering what counts as equivalent, advantageous or adequate even when the material basis of the item remains volatile.

p. 280 Signs of social validation operate as a mechanism for publicizing value, because they make the position of enjoyment immediately legible to third parties. Badges (badges), borders, emblems, titles, frames, public visual effects and other marks aimed at putting the consumer in evidence do not merely accompany the item, but convert acquisition into recognition and enjoyment into demonstration. In this arrangement, the good is also worth something for what it allows one to display and for the social confirmation it produces, such that the utility of the IOC becomes inseparable from its visibility and from its capacity to function as proof of belonging and distinction in real time. With this, the platform comes to govern not only access to the good, but the conditions under which value circulates and stabilizes as reputation, preparing the ground for treating public comparison and the continuous mediation of visibility as economic infrastructure of permanence.

The stability of the sign depends on circulation and community institutionalization, since the relation between a vehicle and a content only becomes operative when it comes to function as a verifiable convention outside any private mental state. Eco illustrates this point by distinguishing individual inference from the socialized rule, indicating that the relation between spots and measles only becomes semiotic when it has been conventionalized and recorded in medical treatises, since there is a sign whenever a human group decides to use one thing as the vehicle of any other Eco(Eco, 2000, p. 36).

Since what counts as prestige, belonging or distinction must be legible to third parties and sustained by rules of circulation, the decisive point becomes who controls these conditions. On platforms, the administration of the semiotic regime is not limited to communicating an offer, because it involves governing the storefront, ordering, categories, display contexts, mechanisms of social proof and criteria of relevance that make an IOC count as rare, prestigious or belonging to a specific circuit. Thus, the platform governs the object itself by governing the correlations that stabilize its public intelligibility, being able to reinforce, displace or empty promises without ostensibly altering the isolated item.

The relevant asymmetry arises, then, when interpretation depends on publicly recorded prior conditions, but the addressee has no effective access to these conditions to verify the link between promise and delivery. Eco sets aside psychologism and situates interpretation on an objective and social plane, by stating that interpretants do not necessarily depend on unreachable mental representations of subjects and are collectively verifiable, because a relation of interpretation is recorded in the treasure-house of intertextuality, a notion identified with that of the encyclopedia Eco(Eco, 1991, p. 112-113).

p. 281 Under the paradigm of IOCs, this means that the promise is not auditable by individual reading alone, because the meaning that sustains value and enjoyment is stabilized by rules and records of the ecosystem, while the platform controls which correlations will be activated and which reading paths will be made available. Semiotic vulnerability describes, on this plane, a structural loss of the capacity for verification, and not a defect of the consumer's attention.

Hence the centrality of requirements such as auditability and functional value equivalence, taken up further on not as conceptual adornment, but as a response to the problem of proof that emerges when delivery comes to depend on governed and reconfigurable semiotic conditions. With this delimited, the next step is to address how the platform converts this semiotic governance into continuous mediation of visibility and reputation, that is, how spectacle functions as economic infrastructure of permanence by stabilizing desire and discipline through public comparison.
