3.1.2 Media, entertainment and the reconfiguration of epistemology

See this section in the PDF

  • Foundations · Sections of chapter 3

p. 98

Postman53 (2006) makes it possible to understand media as a form of organization of social knowledge, and not merely as a channel for the circulation of messages. The passage from electronic to digital media deepens this shift: public space is progressively recoded by the grammar of spectacle, while the ways of producing, legitimizing and recognizing knowledge come to depend on formats, rhythms and criteria of attention specific to the dominant media ecology. This diagnosis has a direct impact on the way consumers perceive products, services and platforms.

In media ecologies, the decisive problem lies not only in the content that circulates, but in the form of circulation that comes to organize perception and credibility. Formats, rhythms, expectations of attention and criteria of relevance become embodied social habits. At this point, Postman's reading serves to describe the passage from a culture strongly organized by argumentative linearity to one traversed by the performativity of show business, in which visibility, impact and retention come to condition the way something becomes socially intelligible.

Postman's contribution matters not as nostalgia for print culture, but as a key for understanding that each medium establishes its own ecology of perception and knowledge. By recoding politics, religion and education in terms of entertainment, television alters criteria of informational relevance, credibility and acceptability of public debate. In the digital environment, this shift does not disappear: it is radicalized by p. 99 platforms capable of personalizing flows, modulating visibility and transforming attention into an operational variable.

For consumption, this shift is decisive. When relevance, credibility and acceptability come to be modulated by entertainment formats, the perception of value is also reorganized. The consumption decision no longer depends primarily on checks of adequacy and performance and is instead mediated by regimes of attention, reputation and belonging. Within them, the form of presentation and the dramaturgy of the offer, expressed in storefronts, trailers, events and narratives of permanent updating, becomes a constitutive component of the experience consumed.

In the platform-operated game, this reconfiguration appears in concentrated form. The value of the object is not apprehended only through its functional characteristics, but through the public staging that precedes and accompanies it. Official media transform items, characters, seasons and events into shareable narrative repertoires, converting purchase and enjoyment into symbolic continuity within the ecosystem.

Riot Games' audiovisual production functions, in this sense, as a device of knowledge applied to consumption. Media do not limit themselves to publicizing what already exists, since they are capable of defining what counts as relevant information about the object, which traits should be perceived as essential and which affects can operate as criteria of evaluation. The consumer does not need to know the item as a technical structure to recognize it as a desirable good. They know it as image, narrative and repertoire of socially shareable signals, produced and distributed by official channels in the format of spectacle. A music video such as POP/STARS fonte(K/DA..., 2018), an example of the productivity of the Riot Music division, is especially relevant because it transforms a set of cosmetics into a public cultural reference prior to use, operating as a matrix of intelligibility for the purchase and for the social recognition of the item's value.

The same logic intensifies in the seasonal cinematics, through which the company presents a visual synthesis of the universe and of the expectations it wishes to stabilize as a common horizon. Still Here Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024d), in presenting itself as a season-opening cinematic and condensing the editorial statement “Fight for tomorrow”54, makes explicit this function of guiding p. 100 the gaze and expectation. Here, the epistemology of consumption does not correspond to expanded access to data about the object, but to a form of mediated knowledge in which the image becomes a criterion of pragmatic truth. The item is what the media allow it to be seen as being. Consequently, utility ceases to be the sole axis of decision and gives way to aesthetic intelligibility, to narrative promise and to the repertoire of signs that enables belonging and distinction within the community.

This media reconfiguration finds a correspondence in the declared norm of the ecosystem itself, insofar as the contract describes skins and related items as licensed Virtual Content, linked to the account, and not as the consumer's property55. When the offer is structured as spectacle, but the object is legally presented as revocable or modifiable access, a tension central to the thesis is formed: the consumer knows the good through its aesthetic and community staging, while its stability remains subject to the platform's technical-contractual regime. For this reason, changes to visuals, animation, effects, symbolic balancing or access conditions cannot be treated merely as functional corrections, bugs or graphic improvements56. In this picture, the epistemology of consumption becomes inseparable from the media architecture that frames what the object “is” and from the contractual regime that controls how much of this “being” remains stable.

The consumption promise is, therefore, publicly performed, while enjoyment is mediated by signals of visibility, prestige and belonging. The unilateral alteration of the item or the character may affect not only a technical utility, but the symbolic position that was produced by the platform itself. Postman contributes precisely at this point, since his critique, formerly directed at show business, once updated by metrified digital ecosystems p. 101, makes it possible to understand how criteria of credibility and value are reorganized until the design of the offer and the dramaturgy of permanence become part of the content consumed.

According to Postman, the rise of television to hegemony among the media redefined the parameters by which information is processed, truths are conceived and intellectual capacities are evaluated. The passage from typographic culture to television altered not only the medium of communication; it transformed criteria of truth, intelligence and seriousness in public discourse Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 23]). Under this regime, television favors fragmentation, reduces the possibility of more complex discourse and replaces the argumentative coherence of typographic culture with public conversations marked by triviality, broken sequence and the progressive abandonment of logic, reason and the rules of contradiction Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 58]).

Politics is one of the central manifestations57 of this shift. For Postman, television reconfigures it as a branch of show business, transforming public debate into a product compatible with entertainment Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 16]). Campaigns come to operate through the formation of images, the management of performances and the replacement of ideologies by appearance, as if political value could be absorbed by the efficacy of ready-made scenes. In this context, “knowing” politics comes to mean visual familiarity, and not necessarily understanding structured by language and discourse Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 86]).

In education, the pursuit of entertainment also alters the criteria of learning. The critical and in-depth process loses ground to recreational forms of access to content, while difficulty, intellectual discipline and the duration of cognitive effort come to be seen as obstacles to efficient communication Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 146]). Hence the assertion that television and image culture do not constitute merely a style of communication, but a threat to the foundations of liberal democracy, insofar as they strike at its material basis: freedom of information Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 91]).

The conversion of the public space into show business matters less for its sectoral object than for its mechanism. The point lies in the formation of a regime in which signs and performances progressively replace arguments as criteria of recognition, p. 102 trust and value. This structure is decisive for the thesis because, on digital platforms, the object of consumption is not merely presented to the consumer; it is staged in formats that produce trust, urgency and adherence, making the consumption experience inseparable from the ecology of attention in which it is promoted and enjoyed.

The central mechanism is fragmentation. The image dismembers reality by tearing moments from their contexts and juxtaposing events without logical or historical connection, recreating the world as a series of idiosyncratic happenings Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 55]). In this environment, intelligence comes to mean knowing many things, and not knowing about them, that is, accumulating disconnected data without in-depth understanding Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 74]). Disinformation, therefore, is not limited to falsity, since it also manifests itself as displaced, irrelevant, fragmented or superficial information, capable of producing an illusion of knowledge and distancing the subject from actual knowing Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 74]).

This form of informational circulation produces a present without historicity, in which television operates as a medium centered on the now and hinders the integration of experience into a broader temporal narrative Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 76]). Public discourse thus loses the form that made it suited to rationality: under print culture, it tended toward coherence, seriousness and continuity; under televisual governance, it comes to be organized by visuality, brevity and the demand for immediate interest Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 65-66]). Information is presented in simplified, insubstantial and decontextualized form, not necessarily through explicit censorship, but because television operates as a meta-medium, directing both what is known of the world and the social ways of knowing Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 58]).

Following this change of foundation, politics, religion, news, education and commerce come to be rearranged as congenial adjuncts of show business, and culture tends to become a generalized arena of entertainment Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 58-59]).

The effect is not only aesthetic, but institutional, because liberal democracy depends on an informational ecology capable of sustaining discernment and deliberation. In this key, the contemporary threat to democracy need not take the form of censorship, since it is realized when political discourse takes the form of entertainment and neutralizes the very seriousness of public debate Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 91-93]). By shifting the center of discourse to performance, the medium lowers the public centrality of substantive themes and weakens the social conditions of critical thought, above all when information is presented in a simplistic and non-contextual way, that is, as information packaged as entertainment Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 92]).

p. 103 If Ellul shows technique as a structural environment, Postman makes explicit how a specific medium, television, reorganizes ways of knowing, debating and deciding. The central point is not television as an isolated historical object, but the finding that the form of the medium alters the form of socially practicable rationality. When public discourse comes to be measured by the capacity to amuse and retain attention, and not by argumentative density or the search for truth, each sphere must fit into short, emotionally marked and visually striking frames.

In this key, television inaugurates an epistemology centered on the image and on fragmented rhythm. Information arrives in brief and disconnected units, which require little effort of concentration and do not favor historical sequencing or causal reconstruction. The problem, for this thesis, lies in the formation of a cultural grammar trained to consume stimuli, and not to organize experiences into minimally stable maps of meaning.

In the digital environment, this logic does not disappear: it is intensified, personalized and made interactive. Feeds, timelines, notifications and gamified interfaces transplant to platforms the same regime of fragmentation, now combined with the permanent collection of data and the algorithmic modulation of attention. The consumer no longer merely watches the flow, since they interact with it, produce data, receive feedback and are continuously repositioned by the architecture of the service. Entertainment ceases to be58 separate content and comes to function as a technical environment of permanence. It is in this governed flow of experiences, adjusted to maximize engagement, time spent and monetization, that the IOC comes to take shape as an object of continued enjoyment.

In transposing Postman's diagnosis to the digital environment, the decisive point is not to assert that television became the internet, but to identify structural shifts in the ecology of attention: from uniform diffusion to personalized distribution; from predominantly passive reception to continuous interaction; from crude audience metrics to the fine metrification of conduct. These shifts reorganize knowledge and attention and, therefore, have direct implications for consumption. The offer comes to depend on technical curation, while the consumer's experience becomes continuously modulable.

p. 104 In this light, the vulnerability of the digital consumer cannot be reduced to a lack of information that more notices, terms of use or highlighted clauses could correct. The duty to inform remains relevant, but it comes to operate within an ecology in which form, curation and visibility precede reading. Before any content is actually interpreted, the technical and media presentation of the object has already defined what appears as visible, urgent, desirable or natural. The will is exercised within a repertoire of references and stimuli previously choreographed by the platform. This asymmetry in the production of meaning sets the stage for the category of semiotic vulnerability, understood as a structural disadvantage in the grammar of meanings that guides the subject's relationship with the immaterial object of consumption. Architecture thus becomes practical normativity because it organizes the field of the perceived, the credible and the natural before the informational duty can produce its effects.

Notes

  1. Influenced by the theory of Jacques Ellul, in the work Amusing Ourselves to Death: Public Discourse in the Age of Show Business (“Divertindo-nos até a morte: o discurso público na era do show business”, our translation), originally published in 1985, the American educator Neil Postman (2006) investigates the influence of television on American culture, exploring the ways in which television, once it became the dominant medium of communication, transformed public discourse into entertainment and had a profound impact on the social, cultural and political epistemologies of the United States. ↩
  2. The cinematic acts as an editorial form of knowledge: it frames what should be seen as relevant and offers a way of reading, instead of offering technical data about items. In the official material “Still Here | Season 2024 Cinematic”, the page itself organizes the content under the title and the statement “Fight for tomorrow.” Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024d). This shows the epistemological function of audiovisual content in the Postmanian sense: the content is presented as a key to interpreting the “now” and the “next”, shaping expectations and criteria of relevance, without the consumer needing to access any technical description of the object of consumption associated with the ecosystem. ↩
  3. The contract qualifies the object of consumption as something that is known not by inspecting the good, but through the access granted under a service regime. Riot states that it may offer the opportunity to “acquire a limited license to access [...] (‘Virtual Content’)” Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e). It then states that the user “has no ownership or right over any Virtual Content” and that unlocking occurs through a “limited non-transferable license” Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e). In epistemological terms, this is decisive because it shifts “knowledge of the object” outside the thing itself, since what exists legally is a permission to access. The public perception of what the item “is” comes to depend even more on the mediations that make this access intelligible and desirable. ↩
  4. The declared norm admits that the identity of the object may change, and this alters the type of “truth” the consumer is able to form about the item at the moment of purchase. The contract defines what is offered as “a license and a personal right [...] revocable” Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e). It then makes explicit the unilateral power to “delete, alter, [...] repackage, reprice” content, and records that “Virtual Products may [...] evolve over time”, including through “game balance, bug fixes, or graphic improvements” Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e). The point is not rhetorical. If the contract assumes that the item may “evolve”, audiovisual and narrative mediation comes to function as a practical source of knowledge of the good, not as ornament, because it stabilizes, on the public plane, what should be understood as the identity and value of the object, even though its configuration remains manageable and alterable. ↩
  5. In the religious field, Postman maintains that television converts religion, without ceremony, into entertainment, removing what made it a historical, profound and sacred practice, with the loss of ritual, dogma, tradition, theology and, above all, spiritual transcendence Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 79]). In this formatting, preachers cease to be mere mediators and come to function as media protagonists, so that “their featured players become celebrities” Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 82]). The author describes this shift with explicit irony in saying that, in these programs, “the preacher is tops” and “God comes out as second banana” Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 79]). God “exists only in our minds”, while the preacher is “there, to be seen, admired, adored”, becoming the star of the show under an essentially structural logic Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 83]). ↩
  6. Postman's final contribution lies in a warning about the dangers of the "distracted society" that the communication mechanism had created, a concept that resonates forcefully in the contemporary context, in which the reality observed by Postman (2006) was raised to exponential levels through the insertion of new digital communication technologies that only reinforced and amplified the dynamics the author had observed. In this context, reflection is needed on the impacts of the media (in the broad sense) on the human capacity to think critically, to understand reality and thus to develop the skills necessary for engaged action as citizens of an increasingly complex society. ↩
