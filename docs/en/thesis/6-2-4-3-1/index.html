6.2.4.3.1 The case of the irregular gifts

p. 609

What is here called the Case of the Irregular Gifts offers a particularly expressive example of how semiotic vulnerability can be converted into allegation, evidence and consequence within the CDC. The interest of the episode lies not only in the occurrence of financial fraud in the Riot Games ecosystem. Fraud, chargeback and improper p. 610 use of means of payment are known risks in digital markets and, by themselves, do not exhaust the legal relevance of the case. The decisive point lies in the way the platform responded to this risk. Instead of treating fraud as a structural problem of its own transactional environment, Riot redistributed the risk onto an undifferentiated mass of consumers, suppressed assets, interrupted an official functionality for the circulation of content and applied sanctions founded on broad presumptions of co-responsibility, including on users who did not knowingly take part in the fraud. The systemic failure of the ecosystem was thus converted into a private criterion of collective attribution to the consumer.

The first layer of the case is that of the institutional normality of gifting. The gifting function did not operate on the margins of the platform, nor did it constitute a clandestine or intrinsically deviant practice. On the contrary, Riot itself presented it as a regular functionality, integrated into the game's client, subject to objective eligibility requirements, quantitative limits, regional restrictions and standardized sending flows. This is relevant because it rules out any attempt to reconstruct the episode as if consumers had entered, from the outset, a manifestly unlawful zone or one outside the official environment.

Gifting was part of the ordinary architecture of the service. The consumer's trust in the lawfulness of the circulation of virtual content through this channel was not a subjective whim. It was trust produced by the platform itself and supported by an officially offered functionality692.

The second layer is that of the operational risk already known to the supplier. Riot maintains, in more than one title of its portfolio, specific guidance for cases of unauthorized charges, financial fraud and subsequent disputes of transactions. These communications show that the problem of chargeback and improper use of means of payment was not an external, extraordinary or unforeseeable fact. It was an institutionally recognized operational risk, with internal procedures for investigation, refund and suspension693. This significantly p. 611 alters the legal framing of the case. When the supplier already knows the fragility of the transactional ecosystem, it cannot treat its effects as if they were residual fault of the consumer or an accident entirely exogenous to the activity. The risk becomes part of the professional sphere of the economic operation itself.

It is precisely at this point that the response adopted by the platform becomes legally problematic. The company deactivated the gifting functionality on the Brazilian server for a long period, suppressed received assets and carried out regressive restorations or blocks on affected accounts, without any equivalent effort being visible, at the level of private policy, to distinguish consistently between intentional fraud, excusable error, acquisition through an apparently legitimate channel and the good faith of the recipient. Since 2020, users have perceived the restriction not as a brief and proportionate measure, but as a lasting functional amputation, accompanied by institutional silence, occasional reintroductions and new suppressions. Community reports are not the central proof of an unlawful act, but they are useful for formulating and illustrating a long-lasting consumer experience, albeit a hypothetical one, in which one can discern the restriction and sedimentation of a broad punitive policy over the Brazilian user base694.

From the standpoint of semiotic vulnerability, the case is especially fertile because it shows that private attribution was built not only on a raw financial fact, but also on a scrambled communicative field. The consumer did not face a simple distinction between an official channel and a flagrantly fraudulent one. The market for Riot Points and external p. 612 codes was traversed by a semiotic gray zone, in which promotional materials and digital storefronts presented the external purchase of virtual currency as an ordinary, safe, advantageous practice compatible with the game's ecosystem. The language used in these environments mimicked commercial legitimacy, visual professionalism, subsequent functional integration with the official client, plausible promises of savings and a rhetoric of security695. In this setting, it is not legally serious to presume that the average consumer would always distinguish, with sufficient clarity, a tolerated partner, legitimate resale, a gray market and fraud linked to a cloned card. The vulnerability does not stem from private naivety, but from the absence of robust communicative demarcation between what was admitted, tolerated, official or prohibited.

This makes Riot's subsequent policy on chargebacks particularly serious. In a specific official policy, the company declares that a bank reversal authorizes suspension of the Riot Account until full reimbursement, and may even lead to a permanent ban in case of recurrence, and extends the sanctioning effect to gift transactions as well, reaching not only those who sent but also those who received the content. The most problematic point is that the policy reveals no prior requirement of robust individualization of the recipient's conduct, nor does it convincingly distinguish situations of knowing fraud from receipt in a context of apparent legitimacy696. The risk of financial reversal, therefore, p. 613 is converted into a mechanism of blocking and functional confiscation for the benefit of the supplier's economic recovery. The consumer comes to function as a shock absorber for the systemic failure of the environment.

From the contractual point of view, this shifting of risk is supported by a set of clauses that seek to lower the legal density of the user's enjoyment. Clause 3.1 presents the use of the services and of virtual content as a limited, non-transferable and revocable license, automatically cancelable upon termination of the account697. Clauses 4.3698 and 4.4699 expressly deny ownership, monetary value, transferability and the consumer's title over virtual content, reiterating that what exists is only a personal, non-exclusive and revocable right of access. Clause 4.5 reinforces the platform's power to delete, alter, move, remove, reprice and reorganize any and all game content or virtual content, with or without prior notice, to the extent permitted by law700. Clause 5.1 p. 614 links this picture with unilateral management of financial risk, future modification of prices and non-refundability701. Clause 14.1 adds a broad limitation of reparation702. The design p. 615 is coherent: precarious license, unilateral mutability, exclusion of value, economic elasticity and indemnity shielding. The case of the irregular gifts shows the concrete use of this arsenal as a contractual shield to absorb, in favor of the platform, the effects of its own operational vulnerability.

The central dogmatic point, however, is that this contractual vocabulary is not enough to convert the consumer into the legitimate addressee of systemic risk. The existence of fraud in the ecosystem does not authorize the supplier to shift entirely onto the good-faith user the effects of the instability of its own monetization environment. The CDC also protects the trust aroused by the way the service is organized, by the functionalities made available to the market and by the legitimate expectations created around the adequacy of the system. The chain of suppliers involved in satisfying these expectations cannot be erased by a revocable license clause703. If the platform itself institutionalizes a given functionality, knows the related risks and allows the circulation of value to unfold in an objectively confusing communicative environment, it cannot treat the consumer as the universal guarantor of the system's failures.

Likewise, the consumer-law microsystem does not admit the unrestricted contractual transfer of the professional risks of the activity to the consumer. The supplier bears risks proper to the economic and operational architecture it chose, and cannot unload them en bloc onto the regular user whenever the frustration of the model materializes. Contractual engineering does tend to transfer risks and duties to the most vulnerable pole of the relationship, but the protective logic of the CDC exists precisely to contain this tendency704. In the case of the p. 616 irregular gifts, this means that Riot could not treat the removal of skins, the suspension of functionalities and the attribution of debt to the good-faith recipient as a mere neutral consequence of its terms of use. The risk of the frustration of the payment and verification system cannot be borne exclusively by the consumer.

The case also clearly shows the passage from semiotic vulnerability to evidentiary vulnerability. Once the assets are removed or the state of the account is reverted, the user comes to depend on old statements, scattered emails, past screenshots and reconstructed memory to prove that a given item of content legitimately formed part of their position of enjoyment. There is a recent report of a rollback with the permanent disappearance of skins and other cosmetics that the consumer claims to have legitimately acquired through different ordinary channels, in a context in which reconstructing the origin of each item becomes extremely burdensome and fragmented. The episode shows how the platform, in carrying out a broad reversal of the state of the account, transfers to the player the almost archaeological burden of reconstituting item by item what was already part of their sphere of enjoyment705. In CDC terms, this reinforces the need to preserve and produce minimum records of the supply, lest the very architecture of the service make the consumer's defense impossible.

There is, moreover, an important structural element in Riot's subsequent reaction. In another title of the same ecosystem, the company began to favor payment instruments less exposed to subsequent dispute, such as prepaid cards and single-use codes, while strongly discouraging purchases on unauthorized third-party sites. The movement is eloquent: the response to the risk of chargeback was not only punitive, but also reorganized the transactional environment toward means more controllable from the supplier's point of view706. p. 617 This confirms that structural alternatives for mitigating the risk existed. The fact further weakens the legitimacy of a solution founded primarily on suppression of assets, functional blocking and expanded sanctions on the consumer.

The relevance of the case, then, goes beyond the historical episode. It shows how the Lex Plataformae can operate on three simultaneous levels. First, it produces a semiotic gray zone in the circulation of value, without sufficiently robust communicational distinctions between the lawful, the tolerated and the unlawful. Second, in the face of crisis, it unilaterally reorganizes the ecosystem and redistributes the risk onto consumers. Third, it uses the language of revocable license, absence of ownership and limitation of liability to justify the retrospective precarization of enjoyment. The result is a private regime of attribution in which the supplier not only controls the environment and its risks, but also unilaterally defines who bears the costs when the system fails.

In a dogmatic key, the Case of the Irregular Gifts allows quite precise allegations to be formulated. The first is that of inadequacy of the service due to a failure in the security and governance of the transactional environment, with subsequent abusive redistribution of the risk to the consumer. The second is that of breach of legitimate trust, because the platform itself offered the gifting functionality as an ordinary and institutionalized channel for the circulation of content. The third is that of the abusiveness of the private response, through an expanded presumption of bad faith and the absence of reliable individualization of conduct. The fourth is that of the ineffectiveness, in the concrete case, of the clauses used to turn enjoyment into an absolutely precarious tolerance and to deny restoration. The fifth is that of evidentiary asymmetry, with the need to preserve trails, transaction histories, the origin of the removed items, the criteria used for the rollback and individualized justification of the measures adopted.

As for claims, the case is also exemplary. It is not merely a matter of residual compensation. The consumer-law regime admits, here, claims for functional restoration of the position of enjoyment, restoration of assets when the user's good faith has not been rebutted by robust individualized evidence, preservation and production of minimum records of the supply, informational correction about legitimate acquisition channels and neutralization of clauses or practices that transfer entirely to the consumer the structural risks of the system. In qualified cases, the episode also serves as a bridge to two central operators. On the one hand, to open auditability, because the controversy exposes the need for verifiable transparency about the criteria for change, rollback and sanction. On the other, to abusive teleology, because the private response does not appear as a mere one-off error, but as a mode of functioning in which the platform p. 618 protects its economic interest through opacity, expanded presumption and precarization of others' enjoyment. The Case of the Irregular Gifts, in short, is not merely an episode of financial fraud. It is a clear laboratory of how semiotic vulnerability operates in IOCs. The consumer was not merely exposed to a transactional risk. They were placed in a confusing communicative environment, deprived of transparent criteria for distinguishing legitimate channels and, later, subjected to a broad private attribution policy that treated them as an extension of the platform's economic problem. It is precisely for this reason that the case matters to the argument. It shows, in a concrete situation, that the relevant asymmetry lies not only in the contract, nor only in the fraud, nor only in technology. It lies in the private governance of meaning, risk and evidence within the enjoyment regime itself.

The existence of fraud, irregularity or internal dysfunction in the supply ecosystem does not authorize the platform to shift entirely onto the good-faith consumer the systemic risk of its own operational architecture. In complex consumer relations, legal protection also considers the legitimate trust aroused by the way the system is organized, by the promises conveyed, by the functionalities offered and by the expectation of normal adequacy of the service. Marques makes this point explicit in stating that, in light of the legal guarantee of adequate and quality performance, “the norms of the CDC end up reaching the whole chain of suppliers involved, directly or indirectly, in satisfying the legitimate contractual expectations of consumers”, imposing protection of the trust aroused in the consumer group by the suppliers' conduct Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 991). Applied to the case of the irregular gifts, this logic prevents terms of use from functioning as an absolute shield protecting the platform against the effects of structural failures of its own transactional environment. ↩
  13. The consumer-law microsystem does not allow the supplier to exempt itself from the structural risks of its own activity through clauses, contractual arrangements or operational designs that convert the consumer into the final addressee of the system's instability. Marques states directly that “the need to protect the freedom of the weaker contracting party leads to imposing new professional risks on suppliers, which cannot be transferred to consumers by any valid manifestation of will” Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 274). The author adds that the market's “contractual engineering” tends to be designed so as to transfer risks and duties to the partner in a non-dominant or more vulnerable position, but that “the risk of the frustration of the contract should not be borne only by the consumer”, on pain of excessive advantage and destruction of contractual balance Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 1185). Applied to the case of the irregular gifts, this rationality prevents the validation of clauses or practices that, in the face of systemic failures of the ecosystem, impose on the regular user the total loss of the utility received or expected. ↩
