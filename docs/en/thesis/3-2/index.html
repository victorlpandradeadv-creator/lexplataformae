3.2 Economic architecture of digital platforms

See this section in the PDF

p. 139

With the reconstruction of the techno-media ambience concluded, the focus shifts to the political economy of digital platforms. The point now is to make explicit the mode of production in which these mediations operate. Drawing on Varoufakis (2024), Dowbor (2025) and Marx (2011; 2013), an arrangement is described in which platforms combine infrastructural dependence, informational rentierism, deliberate production of artificial scarcity and continuous extraction of value. These dynamics do not hover above the service: they materialize in business architecture, private governance and the functional design of digital goods and services.

Business activity organizes factors of production and subjects them to a rationality of viability, expansion and preservation of the business. In contemporary capitalism, this rationality operates under permanent tension: on the one hand, it demands responsiveness to market expectations; on the other, it intensifies profit maximization through revenue expansion, selective cost management, containment of uncertainties and active minimization of risks.

This dynamic projects, within the firm, a permanent imperative to govern contingencies, foreseeing, calibrating and redistributing whatever may affect the stability of the business. The decisive point is that this risk governance rarely remains confined to the organization. It tends to operate through mechanisms of displacement, transferring relevant portions of risk and social cost to third parties and to the State, which is often called upon only afterwards to restore the broken balance.

p. 140 The distinction between internal and external risks makes this rationality explicit. Internal risks are those that bear directly on the performance of the enterprise: production chain, logistics, quality standards, reputation and organizational capacity. As a rule, they remain manageable by the firm, since they depend on governance, compliance, investment in quality and operational control. External risks, although they may be triggered by economic activity, manifest themselves more diffusely and outside the supplier's immediate sphere of control. They reach the health, integrity, assets, privacy, lifetime and informational environment of consumers, as well as competitors, communities and institutional ecosystems. They also include costs of redress, oversight and containment shifted to the public sphere.

It is in the domain of external risks that the paradoxical dimension of the “perfect business” becomes evident: an operation capable of maximizing gains while keeping under control, at least from the business point of view, the risks that threaten its own stability. This threshold can never be reduced to zero, as the consumerist theory of quality recognizes by admitting the inevitability of failures and the need for preventive and remedial discipline of supply. The problem begins when the internal minimization of risk is obtained through the diffuse transfer of its effects to consumers, society and the State.

The managerial reading that reduces the firm to the abstract pursuit of profit obscures the concrete way in which that profit is produced. The firm does not maximize results merely by producing more or selling better; it maximizes results by designing structures that organize responsibilities, filter obligations and distribute burdens. Profit therefore depends on strategies of risk allocation. For this reason, Consumer Law, when it intervenes in the market, is not confined to punishing harm already done.

By establishing minimum standards of adequacy and safety, Consumer Law functions as a legal check on the tendency toward externalization. The supplier is liable because it occupies the structural position of the party that reaps the gains of the activity and must therefore internalize the cost of the risks it creates, preventively and, when necessary, through effective redress.

The industrialist who operates in the food market must ensure, as a condition of the viability of the business, that the product lives up at least minimally to what it promises. Adequacy is not reduced to immediate performance, such as taste, preservation or appearance; it includes safety and quality over time, so that the product does not harm the consumer's physical, psychological or financial integrity. At this point, the State not only may intervene: it must set minimum parameters and hold the supplier liable when they are breached. Regulation and liability function as an institutional response to external p. 141 risk. What, from the business point of view, could be treated as an avoidable cost appears, from the legal point of view, as socially inadmissible harm and as a burden that must be internalized by whoever structures and benefits from the economic activity.

The problem is reconfigured when emerging techniques redesign the market and introduce, on a mass scale, new goods, services and forms of risk. With each innovation that reorganizes the consumption experience, a pattern of supply is instituted that is not immediately intelligible through traditional categories: the way of offering changes, the way of enjoying changes, the mechanisms of value capture change and, above all, the ways in which risk is distributed change.

A specific challenge then arises for Consumer Law. It is not enough to recognize the new object in circulation; it is necessary to understand the techno-media grammar that makes it consumable, desirable and, at the same time, opaque. In the classical industrial paradigm, social and legal inspection of risk can bear on the materiality of the product, such as composition, performance and safety. In the techno-media order, risk tends to shift to conditions of access, regimes of governance, decision architectures and to the semiotic layer that sustains the promise of consumption.

For this reason, the political economy reconstructed here must advance to the point at which perceived value, belonging and recognition are understood as dimensions administered within the ecosystem. It is in this grammar that continuous extraction becomes practicable: the platform captures value because it governs not only transactions, but the conditions of intelligibility, desire and permanence that make the transaction recurrent.

Contemporary business rationality finds there a particularly acute zone of friction. Internal risks remain relatively controllable by management and quality; external risks, in techno-mediated markets, depend on collective recognition and institutional interpretation. The difficulty lies in identifying when a practice transfers an excessive burden to the consumer or produces harm without presenting itself as classic physical or patrimonial injury. In environments organized by technical layers and interface languages, externalization tends to operate through indirect and diffuse routes, in the form of informational asymmetry, opacity of criteria, absence of auditability and loss of control over one's own consumption experience. Hence the need for categories capable of seeing, beyond the product in the narrow sense, the regimes of promise, access and governance that configure contemporary consumption.

The discussion of risk and externalization, therefore, does not function as a generic prologue, but as an entry into the political economy of platforms. When value is produced in p. 142 mediating ecosystems, the rationality of minimizing uncertainties and shifting costs tends to be incorporated into the very architecture of the business. The platform becomes simultaneously the place of the offer and the place of administration of the conditions of enjoyment. With this, the possibility of shifting burdens onto the consumer and onto the public sphere intensifies.

From a macroeconomic perspective, Yanis Varoufakis (2024) stands out for analyzing what he considers a paradigm shift in the global economic landscape. He argues that the capitalist system is on its way to being replaced by what he calls “technofeudalism”, in which capital no longer presents itself primarily as material property and comes to be concentrated “in the cloud”, that is, in digital assets, online platforms and algorithms. The critique deserves recognition for stressing that this cloud capital, held by those he categorizes as “cloudalists” (our translation of “nubelistas”, from the Spanish edition), is characterized by its capacity to reproduce itself without generating proportional extra costs for its owners. Extraction thus expands over those the author describes as “cloud serfs”, subjects who figure simultaneously as consumers and as continuous suppliers of two raw materials essential to the system: data and attention.

From a Brazilian standpoint, Ladislau Dowbor (2025) stands out for his critique of what he calls “rentier capitalism”, a transformative system that, in his account, has redesigned global dynamics and marked a significant break with earlier modes of production. According to the author, this evolution can be described in three broad historical phases. In agrarian societies, domination fell to landowners, who controlled the available resources through force and religious authority. The industrial revolution introduced machines and factories, shifting power to industrial capitalists. In this period, the exploitation of labor tied directly to the production of goods becomes visible, creating a tangible connection between labor, output and public services. The modern digital era, in turn, rests on an economy based on knowledge and information, in which platforms such as Google and Amazon dominate through informational instrumentalization, which has become the most valuable resource.

Dowbor identifies rentier capitalism as a system in which power resides in the possession and control of assets, rather than in the production of goods. Read in this key, platforms such as Facebook, according to the author, exemplify the phenomenon through “demand monopolies”, forcing users to depend on certain services because of their broad social adoption. The system, in his account, depends on financialization, a process that decouples money from real economic activity and enables the continuous extraction of wealth. The author draws on examples such as developing nations trapped in debt cycles by exorbitant interest and individuals p. 143 burdened by expensive credit. Absentee owners, such as hedge funds and investment firms, are said to concentrate the financial returns and neglect the social impacts. In this context, Dowbor criticizes the legislative framework of intellectual property, arguing that it has become a barrier to innovation and ceased to operate as an instrument of progress.

Against this background, it becomes possible to shift the focus from the “medium” to the “mode of production” crystallized in platforms. It is not enough to describe technologies and symbolic mediations. What matters is to understand which form of economic organization is condensed in the architecture of these infrastructures and what place it reserves for the consumer and for digital goods. The aim here is to offer vocabulary for treating digitally based businesses not as simple contractual models (“subscription”, “freemium”, “advertising”, “microtransactions” etc.), but as manifestations of a common structural arrangement, marked by platform technofeudalism, digital rentierism and a specific form of exploitation of the consumer who, contextually, is also characterized as an incorporated external labor force (the “consumer-worker”).

Looking at these business categories from this angle, one does not obtain a neutral classification of forms of monetization. One obtains a map of how the functional architecture of digital goods is designed to enable the continuous extraction of value. Each apparently technical option116 is part of an underlying economic project. The unity of this project becomes more visible when three levels are brought back together. At the macro level, Varoufakis describes platforms operating as cloud technofiefs. At the meso level, Dowbor emphasizes digital rentierism and the toll economy. At the micro level, Marx provides the key to perceiving how digital consumption can function as the invisible labor of a contemporary kind of “informational lumpenproletariat”.

The triad is decisive because it allows us to understand why the Immaterial Object of Consumption must be treated as an infrastructure concept, and not merely as a label for digital goods. It makes explicit that the ontological failure of the CDC is not merely conceptual carelessness, but blindness before a mode of production that reorganizes the very form of exploitation.

Articulated, the three dimensions117 make it possible to reclassify digitally based businesses as architectures of continuous extraction of value, operating on functionally p. 144 defined immaterial goods. This is a reclassification that provides theoretical grounding for questioning the potential unlawfulness of the intentions and purposes embedded in the design of platforms. If the platform mode of production depends structurally on producing artificial scarcity over informational goods and on establishing economic and symbolic captivity over the consumer-worker, that unlawfulness is revealed in the operative purpose with which the business architecture is designed.

The large platforms that operate attention ecosystems (here called cloudalists and subcloudalists) are not limited to offering products or services. They build, around their users, environments of continuous audiovisual distraction. Their business models start from meticulously studied consumption profiles and position the individual within a gravitational field of signs. Games, series, songs, competitive broadcasts, advertising campaigns and licensed products make up a constellation that accompanies the subject throughout everyday life. The result is a global positioning of the brand and its digital goods, such that it becomes difficult to experience digital life outside the contours of this ecosystem.

Symbols endowed with value in the collective imagination can be captured, recombined and protected under intellectual property regimes that favor the economic exploiter, even though the composition of this symbolic capital depends on diffuse contributions. The asymmetry becomes more evident when one observes that the legal order itself recognizes, in certain segments, remedies of subsequent economic participation. The resale right, granted to sculptors and visual artists, exemplifies a mechanism by which creators retain a limited share in the subsequent economic fruits of their work. The reference is not meant to transplant the institution automatically, but to indicate that the full concentration of gains derived from collectively fed symbolic chains is not a legal inevitability. In attention ecosystems, the social function of intellectual property requires at least that the question be raised again about the limits of the full appropriation of value by a single entity when the economic chain depends structurally on authors, artists, communities and consumers.

The maintenance of models in which a single entity concentrates, by business decision and legal design, the totality of gains derived from the economic exploitation of narrative universes built by collective efforts strains the principles of objective good faith and contractual balance. The social function of intellectual property, in a scenario of cloudalists and attention monopoly, requires reconsidering to what extent it is admissible for the exclusive holder to accumulate in full the profits of value chains that depend, structurally, on the diffuse contribution of authors, artists, communities and consumers.

p. 145 The decisive point is that the illustrative scenario does not describe a sectoral anomaly of entertainment, but a general form of economic organization through infrastructure. The capacity to maintain “nominal ownership” and, at the same time, downgrade the enjoyment bundle through unilateral updating, as well as the power to impose sanctions that degrade reputation, visibility and access, reveals that the platform not only offers a good, but administers a territory of circulation of signs, time and payment. At this point, the distinction between nominal title and the effective enjoyment bundle is decisive. On platforms, the formal permanence of the item in the account does not guarantee the preservation of its functional, symbolic or economic value. The object may continue to “exist” for accounting and contractual purposes, while the architecture that gave it utility, distinction or recognition is reconfigured by the supplier.

If in the first part of the discussion the focus fell on technique, media and design as forms of everyday governance, the next step is to make explicit the economic rationality that makes this governance profitable and structural. The anthropology of consumption will enter, along this path, as analytical support for understanding how the visible and the desirable also become economically governed dimensions. Before that, however, it is necessary to show how the architecture of the ecosystem converts dependence and permanence into continuous extraction of value.
