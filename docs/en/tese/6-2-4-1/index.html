6.2.4.1 On abstaining from the term “digital vulnerability”

See this section in the PDF

This section in the layers of the site

  • Dogmatics · Sections of chapter 6

p. 584

The expression “digital vulnerability” will not be adopted here because, although it is descriptively useful, it yields little dogmatically. It names the environment in which the injury occurs, but does not isolate the legally relevant mechanism of the asymmetry. In this vocabulary, “digital” works as a marker of setting, and not as an operator of attribution. That may be enough to signal that technology-mediated consumption has something specific about it, but it is not enough to guide, with precision, the passage from fact to normative consequence. The problem is not merely semantic. It is methodological. To be legally useful, a concept must cut reality so as to make the conflict tractable, stabilizing what p. 585 matters for duty, evidence, unlawfulness and remedy. When the category describes only the environment and not the mechanism, it yields little for decidability Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 81; p. 83])656.

This demand for delimitation is not terminological fussiness. Legal dogmatics works by functional simplification, not by a total description of the social world. Its task is to produce operational concepts that bring the conflict back to the measure needed to make it tractable. Categories too closely tied to the setting tend to fail precisely where the law most needs them, because they keep their descriptive breadth and lose the capacity to qualify the fact in terms of attribution and consequence. That is exactly why the expression “digital vulnerability” proves insufficient. It points to a diffuse field of risks and asymmetries, but does not make sufficiently clear which form of vulnerabilization is at stake or how that form should be translated into duties and protective responses Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 108; p. 247])657.

The difficulty becomes clearer when one looks at recent doctrinal use of the label. Depending on the context, “digital vulnerability” may designate technical and operational fragility, an information deficit, behavioral manipulation, dependence on infrastructure, algorithmic p. 586 opacity, technological exclusion or platform-mediated economic asymmetry. The result is an aggregating but poorly disciplined concept. It manages to bring real contemporary phenomena into a single frame, linked to the consumer journey, data protection, digital advertising, profiling and abusive practices in an information-intensive market Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 167; p. 17)658. The problem is that, by condensing many different layers of risk, the expression no longer indicates which of them matters legally in each case. Instead of cutting the problem, it piles it up. And when the category piles up too much, the evidence scatters and the remedy swings between excessive abstraction and casuistry.

This deficit is especially acute at the level of legal technique. A concept does not serve only to describe a socially worrying situation. It must offer a normative form stable enough to say what must be proven, which duty was strained or breached and which consequence fits the case. Empirical data do not, by themselves, automatically enter the universe of law. Their relevance depends on their insertion into a normative structure that makes them intelligible and operable. There is no raw legal fact. There is fact legally filtered by a normative form capable of turning it into a criterion of practical discipline Reale(Reale, 2001, p. 168; p. 96)659. That is why “digital vulnerability” proves weak. The expression names a state of affairs, but does not on its own provide the bridge between that state of affairs and the normative judgment that should follow from it.

p. 587 The same critique can be put another way. The legislator and the jurist do not act as sociologists who merely portray phenomena. They start from what is in order to determine what ought to be, attaching legally relevant consequences to the conduct provided for. A category that stays on the descriptive plane, tied to the technical climate of the problem, does not perform this function well. Broad labels tend to produce the language of a period without disciplining the reconstruction of the relevant factual basis. It is therefore not enough to recognize that the injury occurs in a digital medium. It must be converted into a normatively filtered category, capable of guiding judgments of lawfulness, duties of conduct and attributable consequences Reale(Reale, 2001, p. 32; p. 189)660.

The same insufficiency also shows clearly in other competing vocabularies. The recent proliferation of expressions such as “electronic”, “virtual”, “digital” or “technological vulnerability” already reveals the perception that there is a new problem, but also the difficulty of naming it with sufficient precision. Such formulas are useful for indicating the setting in which the difficulty emerges, especially when technology operates as an obstacle to access to justice, to understanding or to the exercise of legal positions661. But they remain very close to the technical frame of the problem and are less able to identify the legally p. 588 controllable mechanism of the asymmetry Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 3-4)662. They help to say where the problem happens. They say less about how it arises in legally relevant terms.

This discussion therefore adopts the expression “semiotic vulnerability”. The category targets a mechanism, not a setting. The central point lies not in the fact that the relationship takes place in a digital medium, but in the way signs, discourses, interfaces and framings organize expectation, understanding, adherence, perception of value, perception of risk and perception of frustration. Language does not operate as ornament. It functions as an institutional technique of power. The legally relevant problem becomes the unequal administration of meaning, that is, the way the platform organizes the conditions under which the consumption experience itself becomes intelligible. It is in this key that signification can be analyzed as an instrument of social control, normalization and the production of consensus, and not merely as a neutral vehicle of information Warat(Warat, 1984, p. 18)663.

This formulation has the advantage of shifting attention from the descriptive surface to the structure that produces legal effects. The focus no longer falls on the technological environment as such, but on the socially operative production of meanings that conditions how the experience will be lived, accepted and reproduced. Languages and discourses do not merely transmit informative content. They produce normative resonances, legitimize arrangements and p. 589 make certain modes of functioning appear unquestionable. Hence the pertinence of abandoning environmental labels in favor of categories that isolate the actual mechanism by which meaning is produced Warat(Warat, 1984, p. 15; p. 47)664.

This methodological shift can be reinforced by a limited parallel. Critical categories lose density when they are reduced to the environment, the medium or the setting in which they appear. The problem lies not in the technical medium as such, but in the social relation it mediates. When a category comes to name only the context and not the mechanism, it keeps its intuitive force and loses its operative force. The same risk arises with “digital vulnerability”. The reference to the digital tends to indicate the technological environment of the injury, but not necessarily the specific way in which the legally central asymmetry is produced. The shift to “semiotic vulnerability” seeks to avoid precisely this reduction Debord(Debord, 2013, p. 27; p. 31; p. 120)665.

In the IOC regime, this terminological choice is not only more precise; it is more useful. The platform does not merely provide information. It manages the showcase, the store, events, metrics, signals of rarity, promises of equivalence, narratives of value and the very framings through which consumers perceive what they acquired. The relevant asymmetry is not merely a lack of data. It is the unequal administration of the meaning of the object consumed. In such environments, expectation, consent, perception of value and perception of frustration are modulated by p. 590 language and by design. Semiotic vulnerability makes it possible to name exactly this plane of incidence, without lapsing into subjectivism or environmental generalities.

This choice also brings an operational gain. It reorganizes the duty to inform in a qualitative rather than quantitative key. Ensuring formal access to data is not enough. Information must be correct, clear, precise and conspicuous, without excess, dispersion or barriers that undermine its practical usefulness for the consumer's decision. When communication looks transparent while producing material opacity, the problem lies not only in omission, but in how the meaning of the relationship is organized. The requirement is no longer “more information” but information capable of guiding decision, understanding and proper use of the product or service. This is what allows the move from the environmental label to the normative reconstruction of the mechanism.

The aim is an applicable and minimally reliable discipline. Replacing “digital vulnerability” with “semiotic vulnerability” makes the passage from fact to legal consequence more controllable. The duty to inform can be reconstructed as a requirement of adequacy and usefulness for decision, and not as a mere volume of data. Advertising becomes controllable by criteria of ambiguity, relevant omission, identification and truthfulness. Transparency stops being a showcase word and comes closer to a requirement of institutional verifiability. And the architecture of supply can be qualified, where appropriate, not merely as the context of the abuse, but as the very structured form in which it is produced. The gain is not aesthetic. It is dogmatic. The category delimits the mechanism better, disciplines the evidence better and prepares the remedy better.

Notes

  1. Replacing the expression "digital vulnerability" follows from a requirement of dogmatic operability. In legal discourse, it is not enough for a category to name, in broad terms, a technological environment marked by asymmetries and opacities; it must allow the legally relevant problem to be delimited, criteria of attribution to be stabilized and the passage to duty, unlawfulness, evidence and normative consequence to be guided. In this key, dogmatics is not organized to describe reality exhaustively, but to select, among its many aspects, what matters for the decision. As Ferraz Jr. observes, "unlike zetetic theories, dogmatic ones, concerned with the decidability of conflicts, do not take care to be logically rigorous in the use of their concepts and definitions, for to them what matters is not the relation with the phenomena of reality (describing the phenomena), but rather making a cut in reality, isolating the problems that are relevant for decision-making and diverting attention from the others" Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 81]). An overly broad expression may therefore have descriptive yield and still remain legally weak if it delimits neither the specific mechanism of vulnerabilization nor the form of its normative incidence. This is where the critique of the formula "digital vulnerability" holds, since its breadth tends to name a setting without stabilizing, with sufficient sharpness, the legally central asymmetry. The problem, then, is not one of empirical truth but of dogmatic yield, since, as the author notes, "its problem is not properly a question of truth, but of decidability" Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 83]). ↩
  2. The refusal of excessively broad categories is also justified because legal dogmatics makes a conflict tractable only when it brings it back to a technically controllable plane. What enters legal reasoning is not the whole environment, but only the slice needed for its normative qualification and its decision. Ferraz Jr. stresses this closure when he states that dogmatics works by functional simplification of reality, producing "operational concepts" that make the technical handling of the problem feasible Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 108]). Expressions too closely tied to the setting, though intuitively strong, may fail precisely where the law most needs them, especially in organizing the fact in terms of attribution, evidence and consequence. This is where the insufficiency of "digital vulnerability" shows. The expression points to a diffuse field of risks and asymmetries, but does not indicate precisely enough what the legally relevant mechanism is or how it should be translated into duties and protective responses. Along the same lines, Ferraz Jr. observes that concrete conflicts, even when laden with many social, economic and political dimensions, become legally tractable only when they are brought back to the measure needed for their decidability, and not addressed "in all their concrete extension, but to the extent necessary for their decidability with a minimum of social disturbance" Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 247]). It is this criterion that underpins, here, the preference for a more sharply delimited and controllable category. ↩
  3. The notion of vulnerability in the digital environment is genuinely useful descriptively because it brings together, in a single frame, contemporary risks linked to the consumer journey, data protection, digital advertising and informational opacity. In this sense, the digital market is marked by growing difficulty in ensuring transparency in marketing practices based on monitoring, profiling and message targeting, as well as by opacity in testing processes, the use of dark patterns, the insufficiency of consent as a legitimating basis and recurring security incidents involving personal data Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 167). The category thus clearly captures a contemporary ecosystem of risks and asymmetries. This aggregating character grows denser when hypervulnerability is tied to the consumer's simultaneous position as the target of market practices, as the raw material of the marketing system and as a product traded among its members, to which are added informational asymmetry and the spread of abusive practices founded on predicting and manipulating the consumer journey Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 17). ↩
  4. The refusal of the expression “digital vulnerability”, at this point, follows from an elementary methodological requirement: a legally useful concept cannot merely describe an environment, but must offer a normative form capable of converting social data into an operable criterion of attribution, evidence, duty and consequence. For Miguel Reale, empirical data do not, by themselves, automatically enter the legal universe; their relevance depends on their insertion into a normative structure that makes them intelligible and operable. The mere reference to the “digital” may therefore name a technical or historical setting, but it remains insufficient when it does not stabilize the legally relevant mechanism that explains the injury or guides the system's response. As the author states, “one cannot speak of Law, in the full sense of this word, without encompassing its essential moment of normativity, for social facts are only legal when inserted in a normative context” Reale(Reale, 2001, p. 168). Along the same lines, the legal norm corresponds to the “moment of integration of a class of facts according to an order of values”, and cannot be understood without simultaneous reference to these two poles, which it “dialectically integrates in itself and surpasses” Reale(Reale, 2001, p. 96). The broad label “digital vulnerability” proves dogmatically loose, since it points to a context but does not, on its own, turn social experience into a sufficiently precise legal criterion. ↩
  5. The critique of categories that remain on a merely descriptive plane finds direct support in Miguel Reale. For him, legal construction is not satisfied with simply observing a state of affairs, because Law does not operate as a neutral description of social reality, but as a normative elaboration capable of turning empirical data into a criterion of practical discipline. The legislator therefore does not act “in the manner of the sociologist”, merely portraying facts as they are; on the contrary, he starts from what is in order to determine what ought to be, attaching legally relevant consequences to the conduct provided for, in case the norm is observed or violated Reale(Reale, 2001, p. 32). It is precisely this disciplined passage from fact to norm that keeps the legal concept from dissolving into an impressionistic label or the mere language of a period. In this key, broad categories that only name contexts or environments fail when they cannot stabilize a legally relevant fact-type. Reale states explicitly that “one cannot speak of a legal fact except to the extent and insofar as it is inserted in a normative structure”, which is why “there is no raw fact in Law” Reale(Reale, 2001, p. 189). ↩
  6. When technology comes to be treated as an obstacle to access, to understanding or to the exercise of legal positions, the methodological risk lies in turning this finding into an overly broad category. The vocabulary of “electronic vulnerability” illustrates the point: it makes it possible to name real situations of exclusion or difficulty produced by the technological environment, but tends to work more as a description of context than as a dogmatic instrument of attribution. The focus remains on the fact that technological change can render people vulnerable and exclude them, as happens with those shut out of access to justice in settings of electronic proceedings, Online Courts, Justice 4.0, Justice 5.0 and ODR platforms Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 7). This formulation has real descriptive merit, but its explanatory force rests mainly on the idea of a technological obstacle, and not on a finer identification of the legal mechanism that turns this barrier into a controllable asymmetry within a regime such as the CDC's. The category points to the context of exclusion, but does not stabilize, with the desirable sharpness, the specific way in which the injury is produced. This becomes even clearer when the problem is placed within the broad horizon of Society 5.0, the 4th Industrial Revolution and their systemic effects on access, which call for special attention to the so-called technologically vulnerabilized Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 5). The summary of the so-called sixth renewal wave of access to justice confirms this design: it is presented as a field devoted to analyzing and mitigating technology as an obstacle to a just legal order Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 7). The vocabulary helps to name the barrier, but still falls short of explaining, with sufficient precision, the legal mechanism of the asymmetry. ↩
  7. The recent proliferation of labels such as “electronic”, “digital”, “virtual” or “technological vulnerability” already reveals, by itself, an important methodological difficulty: the field perceives that there is a new problem, but still wavers over how to name it. These expressions are descriptively useful for indicating that certain asymmetries emerge in a technically mediated environment, but they tend to stay very close to the setting in which the injury occurs, and are less able to identify, with dogmatic sharpness, the legally relevant mechanism of vulnerabilization. It is in this sense that one speaks of “cyber or electronic vulnerability”, also called virtual, technological or digital vulnerability, when technology comes to function as an obstacle to access to justice Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 3). This feature is reinforced when the terminological dispute itself turns on whether “digital” or “electronic” covers more ground, that is, on which name best covers the technical environment, and not on which category best individualizes the legally controllable factual basis. The remark that “electronic” could be an even broader term, since it also reaches digital phenomena, makes it all the more visible how far the axis of the formulation remains centered on the technological frame of the problem Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 3-4). The category comes to name the technical climate of the injury, but not necessarily its mechanism. The same happens when technological vulnerability is described as an individual's technical, informational, material or organizational limitation in dealing with information and communication technologies Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 4). It helps to say where the problem happens and in what kind of setting it intensifies, but still offers less precision for reconstructing, in a consumer-law key, duty, evidence, attribution and remedy. ↩
  8. The refusal of the expression “digital vulnerability”, at this point, can be taken further with Luis Alberto Warat, because his semiology of power shifts the focus from the mere technical environment to the mechanism that actually matters in producing subjection. The problem lies not in the “digital” taken as a generic setting, but in the way signs, discourses and communicative framings organize understanding, expectation and adherence. Warat states explicitly that signification must be analyzed “as an instrument of social control”, “as a normalizing and disciplinary strategy of individuals”, “as a formula producing consensus” and “as persuasive power provoking effects of verisimilitude over the material conditions of social life” Warat(Warat, 1984, p. 18). The decisive point, therefore, is not technology as such, but the semiotic work that turns certain conditions of power into an appearance of normality, validity and inevitability. In this key, a more precise legal category must target the mechanism of unequal intelligibility and semiotic capture, and not merely the fact that the relationship takes place in a digital environment. ↩
  9. Warat also offers a useful basis for justifying a legal category centered on mechanism rather than setting, because his reflection on language shifts attention from the descriptive surface to the socially operative production of effects of meaning. For him, languages are not limited to the informative content they seem to transmit; they engender “significant and normalizing resonances of social practices”, taking part in a deeper dynamic of “social production of meanings” Warat(Warat, 1984, p. 15). This makes it clear that the legally relevant problem lies not only in identifying the technical context in which the relationship occurs, but in grasping how signification organizes in advance the intelligibility of experience and conditions how it will be lived, accepted and reproduced. Along the same lines, Warat observes that pragmatic analysis makes it possible to articulate functions such as “persuasion, legitimation, anticipation”, showing how certain discourses produce an “effect of unquestionability and reality over knowledge” Warat(Warat, 1984, p. 47). Hence the pertinence of abandoning broad, environmental labels in favor of categories that isolate the actual mechanism by which meaning is produced. ↩
  10. Critical categories lose density when they are reduced to the environment, the medium or the setting in which they appear. The warning comes through clearly in the formulation according to which the spectacle is not to be confused with a set of images or with its technical means of diffusion, but consists in a social relation mediated by images Debord(Debord, 2013, p. 27). Reducing it to the mass media therefore grasps only its most visible surface manifestation, and not the structural mechanism the category seeks to describe Debord(Debord, 2013, p. 31). The same methodological reasoning makes it possible to reject formulations that, though intuitively expressive, end up naming only the setting in which the injury occurs. The risk is keeping descriptive force while losing dogmatic operability, because the category comes to point to a context without stabilizing, with sufficient sharpness, the relevant factual basis, the duty strained, the required evidentiary standard and the corresponding legal consequence. The critique of reducing the spectacle to “the media”, precisely because this turns it back into a mere instrument and erases the social logic the category sought to denounce, reinforces this methodological caution Debord(Debord, 2013, p. 120). Likewise, the expression “digital vulnerability” tends to grow poorer when it describes the technological environment without isolating the specific way in which the legally relevant asymmetry is produced. The shift to “semiotic vulnerability” therefore does not stem from a preference of vocabulary, but from the search for a category better able to identify the actual mechanism of vulnerabilization, located in the unequal administration of meaning, expectation, perception of risk, the intelligibility of the offer and the conditions for contesting it. ↩
