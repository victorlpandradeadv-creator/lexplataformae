6.1.4 On sectoral regulation and the Legal Framework for Electronic Games

See this section in the PDF

p. 543

The sectoral regulation of electronic games matters, above all, because it dismantles two poor simplifications: the notion that the sector is legally invisible and the notion that naming it in legislation would suffice to resolve the central conflicts of its governance. The legal framework for electronic games expressly recognizes the existence of an industry of its own582, regulates its central economic chain — manufacture, import, commercialization, development and commercial use583 — and provides a normative definition of what counts as an electronic game, including interactive audiovisual works developed as computer programs, devices and accessories, and software consumed by download or streaming584. The sector, therefore, can no p. 544 longer be treated as a legally minor pastime or as a regulatory gray zone. The legislator named it, framed it and explicitly placed it within the horizon of legitimate economic activity, innovation and culture585.

This recognition, however, is framed in terms of regulated economic freedom. The law affirms the freedom of manufacture, import, commercialization, development and commercial use of electronic games, but does so with express reference to Article 173 of the Constitution and to the legislation in force586. This is relevant because the framework does not enshrine a sectoral free-for-all disguised as friendly modernity. On the contrary, it already reveals that the sector is conceived within a frame of legally conditioned economic freedom, including a specific public concern with age rating and with the risks related to the use of microtransaction mechanisms587. In other words, regulation does not operate as an external brake on the sector, but as a recognition that its economic activity mobilizes, at the same time, money, structural asymmetries and sensitive publics whose protection is part of the constitutional order itself.

The same logic appears in the substantive guidelines of the law itself. The framework organizes the sector institutionally through principles and guidelines588 and, in the core most relevant to this thesis, affirms that electronic games must be conceived in light of respect for fundamental rights and democratic values, consumer protection, the integral protection of children and adolescents, and the preservation of privacy, the protection of personal data and p. 545 informational self-determination589. The perspective is decisive because it shows that the legal order already treats the sector as an economic and cultural activity crossed by multisectoral public concerns, and not as a playful enclave left solely to private initiative. The dogmatic relevance of this lies in preventing any attempt at legal infantilization of the problem, as if electronic games still belonged to the regulatory limbo of “it's just entertainment”.

The Digital Statute of Children and Adolescents reinforces this diagnosis by making explicit, on another normative front, that the digital environment is not neutral and that suppliers are subject to positive duties of prevention, protection, information and safety590. The material content of the best interest of the child and adolescent, in this context, already encompasses privacy, safety, mental and physical health, access to information, freedom of participation in society and well-being591. The norm goes further than programmatic discourse by prohibiting loot boxes in games aimed at children and adolescents and banning profiling techniques for targeting commercial advertising592. With this, the legislator recognizes, head-on, that practices typical of the economic organization of electronic games may generate specific risks and warrant their own regulation. Sectoral normativity, therefore, already sees the games market as a site of sensitive monetization, behavioral formation and informational vulnerability.

The entry into force of the new protective regime made this perception even more concrete by directly reaching one of the most characteristic mechanisms of the contemporary monetization of electronic games. By prohibiting loot boxes in games aimed at children and adolescents, as well as in those likely to be accessed by this audience, the Digital Statute of Children and Adolescents not only abstractly recognized the economic and behavioral sensitivity of this market, but began to interfere in the very way products were organized that had previously been presented as fully compatible with the presence of minors in the digital ecosystem Brasil(Brasil, [2025]c). Bringing its entry into force forward to March 2026 reinforces p. 546 this point because it shows that the legislator did not treat the matter as a merely symbolic or deferrable agenda item, but as a concrete regulatory problem, capable of requiring immediate adaptation by suppliers that structure their activity on mechanisms of microtransaction and recurrent capture of attention and spending Brasil(Brasil, [2026]b).

The institutional reaction of Riot Games to the new scenario shows, with particular sharpness, both the strength and the limit of sectoral regulation. Instead of immediately reorganizing the economic architecture of League of Legends to keep its offer compatible with the continued presence of underage users, the company chose to shift the response to the plane of access, announcing that minors could not play titles rated 18+ (for those over eighteen) in Brazil, not even with the consent of their guardians, and tying any restoration of enjoyment to the user's coming of age or to a future re-rating of the game593 Riot Games(Riot Games, [2026]n). At the same time, the company itself clarified that any affected accounts would remain preserved, with items, cosmetics and progress maintained, although frozen until access was later released Riot Games(Riot Games, [2026]m). The fact is dogmatically telling because it reveals, in an almost confessional way, that the value of the consumer relationship is not exhausted by the isolated act of acquisition, nor can it be reduced to abstract ownership of digital records. What is in dispute is the continued enjoyment of a position built over time, whose utility depends on the user's remaining in the governed environment.

The episode offers an especially valuable demonstration, since sectoral regulation proves capable of recognizing specific risks and of pressuring the market, but the private response is still organized according to the logic proper to the Lex Plataformae, that is, through the unilateral reengineering of the conditions of access and permanence, and not through a spontaneous revision of the economic rationality that produced the problem. The result is revealing. Faced with the application of a public norm aimed at protecting children and adolescents, the platform preserves the monetization model and transfers to the vulnerable user the practical cost of adaptation, converting the former condition of tolerated and economically p. 547 exploitable participant into a situation of temporary exclusion from the ecosystem. The methodological importance of this picture lies in showing, with rare clarity, that the sectoral framing of the games market is indispensable, but not sufficient. It sees the risk, names the sector and produces relevant commands; it does not, by itself, solve the dogmatic problem of continuous enjoyment subjected to opaque, mutable and unilaterally administered private governance.

Nor should it be perceived as a kind of loose regulation, devoid of sanction. The statute imposes concrete duties to remove content that violates rights and requires a legal representative in the country with powers to receive service of process594, while the regulating decree designates an administrative authority, provides for blocking orders and even activates the technical infrastructural layer of the digital environment through coordination with Anatel and CGI.br595. This is important because it dismantles the presentation of the platform as a self-sufficient sovereign space. The digital environment is already an object of public institutionality, with authorities, orders, enforcement mechanisms and mandatory points of contact. Pure self-regulation, in this scenario, poorly describes the state of the law in force.

At the same time, the normative recognition of the sector also has repercussions on the structural regime of industrial property. The amendment made by the legal framework in inserting the grant of registration for electronic games into the Industrial Property Law596 shows that games came to be regarded, more robustly, as an object of legal protection within the innovation economy. This matters for the thesis because it confirms that the sector was assimilated by the legal order not only as a cultural and market activity, but also as a reality capable of radiating consequences into the regimes of exclusivity. The consequence, however, is not to turn intellectual property into the total language of the problem. On the contrary, it makes it all the more necessary to contain the proprietarist temptation to capture entirely conflicts that, on the plane of enjoyment, remain conflicts of governed consumption.

This whole sectoral repertoire is relevant for describing the sector, recognizing its institutional categories, identifying typical forms of monetization and making explicit specific public p. 548 concerns. But it does not, by itself, deliver the dogmatic solution to the central problem of the IOC. What is at stake is not only the institutional recognition of the industry, nor the affirmation that electronic games are innovation, culture or regulated economic activity. The decisive point lies in the structure of private power that shapes the continuous enjoyment of the consumed immaterial object. And this structure is not resolved by the mere fact that the sector has its own legal framework.

The central conflict remains elsewhere: in continued performance, in architectural opacity, in unilateral control of access, in relevant changes of enjoyment, in the modulation of visibility, in the administration of inventories, histories and reputations, in evidentiary asymmetry and in the private distribution of the conditions of permanence in the ecosystem. None of this disappears because the sector received a name and principles in law. Sectoral framing helps us see the market; it is not enough to control governance. In IOCs, immaterial product and governed environment are dogmatically inseparable, and for this reason the application of norms cannot be exhausted by the economic-cultural framing of the sector. It must reach the administered enjoyment and the Lex Plataformae that organizes it.

This insufficiency does not disqualify sectoral regulation. It only repositions its reach. The games framework, the protective digital statute and the complementary normativity function as an important repertoire of recognition, public signaling of risks and institutional delimitation of the sector. They show what the State has already perceived: that electronic games mobilize innovation, culture, data, consumption, childhood, advertising, microtransactions and economic power. But the dogmatic problem of the IOC requires an additional step. It requires articulating this repertoire with the constitutional economic order, with the CDC and, in tension, with intellectual property, to face what sectoral regulation does not solve alone: continuous enjoyment as a consumer relationship subjected to opaque and mutable private governance.

The existence of sectoral regulation does not replace the consumer-law infrastructure, nor does it authorize conflicts of enjoyment to be fully absorbed by the grammar of the sector or by the language of innovation. The sector may be recognized, promoted and legally named, and still the decisive question may remain open: what limits apply when the platform unilaterally reconfigures access, use, visibility, continuity and functional equivalence of the consumed immaterial object?

It is this question that the sectoral repertoire, on its own, does not answer. And it is precisely for this reason that the protection of the IOC must be built not on a fetish for the “games framework”, but on the articulation between sectoral regulation, consumer protection, the constitutional economic order and limits on the private exercise of platform powers.
