6.2.4.4 From excessive information to practices of performative transparency: duty to inform and informational adequacy

See this section in the PDF

p. 618

The duty to inform, under the CDC regime, is not satisfied by the mere formal existence of content available to the consumer. In the case of immaterial objects of consumption, information fulfills its legal function only when it organizes, in an intelligible and timely manner, the practical core of the decision. This means that informing is not dumping clauses, FAQs, hyperlinks, peripheral notices and cascading policies only to invoke formal compliance afterwards. Informing is making cognizable, at the point of decision, what actually defines the utility of the performance, its risks, its relevant limits, its conditions of enjoyment and the cases in which it may be lost. The consumer-law system requires, in material terms, that the supplier ensure the consumer clear, precise and conspicuous information about the main characteristics of the product, its economic repercussions and the risks that surround the contracting, so that good faith and trust are not sabotaged by a documentary architecture that delivers words and withholds meaning707.

p. 619 This requirement is especially important in IOCs because the essential content of the relationship is not in peripheral details, but in the very core of the value acquired. Real cost, total cost, mutability of the object, restrictions on use, equivalence parameters, maintenance criteria, cases of loss and consequences of acceptance are not exotic annexes to the contracting. They are the substance of the promised enjoyment. When these elements appear displaced to zones of low visibility, scattered across secondary documents or subordinated to successive cross-references, the duty to inform has not been fulfilled in the material sense, even if the supplier manages to point to dozens of screens, links or supposedly accessible texts. The legal problem ceases to be the simple lack of information and becomes the communicational inadequacy of the supply.

Thus, the key to reading is not quantity, but adequacy. The supplier does not fulfill the legal duty by accumulating documentation and leaving it technically available. Fulfillment is measured by the capacity of the information to reach the consumer in conditions that can actually guide a decision. The posture required is not passive. The supplier must make the information reach the recipient in a useful and structured way, because the consumer's right to information is a more complex problem than the mere lack of data to be provided708. This point is decisive. The standard defense that “it was all in the terms” only works if one accepts, uncritically, that the mere existence of a body of documents suffices to form valid consent. The CDC does not work with this fiction, so comfortable for the supplier.

From this follows the need to recognize that informational excess can also be a form of violation. Volume is not neutral. In certain communicational designs, it operates as a positive technique of opacity. The information exists, but its organization makes practical understanding of what matters unfeasible. Successive layers of documents, serialized links, cross-references, excessively technical language, inverted visual emphasis, cascading disclaimers and the displacement of relevant content to improbable zones of the contracting flow can produce the same legal effect as omission. The consumer receives text, p. 620 but does not receive guidance. The platform stages informational openness while maintaining, under an appearance of compliance, a regime of noise. This is exactly the phenomenon called “performative transparency”.

Performative transparency does not consist in the absence of information, but in the excess, dispersion and defective hierarchization of the essential content. The supplier appears to fulfill the legal duty through abundance, but the communicational design reduces the practical utility of what it presents. This is not a mere aesthetic deficiency of drafting or a discomfort of reading. It is a structure that prevents comparison, understanding of the risk and apprehension of the economic and functional core of the contracting. In a consumer-law key, this failure is legally relevant because it compromises the practical freedom of choice. If the communication reduces price transparency, hinders the comparability of offers and materially interferes with the consumer's capacity to understand the terms, costs and conditions of the product or service, documentary abundance no longer functions as proof of transparency, but as its caricature709.

This point gains special density in IOCs because enjoyment is usually preceded by purchase and acceptance flows designed for speed, impulse, managed scarcity or a promise of distinction. In such environments, displacing the essential content outside the point of decision is more than a failure of organization. It is a shielding technique. The platform offers the object under a certain frame of value and urgency, but places costs, mutability, potential losses and limitations in documentary zones that the flow itself does not encourage one to go through. The consumer is pushed to decide on the basis of the shop window and only later discovers, if at all, the depth of the contractual reservation against their own enjoyment. The legally relevant question, then, is not whether there was text somewhere in the ecosystem. The question is whether the essential was presented in a conspicuous, legible and timely way before adhesion and before exposure to the risk.

p. 621 This is precisely what Article 54, under paragraphs 3 and 4, of the CDC makes explicit. Adhesion contracts must be drafted in clear terms, with conspicuous and legible characters, so as to facilitate their understanding, and clauses limiting rights must be drafted with emphasis, allowing immediate and easy understanding710. The provision is not a typographical whim. It prevents the essential from being hidden in cross-references, low visibility or documentary depth. In IOCs, this command carries even greater weight, because a large part of the relevant limitations falls precisely on the core of the acquired experience. If the platform intends to reserve for itself the power to alter equivalence parameters, reduce functionality, condition the permanence of content, suppress access or redistribute risks, it cannot bury this under textual mountains and then claim that the consumer was informed in advance.

From this perspective, adequate information is that which presents the essential at the moment the consumer needs to decide. Its criterion is neither merely formal nor merely semantic. It is functional. Information must allow use, comparison and concrete evaluation of the performance before adhesion. In practical terms, this means that the informational regime must clearly expose, at the point of decision, the elements that structure the choice: price, total cost, relevant restrictions, ordinary mutability of the object, equivalence criteria, potential loss of items, refund conditions, cases of suspension, limits of use and substantial impacts of updates. When this content is only revealed through later investigation in long documents, consent is already born under a deficit of intelligibility. Adequate information is not that which exists in some remote corner of the system. It is that which arrives in time and with practical utility for deliberation711.

This shift of focus, from the existence of information to its adequacy, neutralizes the supplier's main alibi in platformized markets. The typical defense maintains that the consumer could have read everything calmly, simply by clicking more, opening more links, following more p. 622 cross-references or consulting FAQs and complementary policies. The problem with this defense is that it turns the duty to inform into an obligation of documentary treasure hunting on the part of the consumer. The CDC does not admit this. The protection of trust and good faith prevent the supplier from treating engineered obscurity as proof of contractual freedom. No average consumer can be considered fully informed merely because, in theory, it was possible for them to go through a documentary forest, decode technical vocabulary and reconstruct on their own the hierarchy of what was really essential. When the documentary architecture demands this kind of pilgrimage, transparency has already failed.

For this reason too, excessive cross-references, opaque chains and overly long texts are not legally innocent. The mere mention that certain general conditions exist is not enough to include them validly in the relationship. The consumer must be able actually to read and understand what the clauses mean, which rights they limit and which obligations they create. Furtive texts, an excess of cross-references, dense language and hostile graphic materiality compromise the intelligibility of the essential content and, consequently, the very legitimacy of adhesion712. Obscurity, here, is not a regrettable stylistic detail. It is a way of organizing contractual risk for the benefit of the dominant party.

The notion of performative transparency is useful precisely because it captures this difference between formal existence and decisional utility. There is performative transparency when the supplier can demonstrate informational abundance, but cannot demonstrate that the essential core was presented in a clear, conspicuous and functionally guiding way. The performance of compliance replaces the effectiveness of the duty. The system comes to produce proof of formal coverage of the supplier's risk, not of the consumer's enlightenment. On platforms, this is especially visible when purchase screens emphasize urgency, rarity, bonuses, convenience or distinction and reserve the relevant conditions for contractual depth. The contract ceases to be an instrument of information and becomes a piece of ex post shielding.

p. 623 The dogmatic consequence is important. If excess, noise and performative transparency can constitute a violation of the duty to inform, legal control cannot be limited to an abstract reading of the contract. It is necessary to reconstruct the concrete form in which the information was displayed, the moment it appeared, the interface that conveyed it, the emphasis it effectively received and the position it occupied in the decision path. In IOCs, proof of informational adequacy depends on minimum documentation of the purchase flow, the confirmation screens, the change notices, the way equivalences are presented and the visual location of the relevant limitations. It is not enough for the platform to claim generically that it informed. It must be in a position to demonstrate how it informed, when it informed and with what emphasis it informed.

It is here that the bridge to auditability is prepared. The transparency required by the CDC, in a governed environment, cannot merely be declared. It must be verifiable. This involves minimum preservation of interface trails, version history of screens, relevant messages, change notices and documentation of the communicational design actually employed. When the proof of this informational regime is concentrated in the supplier, the distribution of the burden cannot remain blind to this asymmetry. Otherwise, the duty to inform becomes a comforting fiction for the platform and an evidentiary nightmare for the consumer. The content of transparency needs to be institutionally reconstructible, or its protection dissolves into formalism.

The objective is to prevent the platform from converting documentary excess into a safe-conduct, dismantling the rhetoric that all available information equals adequate information and putting the duty to inform back on its proper axis, which is that of practical utility for consent, comparison and control. In IOCs, this means shifting the analysis from “the text exists” to “the essential meaning was really placed in conditions to be understood”. The CDC, read functionally, does not protect the supplier who documents too much. It protects the consumer who needs to decide on the basis of what really matters. It is this reading that allows one to move, in the following stages, from the critique of noise to the demand for open auditability and to the reconstruction of enjoyment as a position that cannot be degraded under cover of pseudo-transparency.

Notes

  1. The duty to inform, in the CDC system, is not fulfilled by the mere multiplication of texts, links, cross-references, windows, policies and documents formally available to the consumer. Informing, in a material key, means making cognizable in a clear, adequate and conspicuous way the essential content of the consumer relationship, especially that which defines the utility of the performance, its conditions of enjoyment and its relevant risks. This is exactly what is highlighted when interpreting Article 31 of the CDC, affirming that the supplier must ensure the consumer “clear, precise and conspicuous information about the main characteristics of the product”, including data related to its characteristics, its economic repercussion, health and safety Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 877). As a reflection of the principle of transparency, the supplier must offer “clear and correct” information about the qualities of the product and the conditions of the contract, drafting the text clearly, so as to “give the consumer the opportunity” to know the content of the obligations assumed, on pain of the contract not binding them, “even if duly formalized” Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 820-821). Practices of merely performative transparency, therefore, can constitute a violation of the duty to inform. ↩
  2. The duty to inform, in the CDC, is not satisfied by a merely quantitative logic. It is not enough for the supplier to dump documents, links, clauses or blocks of text on the consumer and then claim formal transparency. The legal duty requires positive action aimed at the information actually reaching its recipient in conditions that allow relevant understanding. For this reason, “in the regime of consumer relations, the duty to inform the consumer requires an active posture from the supplier”, it being the supplier who “must make the information reach the consumer” Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 207). The mere formal provision of content, therefore, is not enough, because there can be documentary access without true cognitive appropriation. The consumer's right to information constitutes “a much more complex question than just a problem of lack of information that needs to be ensured” Wada(Beales, Craswell e Salop apud Wada, 2016, p. 82). The duty to inform, thus, is fulfilled not by volume, by documentary accumulation or by mere formal opening of access, but by informational adequacy. ↩
  3. Excess, dispersion, complexity and low hierarchization of the essential characterize forms of functional opacity produced under the appearance of transparency. The starting point lies in the refusal of the simplistic premise according to which it would suffice to add more information to resolve the consumer's informational vulnerability. By recording that the right to information is “a much more complex question than just a problem of lack of information that needs to be ensured” Wada(Beales, Craswell e Salop apud Wada, 2016, p. 82), the logic of mere documentary filling is set aside. This obscurity appears when information is formally provided, but organized in a way that makes comparison and identification of what really matters unfeasible. This is what occurs, for example, in banking packages with numerous products that do not correspond to one another, making it “very difficult for the consumer to compare the benefit packages offered by banks” Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 168-169). The abusive practice may occur when there is material interference with the consumer's capacity to understand the terms and conditions of the product or service, with an excessive advantage obtained from the lack of understanding of the risks, costs or conditions involved Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 141-142). In this key, performative transparency is defined precisely by the conspicuous display of content whose form of presentation produces functional opacity. ↩
  4. “Paragraph 3. Written adhesion contracts shall be drafted in clear terms and with conspicuous and legible characters, so as to facilitate their understanding by the consumer. Paragraph 4. Clauses that imply a limitation of the consumer's rights shall be drafted with emphasis, allowing their immediate and easy understanding.” Brasil(Brasil, [2022]).. ↩
  5. Adequate information, in the consumer-law regime, is not defined by the simple existence of available content, but by its aptitude to present the essential elements of the contracting at the exact moment the consumer needs to decide. For this reason, “information about the performance of the obligation is essential for the consumer” and, “without it, it is not possible to make conscious choices about the product acquired or even about the service contracted” Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 206). This requirement gains density in the prior estimate as a qualified moment of decision, in which the consumer can examine “the quality of the service, the deadline for execution, the materials used”, in short, the essential elements that make up the price and the substance of the performance, being also able to clarify doubts and negotiate the terms before binding themselves Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 211). Informational adequacy requires organized presentation of the essential content in useful time for deliberation. When the structure of the offer “reduces price transparency”, “the comparability of offers” is hindered Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 168), which directly compromises the practical freedom of choice. Adequate information, thus, is that which allows one to use and compare the essential before the decision, and not that which merely exists in some remote corner of the contractual ecosystem. ↩
  6. The legal sufficiency of information, in the CDC, is not to be confused with its mere formal availability or with the existence of a body of documents the consumer could potentially consult. Excessive cross-references, opaque chains, overly long texts or a documentary architecture that hinders apprehension of the real content of the obligation do not satisfy the duty to inform in the material sense. No average consumer could understand obligations assumed in contracts that pile up cross-references to laws and provisional measures in an incomprehensible way, which is why such a bond can be undone for failure of transparency (in this case good faith) at the time of its formation, since the consumer could not understand what obligations they were assuming Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 903). For general conditions to be included, “the simple mention is not enough” that they will be used; the consumer must “be able to read and understand” what the clauses mean, and such texts frequently appear in a “somewhat furtive” way, in long or hard-to-read material Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 91-92). Consequently, documentary excess and formal availability have legal value only if they preserve the material intelligibility of the essential content of the relationship. ↩
