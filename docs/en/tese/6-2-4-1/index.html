6.2.4.1 On abstention from the use of the terminology “digital vulnerability”

See this section in the PDF

  • Dogmatics · Sections of chapter 6

p. 584

The expression “digital vulnerability” will not be adopted here because, although it has descriptive usefulness, it delivers little dogmatic yield. It names the environment in which the injury occurs, but does not isolate the legally relevant mechanism of the asymmetry. The “digital” functions, in this vocabulary, as a marker of scenario, and not as an operator of attribution. This may be enough to indicate that there is specificity in technology-mediated consumption, but it does not suffice to guide, with precision, the passage from fact to normative consequence. The problem is not merely semantic. It is methodological. A concept, to be legally useful, must cut reality so as to make the conflict tractable, stabilizing what p. 585 matters for duty, evidence, unlawfulness and remedy. When the category only describes the environment and not the mechanism, its yield for decidability is low Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 81; p. 83])656.

This requirement of delimitation is not terminological fussiness. Legal dogmatics operates by functional simplification, and not by total description of the social world. Its work is to produce operational concepts that allow the conflict to be brought back to the necessary measure of its tractability. Categories too closely attached to the scenario tend to fail precisely where the law most needs them, because they retain descriptive breadth and lose the capacity to qualify the fact in terms of attribution and consequence. It is precisely for this reason that the expression “digital vulnerability” proves insufficient. It refers to a diffuse field of risks and asymmetries, but does not clarify, with sufficient sharpness, which form of vulnerabilization is at stake or how that form should be translated into duties and protective responses Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 108; p. 247])657.

The difficulty becomes more evident when one observes the recent doctrinal use of the label. “Digital vulnerability” may designate, depending on the context, technical-operational fragility, informational deficit, behavioral manipulation, infrastructure dependence, algorithmic p. 586 opacity, technological exclusion or platform-mediated economic asymmetry. The result is an aggregating but poorly disciplined concept. It manages to gather in a single frame real contemporary phenomena, linked to the consumer journey, data protection, digital advertising, profiling and abusive practices in an informationally intensive market Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 167; p. 17)658. The problem is that, by condensing many different layers of risk, the expression ceases to indicate which of them matters legally in each case. Instead of cutting the problem, it accumulates it. And, when the category accumulates too much, evidence is dispersed and the remedy oscillates between excessive abstraction and casuistry.

This deficit is especially sensitive on the plane of legal technique. A concept does not serve only to describe a socially worrying situation. It must offer a normative form stable enough for one to say what must be proven, which duty was strained or breached and which consequence is adequate to the case. The empirical datum, by itself, does not automatically enter the universe of law. Its relevance depends on insertion into a normative structure that gives it intelligibility and operability. There is no raw legal fact. There is fact legally filtered by a normative form capable of converting it into a criterion of practical discipline Reale(Reale, 2001, p. 168; p. 96)659. It is for this reason that “digital vulnerability” proves weak. The expression names a state of affairs, but does not offer by itself the bridge between that state of affairs and the normative judgment that should follow from it.

p. 587 This same critique can be formulated in another way. The legislator and the jurist do not act as sociologists who merely portray phenomena. They start from what is to determine what ought to be, associating legally relevant consequences with the conduct provided for. A category that remains on the descriptive plane, attached to the technical climate of the problem, does not fulfill this function well. The use of broad labels tends to produce the language of an era without disciplining the reconstruction of the relevant factual basis. Thus, it is not enough to recognize that the injury occurs in a digital medium. It must be converted into a normatively filtered category, able to guide judgments of lawfulness, duties of conduct and attributable consequences Reale(Reale, 2001, p. 32; p. 189)660.

This insufficiency also appears clearly in other competing vocabularies. The recent proliferation of expressions such as “electronic”, “virtual”, “digital” or “technological vulnerability” already reveals the perception that there is a new problem, but also the difficulty of naming it with sufficient precision. Such formulas are useful for indicating the scenario in which the difficulty emerges, especially when technology operates as an obstacle to access to justice, to understanding or to the exercise of legal positions661. But they remain very close to the technical frame of the problem and less able to identify the controllable legal p. 588 mechanism of the asymmetry Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 3-4)662. They help to say where the problem happens. They say less about how it is produced in legally relevant terms.

For this reason, this discussion adheres to the expression “semiotic vulnerability”. The category aims at a mechanism, not a scenario. The central point lies not in the fact that the relation occurs in a digital medium, but in the way signs, discourses, interfaces and framings organize expectation, understanding, adherence, perception of value, perception of risk and perception of frustration. Language does not operate as ornament. It functions as an institutional technique of power. The legally relevant problem becomes the unequal administration of meaning, that is, the way the platform organizes the conditions of intelligibility of the consumption experience itself. It is in this key that signification can be analyzed as an instrument of social control, of normalization and of production of consensus, and not merely as a neutral vehicle of information Warat(Warat, 1984, p. 18)663.

This formulation has the advantage of shifting the gaze from the descriptive surface to the structure that produces legal effects. The focus ceases to fall on the technological environment itself and comes to fall on the socially operative production of meanings that conditions the way the experience will be lived, accepted and reproduced. Languages and discourses are not limited to transmitting informative content. They produce normative resonances, legitimize arrangements and p. 589 generate effects of unquestionability over certain modes of functioning. Hence the pertinence of abandoning environmental labels in favor of categories that isolate the effective mechanism of production of meaning Warat(Warat, 1984, p. 15; p. 47)664.

This methodological shift can be reinforced by a restricted parallel. Critical categories lose density when they are lowered to the environment, the medium or the scenario in which they manifest themselves. The problem lies not in the technical medium itself, but in the social relation mediated by it. When a category comes to name only the context and not the mechanism, it retains intuitive force and loses operative force. The same risk occurs with “digital vulnerability”. The reference to the digital tends to indicate the technological environment of the injury, but not necessarily the specific form of production of the legally central asymmetry. The shift to “semiotic vulnerability” seeks to avoid precisely this lowering Debord(Debord, 2013, p. 27; p. 31; p. 120)665.

In the IOC regime, this terminological choice is not only more precise; it is more useful. The platform does not merely provide information. It administers the showcase, the store, events, metrics, signals of rarity, promises of equivalence, narratives of value and the very framings through which the consumer perceives what was acquired. The relevant asymmetry is not merely a lack of data. It is the unequal administration of the meaning of the object consumed. In environments like these, expectation, consent, perception of value and perception of frustration are modulated by p. 590 language and by design. Semiotic vulnerability makes it possible to name exactly this plane of incidence, without falling into subjectivism or environmental generalities.

This choice also produces an operational gain. It reorganizes the duty to inform in a qualitative, not quantitative, key. It is not enough to ensure formal access to data. Information must be correct, clear, precise and conspicuous, without excess, dispersion or barriers that compromise its practical usefulness for the consumer's decision. When communication appears transparent while producing material opacity, the problem lies not only in omission, but in the way the meaning of the relation is organized. The requirement ceases to be “more information” and becomes information able to guide decision, understanding and adequate use of the product or service. This is the point that allows the passage from the environmental label to the normative reconstruction of the mechanism.

The goal is to reach an applicable and minimally reliable discipline. The substitution of “semiotic vulnerability” for “digital vulnerability” makes the passage from fact to legal consequence more controllable. The duty to inform can be reconstructed as a requirement of adequacy and decisional usefulness, and not as a mere volume of data. Advertising becomes controllable by criteria of ambiguity, relevant omission, identification and truthfulness. Transparency ceases to be a showcase word and comes closer to a requirement of institutional verifiability. And the architecture of supply can be qualified, where appropriate, not merely as the context of the abuse, but as its very structured form of production. The gain is not aesthetic. It is dogmatic. The category better delimits the mechanism, better disciplines the evidence and better prepares the remedy.

Notes

  1. The substitution of the expression "digital vulnerability" stems from a requirement of dogmatic operability. In legal discourse, it is not enough for a category to name, in broad terms, a technological environment marked by asymmetries and opacities; it must allow the legally relevant problem to be delimited, criteria of attribution to be stabilized and the passage to duty, unlawfulness, evidence and normative consequence to be guided. In this key, dogmatics is not organized to describe reality exhaustively, but to select, among its multiple aspects, what matters for the decision. As Ferraz Jr. observes, "unlike zetetic theories, dogmatic ones, concerned with the decidability of conflicts, do not take care to be logically rigorous in the use of their concepts and definitions, for to them what matters is not the relation with the phenomena of reality (describing the phenomena), but rather making a cut in reality, isolating the problems that are relevant for decision-making and diverting attention from the others" Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 81]). For this reason, an overly broad expression may even have descriptive yield, but remain legally weak if it does not delimit the specific mechanism of vulnerabilization or the form of its normative incidence. It is at this point that the critique of the formula "digital vulnerability" holds, since its breadth tends to name a scenario without stabilizing, with sufficient sharpness, the legally central asymmetry. The problem, thus, is not one of empirical truth, but of dogmatic yield, since, as the author records, "its problem is not properly a question of truth, but of decidability" Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 83]). ↩
  2. The refusal of excessively broad categories is also justified because legal dogmatics only makes the conflict tractable when it brings it back to a technically controllable plane. It is not the whole environment that enters legal reasoning, but only the delimitation necessary for its normative qualification and for its decision. Ferraz Jr. highlights this closure by stating that dogmatics operates by functional simplification of reality, producing "operational concepts" able to make the technical handling of the problem feasible Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 108]). Expressions too closely attached to the scenario, although intuitively strong, may fail precisely where the law most needs them, especially in organizing the fact in terms of attribution, evidence and consequence. The insufficiency of "digital vulnerability" shows in this respect. The expression refers to a diffuse field of risks and asymmetries, but does not indicate with sufficient precision what the legally relevant mechanism is or how it should be translated into duties and protective responses. In a convergent sense, Ferraz Jr. observes that concrete conflicts, even when laden with multiple social, economic and political dimensions, only become legally tractable when they are brought back to the necessary measure of their decidability, and not faced "in all their concrete extension, but to the extent necessary for their decidability with a minimum of social disturbance" Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 247]). It is this criterion that supports, here, the preference for a more delimited and controllable category. ↩
  3. The notion of vulnerability in the digital environment has real descriptive usefulness because it manages to gather, in a single frame, contemporary risks linked to the consumer journey, data protection, digital advertising and informational opacity. In this sense, the digital market is marked by growing difficulties in ensuring transparency in marketing practices based on monitoring, profiling and targeting of messages, as well as opacity in testing processes, use of dark patterns, insufficiency of consent as a legitimating basis and recurrence of security incidents involving personal data Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 167). The category thus clearly captures a contemporary ecosystem of risks and asymmetries. This aggregating character thickens when hypervulnerability is associated with the consumer's simultaneous position as addressee of market practices, as raw material of the marketing system and as product traded among its members, to which are added informational asymmetry and the widening of abusive practices founded on the prediction and manipulation of the consumer journey Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 17). ↩
  4. The refusal of the expression “digital vulnerability”, at this point, stems from an elementary methodological requirement: a legally useful concept cannot be limited to describing an environment, but must offer a normative form capable of converting social data into an operable criterion of attribution, evidence, duty and consequence. In Miguel Reale, the empirical datum, by itself, does not automatically enter the legal universe; its relevance depends on insertion into a normative structure that gives it intelligibility and operability. For this reason, the mere reference to the “digital” may even name a technical or historical scenario, but remains insufficient when it does not stabilize the legally relevant mechanism that explains the injury or guides the system's response. As the author states, “one cannot speak of Law, in the full sense of this word, without encompassing its essential moment of normativity, for social facts are only legal when inserted in a normative context” Reale(Reale, 2001, p. 168). In a convergent sense, the legal norm corresponds to the “moment of integration of a class of facts according to an order of values”, and cannot be understood without simultaneous reference to these two poles, which it “dialectically integrates in itself and surpasses” Reale(Reale, 2001, p. 96). The broad label of “digital vulnerability” proves dogmatically loose, since it points to a context, but does not convert, by itself, social experience into a sufficiently precise legal criterion. ↩
  5. The critique of categories that remain on the merely descriptive plane finds direct support in Miguel Reale. For the author, legal construction is not satisfied with the mere observation of a state of affairs, because Law does not operate as a neutral description of social reality, but as a normative elaboration capable of transforming the empirical datum into a criterion of practical discipline. For this reason, the legislator does not act “in the manner of the sociologist”, limiting himself to portraying facts as they are; on the contrary, he starts from what is to determine what ought to be, associating legally relevant consequences with the conduct provided for in case of observance or violation of the norm Reale(Reale, 2001, p. 32). It is precisely this disciplined passage from fact to norm that prevents the legal concept from dissolving into an impressionistic label or mere language of an era. In this key, broad categories that only name contexts or environments fail when they cannot stabilize a legally relevant fact-type. Reale is explicit in stating that “one cannot speak of a legal fact except to the extent and insofar as it is inserted in a normative structure”, which is why “there is no raw fact in Law” Reale(Reale, 2001, p. 189). ↩
  6. When technology comes to be treated as an obstacle to access, to understanding or to the exercise of legal positions, the methodological risk lies in converting this finding into an overly broad category. The vocabulary of “electronic vulnerability” illustrates this: it allows real situations of exclusion or difficulty produced by the technological environment to be named, but tends to operate more as a description of context than as a dogmatic instrument of attribution. The focus remains on the fact that technological transformation can vulnerabilize and exclude, as occurs with subjects cut off from access to justice in scenarios of electronic proceedings, Online Courts, Justice 4.0, Justice 5.0 and ODR platforms Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 7). This formulation has real descriptive merit, but its explanatory force rests mainly on the idea of technological obstacle, and not on the finer identification of the legal mechanism that turns this barrier into a controllable asymmetry within a regime such as that of the CDC. The category points to the context of exclusion, but does not stabilize, with the desirable sharpness, the specific form in which the injury is produced. This becomes even more evident when the problem is inserted into the broad horizon of Society 5.0, the 4th Industrial Revolution and its systemic impacts on access, requiring special attention to the so-called technologically vulnerabilized Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 5). The synthesis of the so-called sixth renewal wave of access to justice confirms this design: it is presented as a field devoted to the analysis and mitigation of technology as an obstacle to a just legal order Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 7). The vocabulary helps to name the barrier, but remains short of explaining, with sufficient precision, the legal mechanism of the asymmetry. ↩
  7. The recent proliferation of labels such as “electronic”, “digital”, “virtual” or “technological vulnerability” already reveals, by itself, an important methodological difficulty: the field perceives that there is a new problem, but still oscillates in how to name it. These expressions have descriptive usefulness for indicating that certain asymmetries emerge in a technically mediated environment, but they tend to remain very close to the scenario in which the injury occurs, and less able to identify, with dogmatic sharpness, the legally relevant mechanism of vulnerabilization. It is in this sense that one speaks of “cyber or electronic vulnerability”, also designated as virtual, technological or digital vulnerability, when technology comes to function as an obstacle to access to justice Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 3). This feature is reinforced when the terminological dispute itself revolves around the greater or lesser vocabulary coverage of “digital” or “electronic”, that is, around the name most apt to cover the technical environment, and not around the most adequate category for individualizing the legally controllable factual basis. When it is recorded that “electronic” could be an even broader term, since it also reaches digital phenomena, it becomes even more visible how much the axis of the formulation remains centered on the technological frame of the problem Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 3-4). The category comes to name the technical climate of the injury, but not necessarily its mechanism. The same occurs when technological vulnerability is described as the individual's technical, informational, material or organizational limitation in dealing with information and communication technologies Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 4). It helps to say where the problem happens and in what kind of scenario it intensifies, but still offers less precision for reconstructing, in a consumer-law key, duty, evidence, attribution and remedy. ↩
  8. The refusal of the expression “digital vulnerability”, at this point, can be deepened with Luis Alberto Warat because his semiology of power allows the focus to shift from the mere technical environment to the effectively relevant mechanism of production of subjection. The problem lies not in the “digital” taken as a generic scenario, but in the way signs, discourses and communicational framings organize understanding, expectation and adherence. Warat is explicit in stating that signification must be analyzed “as an instrument of social control”, “as a normalizing and disciplinary strategy of individuals”, “as a formula producing consensus” and “as persuasive power provoking effects of verisimilitude over the material conditions of social life” Warat(Warat, 1984, p. 18). The decisive point, therefore, is not technology itself, but the semiotic work that transforms certain conditions of power into an appearance of normality, validity and inevitability. In this key, a more precise legal category must aim at the mechanism of unequal intelligibility and semiotic capture, and not merely at the fact that the relation occurs in a digital environment. ↩
  9. Warat also offers a useful basis for justifying a legal category centered on mechanism, and not on scenario, because his reflection on language shifts the gaze from the descriptive surface to the socially operative production of effects of meaning. For the author, languages are not limited to the informative content they apparently transmit; they engender “significant and normalizing resonances of social practices”, inserting themselves into a deeper dynamic of “social production of meanings” Warat(Warat, 1984, p. 15). This makes it possible to understand that the legally relevant problem lies not only in identifying the technical context in which the relation occurs, but in grasping how signification organizes in advance the intelligibility of experience and conditions the way it will be lived, accepted and reproduced. In a convergent sense, Warat observes that pragmatic analysis makes it possible to articulate functions such as “persuasion, legitimation, anticipation”, showing how certain discourses produce an “effect of unquestionability and reality over knowledge” Warat(Warat, 1984, p. 47). Hence the pertinence of abandoning broad and environmental labels in favor of categories that isolate the effective mechanism of production of meaning. ↩
  10. Critical categories lose density when they are lowered to the environment, the medium or the scenario in which they manifest themselves. The warning appears clearly in the formulation according to which the spectacle is not to be confused with a set of images or with its technical means of diffusion, but consists in a social relation mediated by images Debord(Debord, 2013, p. 27). For this reason, its reduction to the mass media corresponds only to the apprehension of its most visible superficial manifestation, and not of the structural mechanism that the category intends to describe Debord(Debord, 2013, p. 31). The same methodological reasoning allows the refusal of formulations that, although intuitively expressive, end up naming only the scenario in which the injury occurs. The risk is to retain descriptive force and lose dogmatic operability, because the category comes to point to context without stabilizing, with sufficient sharpness, the relevant factual basis, the strained duty, the required evidentiary standard and the corresponding legal consequence. The critique of the reduction of the spectacle to the “media-related”, precisely because it brings it back to a mere instrument and erases the social logic that the category sought to denounce, reinforces this methodological caution Debord(Debord, 2013, p. 120). Analogously, the expression “digital vulnerability” tends to become impoverished when it describes the technological environment without isolating the specific form of production of the legally relevant asymmetry. The shift to “semiotic vulnerability”, thus, does not stem from a vocabulary preference, but from the search for a category more apt to identify the effective mechanism of vulnerabilization, located in the unequal administration of meaning, of expectation, of the perception of risk, of the intelligibility of the offer and of the conditions of contestation. ↩
