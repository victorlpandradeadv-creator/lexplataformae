5.1.1 On semiotic vulnerability as an operative concept

p. 395

The development of the structuring categories of the regime of the Immaterial Object of Consumption thus begins with semiotic vulnerability, established as a specific form of vulnerability in platform-governed markets of enjoyment and serving as the p. 396 legal translation of the already consolidated diagnosis of continuous consumption, practical normativity and governance by infrastructure. Enjoyment is not merely promised in the abstract, but continuously shaped by an environment that implements rules, orders priorities, modulates visibility and executes sanctions, so that access, permanence, circulation and reputation become part of the very mode of existence of the object. The purpose of the category is to offer a key for description and control capable of representing the decisive inequality in these environments, in which value, belonging and recognition are mediated by signs, by regimes of visibility and by internal mechanisms of commensuration.

The relevant vulnerability does not arise, therefore, from one-off informational asymmetries, but from a structural asymmetry of intelligibility and agency in relation to the arrangement that governs enjoyment. When the environment controls the parameters of appearance, recognition and commensuration that structure value and belonging, and keeps opaque the criteria for distributing consequences, the possibility of planning conduct, anticipating effects and contesting decisions is structurally weakened. The problem then shifts from "knowing more" to "being able to orient oneself and react". That is, enjoyment may remain formally accessible and, even so, degrade through chronic instability and through compression of contestability.

Semiotic vulnerability may be defined, in summary, as the objective condition of the consumer's exposure to a structural inequality of intelligibility and agency in platform-governed ecosystems of enjoyment, in which the production of meaning and the attribution of value depend on codes, metrics and regimes of visibility administered by the environment itself. It is vulnerability because the consumer finds themselves subjected to internal criteria that qualify conduct, rank appearances and shape objective conditions of enjoyment derived from the nexus between the promise of the object, as presented in the offer, and its effective delivery, as executed by the architecture of the service, without having equivalent means to fully understand the operating logic, contest its parameters or influence the distribution of consequences.

By way of illustration, the mobile version of the empirical laboratory studied is taken as a reference. If, on the computer, the intermediate currency already shifts the price into the fiefdom and forces the consumer to make conversions to understand how much what was bought costs, in League of Legends: Wild Rift this same logic is reorganized for an environment of compartmentalized attention, notorious for facilitating impulse decisions. In the mobile environment, it is not enough to translate reais into an internal currency. Value itself comes to be distributed across a constellation of economic signs that coexist at the same time, with different functions and p. 397 different access, which prevents the consumer from stabilizing a simple cost reference. The game's paid currency fulfills this role of gateway, but it does not close the account, because consumption branches into parallel currencies linked to rewards, performance and events, and each one creates a small circuit of its own within the same service Riot Games +1(Riot Games, 2022l; Riot Games, 2025l; Riot Games, 2021d)410.

The consequence, here, is not “complexity” in the neutral sense, as if it were merely a variety of systems. What sets in is a structural inequality of intelligibility. The consumer ceases to deal with a price that can be compared and audited in a straight line, and comes to deal with multiple internal grammars that require situational reading, because each currency “speaks” a different language. In the classic desktop version, the chance still survives of reconstructing, calmly, the equivalence between the expenditure and the item acquired, above all when the store operates with a dominant monetary core. In the mobile environment, however, this core no longer holds. The experience is designed to operate in fragments of time, and value itself is fragmented along with it, as if the platform were telling the consumer that understanding “how much it costs” is a private effort, which needs to be produced within the game, “however possible” and “with whatever attention is left”. This difficulty is not a neutral side effect of the variety of systems, but operates as a deliberate puzzle, transferring to the consumer the burden of deciphering an economy that the environment designed to be costly to read.

This becomes even more evident when one analyzes how consumption is remodeled into strictly temporary microeconomic dynamics. Events set up their own stores, with their own currencies that exist and remain valid under their own individual deadlines. Enjoyment, then, depends not only on the purchase, but also on the ability to keep up with short windows, spend at the right time and accept that certain balances expire. For the consumer playing with partial attention, in intervals, the intelligibility of value comes to depend on a schedule and on a set of internal conversions that are updated as content flows. The urgency need not be shouted, because it is already embedded in the p. 398 mode of operation, insofar as tokens are tied to dates and cease to exist after a certain period Riot Games(Riot Games, 2022n; Riot Games, 2022m)411.

Likewise, the instability of the real cost is not limited to the internal design of the store. It also appears when the platform adjusts economic parameters by region and runs bonuses that reconfigure, window by window, the perceived advantage of buying paid currency. As a result, the consumer faces not merely an intermediated price, but an intermediated price whose “purchasing power” is affected by future decisions and by promotional regimes, so that external equivalence becomes even less able to serve as an anchor of control. What, on PC, was already opacity induced by conversion and by bundles becomes, on mobile, an even more asymmetric condition of use, because consumption occurs under intermittent attention and under an economic regime that multiplies, expires and recalibrates Riot Games(Riot Games, 2021e)412.

The qualification as semiotic is not anchored in individual deficits of attention, reading or rationality, nor is it confused with a psychological description of use. It refers to the structure of the sign system that organizes the experience and that conditions what appears as delivery, as success, as failure and as loss within the ecosystem. Under the theoretical lexicon of Umberto Eco (1991; 2000), the environment not only communicates information about the object, but structures the field of interpretation in which enjoyment becomes intelligible, so that control of the decisive codes and mediations reconfigures the very possibility of decoding and reaction by the consumer.

p. 399 At this point, before going further in describing how semiotic vulnerability operates today in platform-governed ecosystems, a basic methodological point should be fixed. The inequality of intelligibility that structures enjoyment and its relevant losses is not born with the digital. It is an earlier mechanism, linked to the unequal distribution of decoding competence and to the monopoly of codes that condition access to understanding, to participation and to power. It is in this sense that Warat413 and Gnerre414 are drawn upon to establish, in controllable language, that decoding asymmetry is a mode of power asymmetry and that specialized languages can operate as barriers of exclusion.

Warat makes this core explicit when treating the semiology of power as an analysis of how signification can function as an instrument of social control, a normalizing and disciplinary strategy and a consensus-producing formula, capable of fabricating effects of verisimilitude over the material conditions of social life Warat(Warat, 1995, p. 18). In this key, the asymmetry of intelligibility is not a psychological defect of the consumer, but a discursive technology of government. Objectively, the author maintains that the legitimacy of power depends on discursive work and can be obtained through adequate manipulation of words, which shifts the appearance of publicity and formal validity to the plane of its strategic production Warat(Warat, 1995, p. 18).

This operation is reinforced when one recognizes that the stability of meaning is sustained by prior ideological coincidence, so that the belief in normative univocity and in the unalterability of signifiers does not stem from naivety, but functions as a condition of social efficacy of the ideal of the rational legal norm Warat(Warat, 1995, p. 68). For this reason, obedience can be produced without explicit command, since the social idiom tends to constitute an ordered subjectivity, making it possible to dispense with physical violence and converting it into p. 400 significative violence or imaginary violence, through which social cohesion is maintained by means of the creation of phantasmatic significations415 Warat(Warat, 1995, p. 110)

Gnerre describes language as a mechanism for blocking access to power, stating that “language constitutes the most powerful barbed wire for blocking access to power” Gnerre(Gnerre, 1991, p. 22), and defines the social function of closed codes in noting that “special languages” “exclude from communication the people of the linguistic community external to the group that uses the special language”416 Gnerre(Gnerre, 1991, p. 23). In platform-governed ecosystems of enjoyment, this mechanism tends to be radicalized when the grammar of signs, metrics and visibility, which is part of the performance itself, comes to be administered and executed as infrastructure, converting inequality of decoding into practical inequality of agency and contestation in the IOC regime.

In the empirical laboratory under study, this mechanism can be observed without any theoretical sophistication, merely by paying attention to the use of a single word. Upon entering the League of Legends store, the command offered to the consumer user displays the word “buy”. This is not a neutral verb. Both common sense and even the Brazilian Civil Code itself417 use the verb “buy” in the meaning of acquiring something that becomes “yours”, in the sense that one expects minimum stability, permanence and p. 401 some form of control over what was acquired. Even when one knows that it is a digital item, the idiom of purchase and sale automatically triggers this typical expectation and functions as an interpretive shortcut.

The grammar of acquisition does not end with the verb "buy". It extends into the organization of the inventory as a "collection", an expression that also carries its own patrimonial and semiotic density. Collecting does not mean merely gathering items. It means ordering a collection, recognizing rarity, comparing value, preserving history, completing series, displaying belonging and building a relationship of continuity between subject and object. The collector, in the ordinary social use of the word, is someone who holds qualified dominion over what they collect, even though this dominion may take different legal forms depending on the type of good involved. When the platform presents digital content as part of a collection, it mobilizes this cultural expectation of permanence and turns isolated items into a patrimonial biography, even though, on the contractual plane, the same content is described as an access license tied to the account. For this reason, any account block, removal of an item, substantial alteration of functionality or architectural devaluation of the collection does not affect merely a detachable digital unit. It affects the intelligibility of the collection itself as a bundle of continued enjoyment, whose value depends on internal recognition, minimum stability and preservation of the relationship between the consumer, their items and the ecosystem that gives them meaning.

Alongside this surface grammar, however, there is the technical grammar of the contract. The Terms of Service describe the same items as “Virtual Content” offered under an access license tied to the account Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e). As a consequence, what the interface presents as a “purchase” is requalified by the contractual instrument as conditioned and institutionally administered access. The point is not to claim that the consumer “did not read” or “did not understand”, but to recognize that the ecosystem itself organizes the decision through a sign that frames the relationship as a typical contract, while reserving, on the legal plane, a regime that does not coincide with the ordinary meaning of the verb “buy”.

It is in this friction, simple and verifiable, that semiotic vulnerability becomes intelligible. The relevant asymmetry is not merely lack of information, since information may exist and, even so, not operate as practical guidance when the language of the interface governs the experience and stabilizes expectations in real time. The platform controls the signifiers that shape the perception of what is being acquired and, at the same time, controls the institutional criteria that define what the consumer actually comes to have.

The result is an objective inequality of decoding and agency, because the consumer decides under the grammar of “buying”, but enjoyment takes place under the grammar of the p. 402 “revocable license” attached to the account. This does not justify claiming that the use of the word “buy” is, in itself and in isolation, unlawful. The issue is that, without a core that respects the minimum stability of the good, even the superficial parallel becomes false and therefore leads to the breach of the legitimate expectation of enjoyment, and only then is unlawfulness established. The aim is not to prevent the use of typical expressions, but to guarantee that the expectation is minimally met, by assigning responsibility for the auditability of alterations and requiring the preservation of equivalent functionality whenever a legitimate alteration occurs.

With these premises established, it becomes possible to advance to the properly contemporary description of semiotic vulnerability. The contribution of Warat and Gnerre consists in demonstrating that language, code and decoding competence are, since before the digital, factors of unequal distribution of power and of exclusion from participation. In platform-governed regimes of enjoyment, however, this mechanism ceases to operate merely as a discursive barrier or filter of access to formal arenas and comes to bear on the very delivery of the object, since enjoyment takes place through technical mediations that administer visibility, recognition and attribution of value. Under this framework, semiotic vulnerability is justified as a category for qualifying the IOC regime, preparing the analysis, in the keys of Eco, Debord, Baudrillard and Vanderburg, of the way in which signs, commensuration and regimes of appearance structure enjoyment and its relevant losses.

In environments of continuous consumption, visibility does not operate as a mere aesthetic attribute of the experience, but as a structuring mechanism for ordering and allocating value. The circulation of the object, the perception of its relevance and the way the consumer is positioned in the ecosystem come to depend on criteria of appearance, prominence and accessibility that are defined by the environment and executed through technical mediations. What becomes available for enjoyment, what becomes recognizable as achievement and what becomes identifiable as failure cease to be self-evident and become outcomes administered by internal rules and by hierarchies of exposure.

In this key, Guy Debord's contribution makes it possible to describe how the regime of appearance reorganizes experience and guides conduct through the management of what is seen, what is valued and what becomes socially relevant. When visibility is converted into an infrastructure of recognition and commensuration, the environment not only distributes attention, but shapes the objective conditions of enjoyment and normalizes losses and restrictions as part of the ordinary operation of the service. Semiotic vulnerability then manifests itself as the consumer's dependence on a regime of appearance that can expand, reduce or p. 403 reorder value and belonging without resorting to explicit commands, merely through continuous modulation of the conditions of circulation and prominence.

The centrality of visibility becomes even more decisive when the value of the object ceases to be exhausted in its functional utility and comes to incorporate, as a relevant economic component, the sign-value associated with distinction, prestige and position. In certain ecosystems, what is acquired is not reduced to access to content or to an isolated functionality, since it includes the possibility of being seen, recognized and validated under the internal rules of circulation. Under these conditions, enjoyment involves a relational and public dimension that is part of the core of the object, so that value depends on the permanence and intelligibility of symbolic marks administered by the environment.

Jean Baudrillard's key makes it possible to understand why relevant losses can occur without total suppression of access or formal interruption of the performance. When sign-value is part of the good, alterations that persistently downgrade the consumer's position, erase their visibility, degrade their reputation or restrict their circulation can produce a loss equivalent to the loss of the object itself, even if the “content” remains technically available. Semiotic vulnerability, at this point, reveals that the decisive asymmetry does not consist merely in knowing more or less about the product, but in controlling the criteria of valuation and recognition that determine whether enjoyment preserves, in substantial terms, the economic and social meaning that justified the acquisition.

Semiotic vulnerability is organized in two complementary faces, whose distinction is relevant to avoid diluting the concept into a generic formula. The structural face stems from the very technical-organizational arrangement that governs enjoyment, since the environment produces dependence on meaning and recognition by establishing the codes, metrics and regimes of visibility through which the experience becomes intelligible and socially valued. The consumer's position is asymmetric because the parameters that define what appears, what circulates, what counts as success and what presents itself as failure are defined by internal instances and executed continuously, conditioning their agency.

The strategic face emerges when the administration of this symbolic grammar becomes an instrument of exploitation. In this hypothesis, the same set of mediations that organizes enjoyment can be used to induce conduct, distribute costs and normalize losses by means of selective opacities, asymmetric frictions, reorderings of visibility and gradual adjustments that corrode the enjoyment bundle without producing, immediately, a perceptible total rupture. The distinction between these faces makes it possible to recognize that semiotic vulnerability is not limited to an inevitable scenario of technical mediation, but also provides language to identify p. 404 when the structure of the ecosystem is mobilized in a way oriented toward exploitative purposes, without this identification depending on express contractual commands or ostensibly abusive textual promises.

Before going further in describing semiotic vulnerability in its properly contemporary form, two essential limits should be raised. The first concerns the intensification of vulnerability through choice architecture and through the action of controlling intermediaries, capable of shaping conduct and reactions through technical mediations. The second concerns performative transparency, whereby information exists but does not turn into practical intelligibility when excess and incomparability function as operational noise within the environment.

In this context, the intensification of vulnerability through architecture is not limited to a diffuse environmental effect, since it can be described in a dogmatic key as a mode of managing behavior and the very form of performance. In continuous and platformized digital contracts, control over choice paths, frictions, rhythms of contracting and conditions of permanence shifts the asymmetry to the plane of execution, in which the consumer's decision is shaped by technical mediations and by intermediaries that do not act as simple neutral channels.

In this direction, Claudia Lima Marques418 notes that “now vulnerability and the new harms also come from the design and the action of the intermediating third party”, adding p. 405 that this digital intermediary is the “access guardian (gatekeeper)”, increasingly dominant in the contractual “time” and “mode” Marques(Marques, 2024, [p. 3]). The consequence, for the category of semiotic vulnerability, is that the asymmetry of intelligibility and agency can be produced and exploited by the interaction architecture itself, making the design of options and of avenues of reaction legally relevant as part of the fact that intensifies vulnerability in the IOC regime.

This point becomes sharper when interface patterns are described as a sanctionable commercial practice, since abusiveness ceases to depend on an isolated textual statement and comes to include the way choices are presented and induced. In this sense, the authors note that “several of these provisions are related to dark patterns, which allows us to conclude that under the Brazilian legal framework it is possible to sanction several cases of deceptive design” Bergstein(Marques; Mendes; Bergstein, 2023, [p. 4]). The relevance, for the IOC regime, lies in recognizing that vulnerability can be intensified by choice architecture and by presentation techniques that reorganize the perception of risk and hinder reaction, even if the supplier's formal discourse remains neutral or ostensibly “transparent”.

The problem worsens when transparency is simulated through excess, dispersion or heterogeneity of information and conditions, so that the multiplication of options and descriptions comes to function as an obstacle to comparability and, therefore, to the practical intelligibility of what is being supplied. In a different context, but with a directly transposable mechanism, Wada419 observes that “it was very difficult for the consumer to compare the benefit packages offered by banks, since there were countless products included and, in most cases, they did not correspond to the competing bank's offer”, a circumstance that led to the issuance of a resolution to pre-establish services and “facilitate consumers' comparison” Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 168-169). The relevant point is that information may exist and, even so, remain innocuous as an instrument of control when the environment organizes the offer so as to prevent substantive comparison and assessment, converting formal compliance into operational noise. Under this logic, semiotic vulnerability includes the consumer's exposure to p. 406 communicational structures that appear open, but produce opacity by making the formation of comparative judgment and the identification of relevant degradations unfeasible.

Semiotic vulnerability intensifies because technical mediation does not operate merely as a neutral channel of access, but as a form of routinization of expectations and dependencies. In Vanderburg's theoretical perspective, this process can be described as the everyday sedimentation of forms of mediation that naturalize patterns of conduct and evaluation, converting certain frictions, downgrades and restrictions into expected components of the experience. The consequence is that relevant degradations can be internalized as a "normal cost" of the service, which weakens the capacity to identify the harm and shifts contestation to a terrain in which the decisive criteria remain opaque or unavailable. Semiotic vulnerability, at this point, is not merely the difficulty of obtaining information, but exposure to a regime in which the very measure of what counts as loss is produced by the environment that distributes losses.

The description of this vulnerability requires attention to the technical ecology in which enjoyment occurs, so that the concept is not reduced to an abstract reading of the sign disconnected from the forms of mediation that produce and stabilize it. Under Postman (2006) and Ellul (1968), technique imposes rationalities, rhythms and criteria of pertinence that reorder social experience and make certain consequences predictable without their being presented as explicit normative decisions.

Such mediations reveal that design and organization are not elements external to the object, but choices that structure possibilities of action and distribute costs in the regime of enjoyment, defining frictions, incentives, forms of circulation and parameters of recognition that make certain losses more likely than others. In Feenberg's key, these choices can be treated as socially situated and, therefore, susceptible to legal description when they produce persistent asymmetries of intelligibility and contestation.

Recognizing this dimension does not imply converting every design decision into unlawfulness, but authorizes the analysis of design as a form of infrastructural power capable of guiding conduct and normalizing harm by means of technical and organizational patterns. With this, it becomes possible to prepare a language of attribution that reaches purposes inscribed in the architecture of the service, especially when the mediation structure is mobilized to produce dependence, selective opacity and relevant degradations without the need for express contractual commands.

Even when there are formal clarifications about rules of use or about abstract possibilities of alteration, the decisive asymmetry remains when the environment retains for itself the p. 407 administration of the codes of valuation, the regimes of visibility and the internal criteria that qualify conduct and distribute consequences. Informing, in this picture, describes the risk in generic terms, but does not give back to the consumer equivalent means of understanding, verifying and contesting the decisions that reconfigure their position.

For this reason, legal protection needs to reach the practical normativity produced by infrastructure, under which relevant degradation can result from persistent modulations of visibility, reputational downgrades, selective functional restrictions and internal sanction mechanisms that do not present themselves as textual nonperformance, but operate as erosion of the enjoyment bundle. Semiotic vulnerability, by shifting the focus to the governance of the sign and of mediation, prepares the demand for criteria that make such effects verifiable and attributable, without reducing control to an episodic assessment of formal consent.

Another relevant contribution to the discussion derives from A proteção do consumidor contra as práticas comerciais abusivas no código de defesa do consumidor: novo ensaio para sistematização e aplicação do direito do consumidor, the doctoral thesis of Ricardo Morishita Wada (2016), in which the author proposes a systematization of abusive commercial practices in the CDC, with special attention to the structural effects of certain offer and market architectures on comparability and control. Especially relevant is the instrument Wada offers for treating opacity and “transparency” as problems of structure, not as one-off communication failures, making it possible to describe situations in which the appearance of informational compliance coexists with mechanisms that degrade practical intelligibility and hinder control and attribution. ↩
