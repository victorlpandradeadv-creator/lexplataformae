6.2.4.2 On language, code and meaning-vitiated consent

See this section in the PDF

p. 601

In consumer relations, the problem of information was never merely quantitative. Contract, offer, interface and rules are forms of language, and it is through them that the market organizes expectation, adherence, comparison and resistance. A significant part of contractual injustice therefore arises not from the plain absence of data, but from the way legally relevant meaning is produced, distributed and withheld. In a platform environment, consumers may receive texts, dialog boxes, links, policies and notices in abundance and still remain beyond any real understanding of what they accept. It is on this plane that semiotic vulnerability becomes intelligible in its communicative aspect. The law faces not merely a failure of information, but a regime of communication that determines who is able to understand, who is able to compare and who is able to contest.

The CDC already contains the normative core of this problem. The offer and presentation of products or services must ensure correct, clear, precise and conspicuous information681. This means that the technical complexity of supply does not entitle the supplier to hide behind indecipherable vocabulary, cascades of documents or communicative architectures that deliver words and withhold meaning. Likewise, consumer contracts do not bind consumers who are not given a real opportunity to learn their content beforehand, nor when they are drafted in a way that hinders understanding of their meaning and scope682. The decisive point, therefore, is not proving that “the text was there”, but assessing whether the legally relevant meaning could actually be known.

p. 602 This conclusion requires recognizing that language is not a neutral vehicle. Normative, contractual and institutional texts operate as techniques for producing selective intelligibility. They do not merely inform; they stabilize meanings, distribute access to significance and filter the possible readings in advance. Strictly speaking, contract and interface are not transparent containers of data, but institutional forms of organizing meaning. Legal and regulatory language is always shot through with strategic, ideological and pragmatic factors, which is why the problem of consent cannot be reduced to the physical availability of the statement. Consumers may receive the text, click, accept and even repeat its terms without actually grasping what it means in practice683.

The point goes deeper once one sees that the coherent organization of discourse also silences problems, excludes uncomfortable readings and stabilizes a semantic frame controlled in advance. The appearance of univocity, closure and naturalness in the contractual text is not necessarily a sign of neutrality. Often it works precisely as a mechanism of communicative subjection. What is at stake is not only what the text says expressly, but how it defines the field of what can be said, compared and contested684. On platforms this is especially important, because contractual language p. 603 does not come alone. It works together with the interface, the sign-up flow, the selective highlighting of information, the placement of links and visual framings that steer consumers to accept before they understand.

This is why the asymmetry does not stem only from missing information. It can be produced by special languages that function as barriers of competence. Consumers receive the text, but not real access to the code that organizes its meaning. Technical, legal and business language has a social function of distinction and exclusion. It speaks to the initiated and, at the same time, reaffirms the position of those who master the code. In practical terms, merely making terms, policies or notices available does not eliminate semiotic exclusion when full understanding remains reserved to those who master the system's grammar685. The difficulty, then, is not episodic. It is structural. Consumers do not merely get one piece of data wrong. They adhere within a frame of language that already places them outside the center of intelligibility of the relationship.

It is at this point that one can speak of meaning-vitiated consent. This is not a one-off factual error, such as mistaking a price or a single deadline. It is an inability, p. 604 structurally produced by the communicative conditions of supply, to decode, compare and anticipate relevant consequences. Submission stems not only from express omission, but from an architecture of language, interface and framing that conditions in advance access to the legal and economic meaning of the decision. Consumers may be formally informed and still be materially cut off from the meaning of what they accept. This is the communicative form of semiotic vulnerability.

Under the CDC, this brings the problem back to the duty to inform in its material sense. Suppliers do not discharge this duty merely by making the content technically accessible through a click, a scroll or a download. What matters is whether the average consumer could read and understand what those clauses meant, which rights and obligations were being assumed and what practical consequences followed from adhering. A bare reference to the existence of general terms is not enough when the meaning remains opaque686. In this picture, the principle of transparency is no peripheral formality. It goes to the very essence of the transaction, because information forms part of the content of the contract and, if it fails, it compromises the legal quality of the performance.

This explains why contractual obscurity, in consumer relations, is not an innocent stylistic flaw. It is evidence of structural disloyalty in the way consent is organized. In adhesion contracts, consumers take no part in the drafting and enter the relationship trusting that there is a minimum of textual intelligibility and fairness. Review of these clauses is therefore not limited to a neutral grammatical examination, but is guided by objective good faith and by the protection of the adhering party's trust. Pre-drafted clauses must be read so as to respect the legitimate interests of those who accepted them without being able to negotiate them, and obscure drafting triggers precisely the presumption that the supplier failed in its duty to organize consent on fair terms687.

p. 605 This asymmetric communicative regime also helps to explain why semiotic vulnerability is not a loose cultural metaphor. It describes a real disadvantage in decoding, comparing and resisting, produced by the way the market organizes its language. In IOCs, this is amplified because the supplier's communication is not limited to the written contract. It extends to showcases, in-game stores, purchase screens, event messages, update announcements, metrics, rankings, signals of rarity and framings of value that are part of the consumption experience. Consumers face not just a text. They face an entire grammar of governed enjoyment.

The dogmatic consequence is twofold. First, the duty to inform must be read as a duty of intelligibility, adequacy and verifiability, and not as the mere delivery of a volume of documents. Second, consumer protection in platform environments requires standards capable of reducing the asymmetry of meaning and the asymmetry of evidence at the same time. If the platform controls the language through which the object is presented, understood and accepted, it also tends to control the records, framings and traces through which adherence and frustration may later be reconstructed. Language, code and consent are therefore not a peripheral topic. They are one of the grounds that justify, in what follows, requirements of open auditability and criteria of restoration by functional value equivalence.

Notes

  1. “Art. 31. The offer and presentation of products or services must ensure correct, clear, precise and conspicuous information, in the Portuguese language, about their characteristics, qualities, quantity, composition, price, warranty, expiry dates and origin, among other data, as well as about the risks they pose to the health and safety of consumers.” Brasil(Brasil, [2022]). ↩
  2. “Art. 46. Contracts that regulate consumer relations shall not bind consumers if they are not given the opportunity to learn their content in advance, or if the respective instruments are drafted in a way that hinders the understanding of their meaning and scope.” Brasil(Brasil, [2022]). ↩
  3. Legal and institutional language is not neutral; it operates as a technique for producing and stabilizing meaning, steeped in political and strategic factors, which rules out the image of the contract or the interface as mere transparent containers of information. Pragmatics applied to law makes it possible to see that ideology is “a factor inseparable from the conceptual structure made explicit in general norms”, which requires the interpreter to read in a way that uncovers “the connections between the words of the law and the political and ideological factors that produce and determine their functions in society” Warat(Warat, 1984, p. 47). Legal language thus does not merely inform; it organizes some possible intelligibilities and blocks others. This finding is crucial for understanding how consumer consent can be vitiated by structurally produced meaning, and not merely by a one-off absence of data. Normative discourses involve a “game of persuasion and mythification” that stereotypes meanings and legitimizes the reproduction of power under an appearance of normality Warat(Warat, 1984, p. 45). Through the use of “a language impregnated with stereotyped significations”, what is introduced, “under the supposed appearance of an objective description, is an ideological dimension not formulated in legal language”, the central aim of such expressions being “to influence and determine opinions” Warat(Warat, 1984, p. 71). As a result, consumers may perfectly well receive the text, click, accept and even repeat its terms without ever grasping what it really means in practice, because communicative subjection depends not only on lack of access to the statement, but on the selective and asymmetric way in which meaning is produced for them. ↩
  4. Normative, contractual and regulatory texts produce selective intelligibility and can organize forms of communicative subjection, since the coherent organization of discourse itself works as a filtering mechanism that silences problems and excludes uncomfortable readings. The “coherent organization of a discourse” can “silence problems” and, through these silences, perform “political functions in society”, while it “explicitly forces subjects to say or repress certain things” Warat(Warat, 1984, p. 50). Intelligibility is thus not distributed neutrally; it is produced selectively. This selectivity is reinforced when legal discourse presents itself with an appearance of univocity and closure: belief in normative univocity presupposes “prior ideological coincidence”, and the unalterability of signifiers sustains the ideal of a rational norm precisely because it secures certain “social effects of the law in society” Warat(Warat, 1984, p. 68). The critique of the claim that legal language is closed shows that the demands for totality and closure of the system are not mere logical requirements, but ideological representations that serve specific functions of the legal form Warat(Warat, 1984, p. 54). As a result, institutional texts can organize communicative subjection not only through what they say expressly, but through the way they stabilize meanings, exclude interpretive alternatives and induce adherence to a semantic frame controlled in advance. Acceptance of rules, framings and institutional conditions can be produced discursively, with no need for an explicit command, through a “discursive work” that manipulates words and stereotypes to manufacture acceptance and naturalize authority. Consensus on the legitimacy of power results from discursive work, which is effective precisely because it operates through “a game of stereotyping”, whose primary function is legitimation Warat(Warat, 1984, p. 18). Power produces meanings that make adherence seem spontaneous, reasonable or inevitable, constituting an “ordered subjectivity”, shaped by the “social idiom” and progressively alienated from the ends of the power that shapes it. In this situation physical violence becomes largely dispensable, replaced by “significant violence” and “imaginary violence”, which can neutralize the critical power of subjectivity and produce internalized obedience Warat(Warat, 1984, p. 110). Certain effects of acceptance spread with no need for explicit demonstration, relying on stereotypes, topical formulas and beliefs already settled, so that discursive alignment spreads “like an infection” or “contagion” Warat(Warat, 1984, p. 88). Communicative subjection therefore depends not only on what is said in imperative form, but on the production of a horizon of meaning in which obeying, adhering or accepting comes to seem simply the normal course of things. ↩
  5. In platform-mediated contractual environments, the fragility of consent stems not only from the absence of information, but also from the organization of a functionally exclusionary language, which allows the message to be formally received without ensuring actual access to its meaning. The problem is therefore not reduced to the occasional lack of a given piece of data, but involves a structural asymmetry of intelligibility: consumers come into contact with text, interface and contracting commands, but remain outside the code that organizes the real legal meaning of the transaction. This is where Gnerre offers direct support, by showing that certain specialized languages serve not only to communicate, but also to distinguish and exclude. As he states, "the central function of all special languages is social: they have a real communicative value but they exclude from communication the people of the linguistic community external to the group that uses the special language and, on the other hand, they have the function of reaffirming the identity of the members of the reduced group that has access to the special language." Gnerre(Gnerre, 1991, p. 23). Merely making terms, policies or notices available therefore does not eliminate semiotic exclusion when full understanding remains reserved to those initiated in the technical, business or legal code of the relationship. Along the same lines, Gnerre observes that "language constitutes the most powerful barbed wire for blocking access to power", adding that drafting a document with legal value requires more than knowing the language in the ordinary sense, since one must master "a whole complex and archaizing phraseology that is customary" Gnerre(Gnerre, 1991, p. 22). Transposed to digital consumption, this shows that a defect of consent may arise not only from concealing information, but from the structural withholding of meaning itself. ↩
  6. Under the CDC, the duty to inform is not satisfied by merely making content formally available, nor by the bare physical existence of clauses accessible through a click, a scroll or a reading that is possible in the abstract. It is a material duty of clarity and understanding, aimed at ensuring that consumers have actual access to the legal and practical meaning of what is presented to them as the basis of consent. Communicative asymmetry is therefore not resolved by proof that the text was "there", but by establishing that its content could really be known by the average consumer, especially in contexts of strong unilateral pre-drafting: "the mere mention that CONDGs will be used in the contract is not enough, it is necessary that the ordinary man can read and understand what those clauses mean, which obligations and rights he is accepting" Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 91-92). The exclusion of intelligibility undermines the legitimacy of consent because it affects the very material content of the information legally owed, in conflict with objective good faith and the protection of trust. The principle of transparency governs the pre-contractual stage and the conclusion of the contract itself, being "more than a mere formal element", since it "affects the essence of the transaction", as information "is part of the content of the contract or, if it fails, represents a failure in the quality of the product or service offered" Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 819). Obscure texts and communicative architectures that deliver words while withholding meaning produce a zone of materially vitiated consent. ↩
  7. Under the CDC system, review of adhesion contracts and obscure drafting is not limited to a neutral grammatical examination, but is guided by objective good faith and by the protection of consumer trust, in light of contractual justice and the legitimate expectations of the adhering party. With pre-drafted clauses, consumers take no part in formulating the text and often enter the relationship trusting that the drafting respects a minimum of fairness and intelligibility. The new function of contract law therefore puts the principle of interpretation in accordance with good faith back at the center, so that Article 47 of the CDC, together with the general clause of Article 51, IV, allows pre-drafted clauses to be interpreted "in a way that respects the trust and the legitimate interests of the consumers who accepted these pre-drafted clauses" Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 94-95). Obscurity is not an aesthetic flaw, but evidence of structural disloyalty in the way consent is organized. Breach of the duty of clear drafting is sanctioned by Article 46 of the CDC, under which contracts do not bind consumers if they are drafted in a way that hinders understanding of their meaning and scope, a sanction rooted in the "presumption of bad faith of the supplier-drafter of the contract" Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 902-903). An incomprehensible clause is not proof of robust consent; it is a symptom of betrayed trust with a veneer of drafting. ↩
