3.2.5 The business model and the architectural teleology of platforms

See this section in the PDF

Concepts in this section

  • Abusive teleology

  • Foundations · Sections of chapter 3

p. 243

The image of the contemporary fief becomes more than a metaphor when one observes the real structure of the bond in platform-operated games. The territory is not an “open market”, but a space enclosed by account, access structure, terms of use, curation of access and its own mechanisms of sanction, so that the consumer's permanence depends on continuous authorization and on conformity to a practical normativity. Tribute presents itself as fulfillment of a requirement in attention and dedication, microtransaction, pass, subscription or purchase of signs of distinction, while the decree manifests itself as patch, rework and unilateral reconfiguration of internal economies that alter the substance of enjoyment without formally extinguishing the nominal ownership of the good. The feudal logic, in this sense, does not describe a medievalizing aesthetic, but a form of economic organization in which the platform holds the monopoly of infrastructure and the power to rewrite, over time, the material conditions of valorization and of the existence of consumption.

Digital platforms can be understood as teleological architectures of continuous extraction, institutional and technical-functional designs aimed at stabilizing rent and power in markets for immaterial goods, shifting economic command from the product to the conditions of access, permanence and circulation. The dialectical reading of Varoufakis (2024) and Dowbor (2024), combined with the structural homology of the lumpenproletariat in Marx (2011; 2013), makes it possible to reconstruct, in layers, the economic core that sustains this form of power.

For Varoufakis, the key is the shift of command to infrastructure. The platform ceases to be merely a company that sells a product and comes to operate as an informational landlord that controls the conditions of entry, permanence and circulation in a governed environment. The figure of the “cloud serf” does not function here as a moral metaphor, but as a social form derived from a market in which enjoyment depends on continuous private intermediation and in which exit becomes costly through loss of history, repertoire, reputation, p. 244 ties and accumulated investments, including in the form of identity and symbolic capital objectified in progress, collections, public performance records and signs of status in the ecosystem.

Dowbor offers the meso-structural register of this transformation by describing informational rentierism as a mode of extraction based on “turnstiles” and “tolls”, in which rent shifts from the act of producing to the act of controlling passage. The platform stabilizes value not through natural rarity of the object, but through institutionally produced rarefaction, coupled with authorization regimes and legal-technical mechanisms that reintroduce friction where technique would tend toward abundance. In this framework, monetization ceases to be a simple price and assumes the form of recurrent charging for permanence, for continuity and for predictability of access, that is, for governed enjoyment itself.

This institutionally produced rarefaction is not sustained only by commercial decisions or by “market” scarcity, but by a legal-technical enclosure of the informational. Intellectual property mechanisms, articulated with contractual licensing regimes, technological restriction measures and interoperability controls, operate as an institutional technology of governance of circulation, since they transform reproduction into authorization, use into revocable permission and access into a continually renegotiable condition. By reintroducing friction where technique would tend toward abundance, this enclosure stabilizes the capacity to impose tolls, modulate the experience and prevent alternative forms of enjoyment and competition, making control of access an asset as valuable as, or more valuable than, the immaterial object itself.

Marx, in turn, presents a critical instrument for identifying the point at which consumption ceases to be mere expenditure and comes to function as invisible production. The platform does not extract value only when it receives payment, but when it captures activity, including the time, attention, conduct, symbolic validation and social participation of users, then converted into data, metrics, network effects and competitive advantages. The user appears formally as external to the wage relationship, but is materially incorporated into the regime of valorization as an unrecognized productive exterior, providing the continuous input that feeds the machine of monetization and governance.

From this convergence, the business model of platforms can be described as an architecture of continuous extraction applied to ontologically non-scarce goods. Its stability depends on three structural operators that act in an integrated way: production of artificial scarcity, manufacture of risk as a discipline of presence and capture of the user's diffuse productivity as economic and semiotic input. The platform does not p. 245 limit itself to offering goods and services. It governs the conditions of enjoyment, converting access into permanence and circulation into authorization.

The articulation between artificial scarcity, manufactured risk and diffuse productivity makes it possible to understand why, in IOC markets, competition tends to take on the character of a tournament and concentration becomes structural. When the platform holds the infrastructure of circulation and the grammar of symbolic value (by defining what counts as rarity, what counts as status, what counts as meta and what counts as belonging), it transforms competition into a contest over ecosystems and over governance capacity, and not over isolated products. Whoever wins the contest over the ecosystem comes to hold not only market share, but the power to impose conditions of access, permanence and visibility, reorganizing behaviors at scale and converting private rules into competitive advantage.

This feeds back into a captivity of demand: switching costs, functional and symbolic dependencies, network externalities and accumulated investments make exit costly and permanence structurally probable. In this scenario, artificial scarcity is not a marketing expedient. It is the instrument for stabilizing power.

This stabilization is not explained only by “consumer preferences” or by superior efficiency in an isolated product. It is explained by the fact that, in the immaterial realm, control of the environment can coincide with control of the market itself. The route of access to the object is, at the same time, the private infrastructure that organizes its enjoyment and administers the conditions of circulation. Competition then shifts to the contest over command of gates, protocols, interfaces, catalogs, recommendation regimes and rules of permanence, that is, over control of devices that function as essential market infrastructure. Economic power comes to reside in the capacity to define the possible experience, modulate the time of enjoyment, impose exit costs and produce functional and symbolic dependencies that, in practice, convert formal freedom of choice into governed permanence.

Artificial scarcity is the first operator of this architecture because it reinserts rarity where technology would tend toward abundance. In immaterial goods, reproducibility puts pressure on price and dissolves natural rarity, so that it becomes functional to produce rarefaction by institutional decision, by means of windows and rotations of availability, temporary exclusivities, seasons and limited events, access tiers, countdowns, editions and forms of conditional acquisition. Scarcity, here, does not describe the object, since it describes the access regime and is a mode of governing circulation.

Manufactured risk is the second operator, since it converts the access regime into a practical discipline of return and presence. The relevant risk is not only the classic risk of the product's p. 246 malfunctioning, but the risk of missing an opportunity, of falling behind and of suffering symbolic and functional depreciation within the ecosystem, with the evaporation of value accumulated in the form of status, repertoire, progress and belonging. This grammar of risk is produced by recurrent mechanisms, such as imposed schedules, with seasons, availability windows, rotations and limited events, sanctions and downgrades, with loss of position, visibility, rewards or access to certain layers of the service, and planned uncertainty, with opaque criteria, unilateral modulation of rules and calculated instability of what counts as meta or as value. By organizing the experience as continuous administration of the possibility of loss, the platform creates an environment in which permanence ceases to be an episodic option and comes to operate as rational conduct protecting investments and position, so that consumption ceases to be an event and comes to function as a routine.

Diffuse productivity is the third operator, since it closes the economic circuit without depending exclusively on payment. The user's activity sustains the ecosystem and is converted into an asset, into data and metrics, social proof and diffusion, symbolic validation, network effects, patterns of comparison and hierarchies of visibility. In this arrangement, the user is not merely a recipient of an offer, because they participate materially in the valorization of the environment that governs them. This participation need not appear as labor to operate as input, since it manifests itself as capturable and recombinable presence, circulation and conduct, feeding monetization strategies and the very competitive stabilization of the platform.

The question is thus objectively detached from the moral dimension and becomes a legal-structural one. The diagnosis does not depend on attributing the phenomenon to “compulsive consumption” or to “bad choices”, because the relevant coercion does not present itself as an explicit imposition of purchase, but as dependence produced by design, by governance and by narratives that organize value and belonging within a closed circuit.

The economic architecture of the platform contains a teleology of continuous extraction that materializes in recurrent mechanisms. Among them is the unilateral modulation of the conditions of enjoyment, with alteration of rules, prices, access tiers, ranking criteria and recommendation regimes. Also present are functional opacities that prevent verification and contestation, such as non-auditable criteria, informational asymmetries and uncertainty about what determines results. Added to this, finally, is the continuous calibration of behavior, by means of A/B tests, gamification and selective frictions that make certain paths natural and others costly. When the consumer is kept as a serf of the infrastructure by means of artificial scarcity and manufactured risk, the legal question ceases to be p. 247 merely contractual and becomes one of the legitimacy of the architecture, that is, of the economic purpose incorporated into the normal mode of functioning of the service.

This structure explains why, in the illustrative scenario of the thesis, the player's frustration is not mere subjective “displeasure”, but a symptom of an economy of power in which the consumer does not merely buy an item, but enters and invests in a governed regime of belonging that has a direct interest in modulating their emotions through cycles of satisfaction and frustration. The rework that redesigns a character, effects and position in the metagame is functionally equivalent to a reordering of the territory that devalues past investments without offering an internal mechanism of value equivalence, and without allowing the consumer to take what they acquired outside the platform's jurisdiction. The contemporary fief is sustained precisely by this asymmetry, since exit is formally possible, but economically irrational when reputation, history, ties, signs of distinction and acquired goods remain trapped in the same technical enclosure.

The closer the platform comes to practical sovereignty over access, visibility and the meaning of what was acquired, the more the axis of protection needs to bear on integrity, restoration and controllability, and not on a narrow notion of one-off delivery.

The question that arises, therefore, is to what extent Law can impose limits on business models that, in order to monetize the immaterial, need to transform access into dependence, promise into a permanent cycle and enjoyment into a toll circuit. The problem shifts from the atomized examination of events and clauses to the evaluation of the economic purpose incorporated into the arrangement, that is, to the examination of an institutional design that produces continuous extraction as a normal result of its functioning. This purpose is not sustained only by direct economic coercions, but by the administration of the social conditions of recognition of value within the ecosystem. The closing mechanism of this economic architecture is the governance of the visible, the desirable and the recognizable, by which technical abundance is converted into socially perceived scarcity and belonging is transformed into a discipline of permanence.
