4.1.3 The simulacrum and the value of digital goods

p. 295

So far, it has been argued that, on platforms, the promise of the IOC circulates as a continuous social scene and that visibility, comparison and public validation cease to be side effects and become part of the very economic infrastructure of permanence. It remains to explain how this circuit keeps value operating when the relevant enjoyment changes over time, without this appearing as an evident rupture of the offer. The hypothesis is that, in certain regimes of immaterial consumption, symbolic equivalence performs the economic function of delivery, preserving prestige, belonging and commensurability in the ecosystem even when enjoyment is displaced by continuous governance. To describe this mode of operation without moralism, an operational definition of simulation and simulacrum must be fixed in the vocabulary of Jean Baudrillard326.

Simulation shifts the debate and describes a regime in which sign equivalence sustains value and recognition without requiring a stable reference in enjoyment Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1991, [p. 9-10]). The difference is structural, because pretense preserves the reality principle, while simulation throws into crisis the boundaries between true and false, real and imaginary327 (Baudrillard, p. 296 1991, [p. 9-10]). In this framework, the map ceases to represent the territory and comes to precede it, and the signs of the real328 can replace experience itself with an operative double sufficient to short-circuit verification329 Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1991, [p. 8-9]).

His perspective becomes especially useful when he observes that, in consumer society, the socially valued promise tends to demand measurement and proof through objects and signs, which excludes from the ideal of consumption a happiness that dispenses with these markers Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1995, p. 47). This makes it possible to argue, without reducing the argument to a denunciation of illusion, that value can be maintained by signs of delivery and by visible criteria of equivalence, which warrants treating the simulacrum as a mechanism capable of preserving the economic circuit of the IOC even when the relevant enjoyment changes.

Here, simulacrum describes a functioning in which sign equivalence suffices to keep value circulating and the promise appearing fulfilled. Simulation thus functions as a mechanism of restoration by appearance, in which “delivering” comes to mean keeping the item within a regime of signs that continues to produce belonging, rarity and commensurability, even though the practical conditions that sustained this position are displaced by continuous governance. The analytical gain is to separate the public continuity of value from the effective continuity of the purchased experience, making it possible to describe capture not as a “lie”, but as the administration of criteria of equivalence that operate on the symbolic plane and that can conceal relevant losses without ostensible rupture of the object. With this, sign-value can be treated as an economic dimension proper to consumption, and not as a mere by-product.

p. 297 Baudrillard provides the key to treating the value of the IOC as something that circulates through distinction and belonging, and not as the simple enjoyment of an “object” taken in isolation. The starting point is the refusal of consumption as appropriation of use value, since “one never consumes the object in itself” and objects “are always manipulated as signs that distinguish the individual”, whether by affiliation to the reference group or by demarcation before a higher status Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1995, p. 60). With this, consumption comes to be described as a social operation of classification, in which the promise of the good is inseparable from what it allows one to signify publicly. This framework is completed when consumption is defined as a “system of exchange of signs” that integrates the group, functioning at the same time as a morality and as a “system of communication”, so that pleasure ceases to be the rational end and appears as the individual rationalization of a process whose ends lie elsewhere330 Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1995, p. 78). The framing makes it possible to establish that distinction, prestige and belonging are not side effects of immaterial consumption, but part of the economic circuit that stabilizes value and comparability within the ecosystem, which makes it necessary to make explicit how certain signals function as “signs of delivery” capable of sustaining equivalences and recognition even when the relevant enjoyment shifts.

The sign-value described by Baudrillard makes it possible to treat IOCs as goods whose delivery is stabilized by signs of position, and not only by some isolated technical utility. This requires naming the “signs of delivery” that make the promise economically operative on platforms, because it is these signals that transform acquisition into recognition and make the good function as a socially legible difference within the ecosystem. Novelty, rarity, exclusivity, prestige, belonging, distinction and “edition” are not communicational adornments. p. 298 They are classificatory categories that define what must be read as superior, desirable and “of the moment”, organizing hierarchy and comparison in real time.

When the markers remain stable and circulable, the economic circuit of the IOC can continue operating even under internal reconfigurations of the object, since what sustains value is the permanence of the public legibility of difference. For this reason, it is necessary to show why symbolic equivalence proves capable of replacing, to a certain extent, the reference to effective enjoyment, describing how simulation can “deliver” through signs that short-circuit the verification of the purchased experience.

The symbolic equivalence relevant here is the displacement of delivery, from direct utility to confirmation by signs. Baudrillard makes this displacement explicit by maintaining that, as a concept specific to contemporary society, consumption is not structured by needs and natural utility, but by the reorganization of this primary level into a system of signs Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1995, p. 79). On this reading, delivery is no longer reduced to the enjoyment of a content and comes to depend on the maintenance of a circuit of legibility that produces value through distinction and visible proof. The consequence appears when the author describes opulence as an accumulation of signs of happiness and points out that the satisfactions conferred by objects can operate as the anticipation of a virtual satisfaction, lived as a miracle in everyday practice Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1995, p. 22-23).

At the same time, this equivalence is not sustained by correspondence with a stable reference, because simulation sets in through the “liquidation of all referentials” and their “artificial resurrection in systems of signs”, material that offers itself “to all systems of equivalence” and to a “combinatorial algebra” Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1991, [p. 9]). These elements make it possible to maintain that, in IOCs, restoration can operate as the preservation of signs and of commensurability, keeping the promise economically active through visible markers even when the relevant enjoyment is displaced, which prepares the next step, in which the structural instability of the object will be established as a condition of the ecosystem and not as an exception.

A contemporary example of how signs of delivery can be artificially reordered to sustain value, commensurability and prestige is the creation of the Exalted tier and of the acquisition space called Sanctum, presented as a shelf “above” the traditional top of the catalog. After the controversy involving Soul Fighter Samira and the public discussion about the elasticity of the meaning of Ultimate, the platform does not “correct” the semantics of the top. It reorganizes the hierarchy from above, creating a new marker of prestige and coupling it to a form of acquisition that transforms scarcity, urgency and randomness into mechanisms p. 299 constitutive of value. The Sanctum is described as an exclusive shop in which the user buys Ancient Sparks331 for 400 RP and uses them to draw rare content, with a specific “banner” for Exalted skins, in addition to temporal rotation and no guarantee of return Riot Games(Riot Games, 2025d)332. The institutional justification frames Exalted as a set of “hyper-exclusive skins”, that is, goods explicitly described as luxury within the catalog, not as a simple “natural evolution” of the Ultimate tier Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024g).

What matters for the argument of the simulacrum is not the moralization of gacha333, but the way sign equivalence reorganizes what counts as the top and shifts the proof of delivery from the content to the very consistency of the circuit of signs. The official description of the Sanctum records that Exalted skins are the only S Tier content of the corresponding banner, with an extremely low chance and a guarantee only on the eightieth draw, in addition to availability for a limited time Riot Games(Riot Games, 2025d). The consequence is that exclusivity ceases to be a social inference and becomes architecture. Prestige is produced by rule, not by necessary material backing. In this logic, “being top” ceases to mean only “delivering more” and comes to mean “existing within a regime of rarity and controlled access” capable of manufacturing, by itself, the public legibility of distinction.

The effect becomes even clearer when one observes that the material grammar presented as Exalted recapitulates, to a large extent, the grammar historically associated with the top of the catalog as Ultimate, based on transformation, multiple forms, sensory layers and in-game reactivity. The first Exalted skin, Arcane Fractured Jinx334, is described as a package with multiple forms, with “two models” distributed across “three forms”, with p. 300 VFX335 oriented to narrative and specific voiceover336 per form, switchable in a match through a dedicated mechanism Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024g). In general terms, what distinguishes the forms presented by the skin is not the functionalities, but the fact that it is the official skin of the character that portrays her in her “alternative versions” as portrayed in the series Arcane. In terms of symbolic functioning, the tier “above” need not be substantially superior to the previous top to operate as a new top. It suffices that the platform reorganize the regime of signs and attribute to it a category as a marker of luxury linked to mostly aesthetic-cultural elements, while coupling it to a regime of acquisition that produces the promise of exclusivity as proof.

The reorganization also appears in the public chaining of releases. The system is designed to sustain a rotating showcase in which the top is not only expensive, but temporally contingent, with scheduled replacements. The same space that offers Exalted skins confirms the logic of window and rotation by presenting subsequent entries and replacements of offer, such as Sahn-Uzal Mordekaiser337 and Spirit Blossom Morgana338 in specific periods Riot Games(Riot Games, 2025b).

In the case of Morgana, the staging of the launch itself reinforces the luxury framing through artistic prestige external to the game, with the announcement of a musical collaboration associated with the item, which functions as a social marker of distinction that does not stem from the “technical” content itself, but from the symbolic capital mobilized to value it Tan(Tan, 2025)339. In 2026, the same pattern repeats with Revenant Reign Viego340, presented as an Exalted skin p. 301 accompanied by its own animation and insertion into the institutional circuit of updates source · Riot Games(REVENANT..., 2026; Riot Games, 2026f).

The consequence, on the plane of the simulacrum, is that the sign of the top is kept operative through continuity of showcase, rotation and presentation, while the catalog can couple to the tier external signals of prestige to sustain the narrative of luxury, even when the content remains within a premium grammar already known to the ecosystem itself.

On the economic plane, the reorganization makes it clear that the increase in cost is produced mainly by the engineering of acquisition and not by a necessary and proportional increase in the material backing of the content. Riot itself records that an Ultimate skin is priced at the level of 3250 RP Riot Games(Riot Games, 2023a). The Exalted, by contrast, operates through attempts at 400 RP per Ancient Sparks, with a guarantee by the eightieth attempt, which sets a ceiling of [80 × 400 RP = 32.000 RP] Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024g; Riot Games, 2025d)341. In RP, the maximum value of the Exalted is almost ten times the value of an Ultimate, since [32.000 / 3.250 ≈ 9,85] Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024g; Riot Games, 2023a).

Based on the official RP prices for Brazil, the difference becomes even more significant. While an Ultimate skin of 3250 RP tends to cost342 something close to R$ 102,80 in the most cost-effective purchase arrangement to reach that level343, the ceiling of 32.000 RP requires large packages and can reach something close to R$ 911,60344 in a typical minimum arrangement Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024c)345. The economic leap thus functions as illustrative empirical evidence that luxury, in this arrangement, is produced mainly by signs and by administered access, and not by a necessarily superior material leap. In traditional markets, the qualification of luxury presupposes a recognizable backing of material, technical and symbolic superiority capable of sustaining, before the consumer, the implicit expectation of quality and of distinction proportional to the price. In the Sanctum, however, the leap in cost is produced above all by scarcity, rotation and limited time, while the increment of p. 302 content remains, in essence, within the grammar that historically already organized the top of the catalog Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024g; Riot Games, 2025d).

In this sense, the Exalted tier makes legible a simulacrum in an advanced state. The ecosystem repackages the already known premium grammar as a promise of the top and reinscribes it as luxury through a regime of random acquisition and an implicit spending ceiling, so that “delivery” comes to be inferred from the consistency of the circuit of signs, from engineered rarity and from the social proof of exclusivity. What previously presented itself as a verifiable technical criterion to justify the top is shifted to a criterion of access and sign, which becomes, itself, the engine of legitimation of equivalence. Value, here, does not depend on a stable reference in enjoyment. It depends on the maintenance of a regime of signs capable of producing belonging, rarity and commensurability as delivery.

In IOCs, the maintenance of the regime of signs can preserve value and commensurability even when what matters in concrete enjoyment changes. It is for this reason that immaterial consumption lends itself to capture by simulacrum. Instead of depending on a stable reference, as would occur with goods whose use imposes itself through their own materiality, the IOC exists as a position of enjoyment within an ecosystem that can reorder categories, signals and criteria of relevance over time. Thus, instability does not appear as a one-off defect of the product, but as a structural condition of the ecosystem, since the same environment that delivers also decides what will be perceived as delivery, through which marks and under which parameters of comparison.

When the regime of signs remains operative, relevant reconfigurations can occur without ostensible rupture. The effect is that relevant losses can be absorbed as an adjustment of the ecosystem, instead of appearing as nonperformance, because the proof of delivery tends to be read through the preserved signs of status and belonging, and not through the objective verification of the specific enjoyment that was at stake. It is here that the simulacrum touches the legal problem: the distinction between nominal restoration and effective preservation of the position of enjoyment, and the requirement of parameters of verifiability capable of preventing symbolic equivalence from functioning as a zone of impunity.

Baudrillard makes it possible to treat instability as a structural condition of consumption, and not as an episodic defect, because renewal and substitution appear as a requirement of the very system of differentiation. The law that runs through the universe of consumption is described as the “law of renewal of distinctive material ‘from the top down’”, which conditions the innovation of objects as social material of distinction, and not as an ascending response to needs Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1995, p. 62). In the same register, obsolescence ceases to be a p. 303 technical contingency and becomes part of the design of the cycle: one does not produce in view of duration, but “in view of its death”, through a “calculated obsolescence” that condemns the object to the ephemeral Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1995, p. 42).

The argument establishes that the relevant stability is not the stability of the item, but the stability of the movement that reorders differences, since the system needs the distinctive material to expire in order to preserve distance and to keep the hierarchy operative. This logic connects directly to the way differences are produced, since signification does not operate as personal expression, but as a “differential, marginal and combinatorial” variation, dependent on the “industrial production of differences” Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1995, p. 88), and is lived by the consumer as freedom and aspiration when, in truth, it corresponds to “conditioning of differentiation” and “obedience to a code”, in which differentiating oneself amounts to instituting a “total order of differences” that surpasses the individual346 Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1995, p. 60). With this, the simulacrum ceases to be merely a mechanism of one-off restoration and appears as a way of keeping value operative in an environment in which exchange through signs demands constant renewal, which makes it possible to advance to the legal closing of the block: if the ecosystem can reconfigure value by code and by signs, it becomes necessary to make explicit why auditability and equivalence of p. 304 functional value function as parameters of verifiability against merely nominal restorations.

The practical consequence of this framework is that the delivery of the IOC comes to be evaluated, within the ecosystem, by continuity of legibility and by maintenance of distinctive markers, and not by transparent preservation of the specific enjoyment that was at stake. Since the renewal of distinctive material and obsolescence operate as engines of the system, the environment can shift categories, reorder relevance and recalibrate signals without this appearing as rupture, because what is maintained as “proof” is the persistence of the circuit of recognition. In this condition, restoration can take the form of nominal equivalence, preserving the name, the class, the label, the displayable position and the internal commensurability of the item, while restrictions, effects, access, relevant uses or performance conditions that constituted the acquired position of enjoyment are altered.

It is there that the simulacrum becomes legally relevant, because signs can function as a zone of impunity, sustaining an appearance of delivery while the experience is reconfigured by continuous governance. Hence the requirement of parameters of verifiability, with auditability and functional value equivalence.

When simulation becomes a regime, equivalence ceases to depend on a stable reference and comes to operate as an internal circulation of signs. Baudrillard describes this displacement by maintaining that the system becomes a gigantic simulacrum, no longer capable of being exchanged for the real, but exchanging itself in itself in an uninterrupted circuit whose reference and circumference are located nowhere Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1991, p. 10). The key is that value can remain operative because the proof of delivery shifts to the consistency of the circuit itself, that is, to the public stability of markers and equivalences that the ecosystem keeps in operation.

In this framing, preserving the legibility of the item and the commensurability of its place in the hierarchy may suffice for the offer to seem fulfilled, even though the effective enjoyment has been reconfigured from within. On platforms, this appears when restoration maintains label, displayed category, place in the showcase, rarity marker, prestige frame and parameters of comparison, so that the object remains legible as “the same” for the ecosystem, even if what sustained, in practice, the position of enjoyment changes.

Equivalence proves true for the system because it preserves the parameters of recognition, not because it preserves the specific functions, restrictions, practical effects, modes of display and symbolic effects that made that IOC count as a position. Nominal restoration becomes a structural mechanism of capture, since it allows the circuit of value to keep p. 305 functioning through continuity of signs while the relevant experience is displaced from within, without ostensible rupture and without a clear marker of loss. This prepares the next step, in which it will be necessary to anchor, in Baudrillard, the idea of exchange through signs and of substitution by equivalents as a mode of functioning of value, in order to sustain theoretically that imagistic restorations can keep the economic circuit operating even when they do not preserve the purchased enjoyment.

The mechanism of nominal restoration appears clearly in the way the platform is able to reconfigure, by update, what the ecosystem calls a “champion”. The sign remains identifiable, remains counted and remains displayable, since the name, the presence in the account and the public frame of belonging are not removed. Only here a “champion” is not a character automatically available like the roster of a classic game. It circulates as a store asset, displayed and acquirable within the client itself, and its presence in the account functions as a public sign of collection and belonging347. This acquisition can occur through internal currencies, but can also result from direct monetary spending, insofar as Riot sells premium currency for this purpose, which brings the champion structurally closer to the status of other equally “purchasable” virtual items, such as skins348. Consequently, when the name remains while the functional content is replaced, what is preserved is not a mere “menu option”. What is preserved is the institutional form of delivery that the system recognizes as possession, even though enjoyment has been displaced from within.

Riot describes this regime as legitimate and ordinary updating, that is, as continuous governance of the service. The effect, on the plane of consumption, is that the promise is no longer a relation between the consumer and a relatively stable set of properties of enjoyment and becomes a relation between the consumer and a system that preserves the sign of sameness while reserving the power to shift, from within, the conditions that made that position count. The simulacrum, here, does not deceive in the banal sense. It stabilizes. It p. 306 replaces the preservation of enjoyment with an operative equivalent, which circulates as public proof that “the item is still there”, and, for this reason, dispenses with the stable reference.

The case of the champion Aatrox shows nominal restoration in its “classic” form. Riot itself, in a public text, states that the changes would be “the biggest” ever made on the gameplay plane and that Aatrox “need[ed] to be rethought from scratch” Riot Games(Riot Games, 2018c)349. This is not fine-tuning. It is a complete overhaul, but packaged as continuity, since the public sign remains and social equivalence is reorganized around it.

The case of the champion Mordekaiser, in the rework that pushed him into the duo lane dynamic, is even more radical because nominal restoration is not limited to swapping the functional content of the “same” champion. It shifts the axis of experience to a different regime of coordination, in which enjoyment is no longer mostly calibrated for a game of individual reading of a lane and comes to depend on a pair logic, with another kind of trading, another kind of power window and another kind of risk. It is still the same game, just as volleyball is still volleyball. But the change alters the role the player performs within the system.

It is the difference between having an outside hitter, whose excellence is measured by jump, ball timing, variety of attack and the ability to decide the point against the block, and, by unilateral decision of the team's internal rules, seeing this athlete converted into a libero, whose excellence is measured by defensive reading, short movement, forearm pass control, coverage and stability of reception350. The athlete remains on the roster, remains “the same name” on the scoresheet, remains in uniform and present on court, but the practice that justified the investment and that defined the technical pleasure of enjoyment has been replaced by another form of participation.

This is the appropriate image for the simulacrum: the continuity of the sign is preserved, while the content of the experience is rewritten from within, and this can happen in such a p. 307 deliberate way that, in a recorded public discussion, a developer describes Mordekaiser as “forced”351, in the context of the discussion about the competitive strategies related to melee bot lanes352 MOOBEAT(Moobeat, 2015).

The institutional structure of the ecosystem itself confirms that this is not an exception, but a grammar of operation. Riot maintains a support page that defines “champion update” as a graphic or kit change and acknowledges, head-on, the discomfort of seeing one's favorite champion “changing out of nowhere” Riot Games(Riot Games, 2021c)353. The update does not appear as a defect of the service. It is presented as part of the normal mode of existence of the object. And when even third-party programs come in to “unlock champions” on condition of a bond and maintenance of the benefit, what is exposed is precisely the administered character of access, and not a roster offered willingly Riot Games(Riot Games, 2022o)354. The IOC, in this cut, exists as an administered and rewritten sign, and public commensurability functions as the backing of continuity. It is exactly here that the distinction between nominal restoration and effective preservation of enjoyment ceases to be a detail and becomes the very place of the problem.

The inversion operated by the simulacrum can be described particularly clearly when the acquisition of the sign comes to be worth more than the experience that should justify it. There are situations in which the consumer invests dozens of hours of their finite life to obtain a digital item whose relevance lies, above all, in the symbolic inscription it produces in the ecosystem. In this configuration, the symbol no longer represents the fun, the challenge or the lived enjoyment, since it replaces this experience as the measure of what “was worth it”. The map p. 308 precedes the territory not only on the cognitive plane, but on the existential plane, since the validation of effort shifts to the possession and display of the sign, and the lived is reinterpreted retroactively as a necessary means to an end that is, in itself, sign.

The value of the IOC becomes hyperreal when the platform offers signals sufficient to short-circuit the verification of enjoyment and to convert the investment of time into evidence of delivery. Thus, the logic works by self-confirmation: the greater the cost in time, the greater the subjective and social need for the sign to “be worth it”, and the more the ecosystem publicly recognizes this sign, the more it becomes real as value, even though its reference in concrete experience is unstable or degradable. Effort does not guarantee the referent, but reinforces the efficacy of the simulacrum, because it increases the consumer's dependence on the circuit that transforms time into prestige and prestige into proof of meaning.

This explains why nominal restorations can be economically effective, since consumption operates by distinction and by visible criteria of equivalence, and inner enjoyment does not suffice as a social measure of value Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1995, p. 47; p. 60).

If the value of the IOC circulates through position and through visible criteria of equivalence, then the standard of delivery that matters cannot be reduced to minimal technical execution or to isolated formal statements. The offer, in these markets, materially includes the form of recognition of the good in the ecosystem, since it is this public legibility that stabilizes commensurability and makes the promise operate as “delivery” for the consumer and for the community. For this reason, a legal reading of adequacy that treats the good as a closed unit and evaluates performance only by what remains technically accessible tends to mistake the object, because it ignores the semiotic dimension that integrates the acquired position of enjoyment itself. Therefore, controlling adequacy in immaterial consumption requires considering whether restoration preserves the position of enjoyment in its relevant elements, and not only whether it preserves a nominal label or a residual access that continues “working”. The fit does not anticipate the dogmatic development, but establishes the starting point for it, because it shows that the decisive discrepancy can occur without ostensible rupture of the item, precisely when the circuit of signs keeps operating as proof of delivery while the relevant experience is reconfigured from within.

The distinctive dimension does not enter consumption as an optional side effect, because it functions as a structural rule of integration and hierarchization. Consumption operates as a system of ordering of signs that integrates the group and organizes social exchange, imposing itself through largely unconscious constraints, so that the structural organization surpasses individuals Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1995, p. 78). For this reason, difference is not an adornment freely p. 309 chosen after the utility of the object has been met, but the very form through which conducts and objects become legible within a scale of positions.

It is in this sense that Baudrillard states that the consumer lives their distinctive conducts as freedom, aspiration and choice, and not as conditioning of differentiation and obedience to a code Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1995, p. 60). The subjective perception is of autonomy, but the logic that organizes this autonomy is social and codified. “Differentiating oneself”, in this regime, does not mean affirming a real singularity; it means entering the combinatorial game of differences, instituting the total order of differences that constitutes the fact of total society and surpasses the individual Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1995, p. 60). The promise of personalization appears as the maximum of individuality, but the mechanism described is the opposite: differentiating oneself tends to amount to adopting models and renouncing real difference, because “personalizing” differences are hierarchized and converge toward models from which they are produced and reproduced Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1995, p. 88-89). In structural terms, the fundamental logic is that of differentiation and personalization under the sign of the code, which produces individuals as “personalized” in conformity with general models Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1995, p. 93).

Taken together, Baudrillard's premises establish that the legally relevant function of the good is not exhausted by the isolated utility of the object, because what is at stake is its capacity to inscribe the subject in a grammar of recognizable positions, comparable and validatable in a social circuit. Utility remains, but does not govern alone. The core is insertion into a regime of legibility and hierarchy, in which enjoyment and value depend on participation in an order of differences whose intelligibility is provided by the system itself.

In an IOC, this translates into position in the ecosystem. The delivery that matters is not only the act of “making something available” to the user, but the maintenance of the criteria by which difference is produced, read, displayed and recognized. For this reason, restorations that preserve only the name or the appearance can keep the code functioning and, even so, destroy the position of enjoyment that conferred meaning and value on the good. In these cases, the consumer remains before a “compatible” sign, but loses, in practice, what integrated their legitimate qualitative expectation about what was acquired, to the point that the decision to contract might not have occurred if this alteration had been present from the start. The result is a formal continuity with material rupture, because the system remains legible, but the experience that was sold as equivalent ceases to exist.

When consumption operates as obedience to a code of differences and when the validation of value depends on recognizable signs, the consumer's trust tends to shift from the isolated item to the ecosystem that sustains the legibility of this position. p. 310 Practical security ceases to rest on the verification of a one-off enjoyment and comes to rest on the expectation of continuity of the circuit of recognition, that is, on the belief that the environment will keep guaranteeing comparability, prestige and belonging on the same terms on which the acquisition was made. In IOC markets, this displaced trust is functional, because the position only “has value” while the ecosystem keeps active the criteria of reading and the public markers that make difference circulate as difference. The problem appears when the simulacrum allows this continuity to be staged by sign equivalences, preserving the signal of delivery and the social commensurability of the good even under relevant shifts in enjoyment, in restrictions and in the practical effects that constituted the acquired position. In this condition, trust becomes exploitable by continuous governance, because the platform can recalibrate from within the bases of experience and, even so, maintain the appearance of fulfillment through the visible stability of the code, which makes it difficult to turn relevant loss into demonstrable rupture. This makes the core of the evidentiary problem clearer: the promise-enjoyment nexus tends to be replaced, in practice, by a promise-signal nexus, and it is this shift that makes auditability and functional value equivalence necessary responses of verifiability, and not moralism against the symbolic.

Displaced trust stabilizes with more force when the ecosystem itself comes to produce, from within, the evidence of what counts as real and as delivery, dispensing with external verification of enjoyment. In Baudrillard, this logic appears as the tautology of the model, in which validation does not depend on confrontation with a referent, since reality is nothing but the model expressing itself, and the circulation of signs leads only to a whirling evidence, which allows one to ask whether this is not pure tautology and proof by existence355 Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1991, p. 135). For IOCs, this key makes it possible to describe why the consumer tends to trust the environment that validates, compares and makes visible the acquired position, since the p. 311 practical proof that it “delivered” comes to be the regular functioning of the circuit of recognition itself. When confirmation comes from the regime and not from the demonstrable preservation of the relevant enjoyment, internal losses can be absorbed without ostensible rupture, provided the ecosystem keeps producing the public equivalence of the good. It is this self-reference that makes restoration by signs plausible, preparing the next step, in which the distinction between nominal equivalence and effective preservation of the position of enjoyment can be made explicit as a problem.

If the validation of value can be produced by the circuit of signs itself, the restoration of the IOC cannot be treated as a simple nominal substitution arbitrated by the administrator of the ecosystem. What was at stake in the acquisition was not only the persistence of a label, a class or a prestige tag, but a position of enjoyment with specific functions, restrictions and symbolic effects, dependent on the way the environment makes this position operative and recognizable.

In this scenario, “restoring” cannot mean preserving only the signal of delivery and the public commensurability of the item, allowing what constituted the relevant experience that sustained the value of the position to degrade or change without demonstrable criterion. The consequence is objectively identifiable, since legally adequate restoration must be read as substantive restoration of the position of enjoyment and not as merely nominal or imagistic equivalence, since equivalence by appearance is precisely the space in which the simulacrum allows the economic circuit to keep functioning without preserving what was effectively acquired. It is in this direction that the parameter of functional value equivalence becomes necessary, since it constitutes the minimum requirement capable of preventing the platform from turning the sign into a zone of impunity by arbitrating “equivalents” that keep it apparent without preserving the relevant delivery legitimately acquired.

In the cut of the simulacrum, the closing of the mechanism lies in the fact that equivalence no longer depends on transparent preservation of enjoyment, since it comes to operate through signs that, in the very social body of consumption, produce practical effects equivalent to what, in referential regimes, would be supposed to exist as a verifiable foundation. Baudrillard's formulation helps to stabilize this passage by distinguishing dissimulation from simulation and by setting aside the reading that simulating would merely be lying. In the example mobilized by the author, simulation is not limited to staging a nonexistent state, insofar as it produces symptoms, since the simulator determines in himself some of the respective symptoms Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1991, [p. 10-11]).

With this, the external point of reference ceases to be an operational requirement, because proof no longer depends on verifying a hidden real, but is short-circuited by the efficacy of the sign, p. 312 which manufactures by itself the indications and effects it should merely represent. This makes it possible to treat the primacy of appearance as a form of value, since the system does not require a stable ground of experience to recognize delivery, but verifiable and circulable symptoms. For the IOC, maintaining label, nominal rarity, visibility, comparative position and public markers can function as delivering even when relevant functions, restrictions and effects have been displaced by continuous governance. This is the point at which restoration by symbolic equivalence becomes a structural risk, which prepares the next step on exchanges through signs and substitutions that preserve the economic circuit while downgrading, from within, the acquired position of enjoyment.

The simulacrum, when it operates as equivalence by symptoms and as validation by visible criteria, creates a zone of “delivery by appearance” that tends to escape both everyday perception and traditional forms of proof. The reason is structural. The position of enjoyment of the IOC does not depend only on an isolated item, but on a set of conditions of intelligibility and recognition that the platform governs in layers that rarely present themselves as a clause or as an explicit alteration of the “product”. Internal classifications, showcase curations, highlighting criteria, relevance metrics, matchmaking rules, algorithmic orderings, circulation scripts and even the public signs of prestige themselves can be recalibrated without this translating into an identifiable event of “breakage”, and, even so, these adjustments alter what the item is worth, for whom it is worth and how it is recognized as valuable. In this environment, relevant losses can be absorbed as a “normal adjustment of the ecosystem”, because social proof keeps working and the symptoms of equivalence remain available, even if the enjoyment effectively acquired has been degraded or displaced. Semiotic vulnerability then appears as a loss of verifiability. The consumer not only does not know the terms; they cannot demonstrate, with ordinary means, the nexus between promise and enjoyment when the reference itself has been replaced by operative signs and when the decisive variables are opaque, dynamic and administered unilaterally.

This neutralization of proof is not metaphorical. In Baudrillard, the decisive point is that simulation does not merely dissimulate or pretend, because pretending leaves the reality principle intact, with the difference merely disguised. Simulation, on the contrary, calls into question the difference between true and false, real and imaginary, producing a scenario in which, if the simulator produces true symptoms, he cannot be objectively treated either as ill or as not ill Baudrillard(Baudrillard, 1991, [p. 10]). When the system operates through signs capable of producing effects equivalent to those of the real, the external criterion of verification loses ground, since the very p. 313 distinction that would allow the rupture to be demonstrated is neutralized by the functioning of the sign. For the legal regime of the IOC, the consequence is that restoration by appearance tends to be hard to capture by traditional proof. The platform maintains signs of continuity, prestige and commensurability as operative symptoms of delivery, while it alters relevant functions, restrictions and conditions of enjoyment, without this presenting itself as a demonstrable difference on the plane of the real of the object. The result is an asymmetry of verifiability, which makes it plausible to operate equivalence by appearance as a practical shield against the demonstration of nonperformance.

The legal contribution of the simulacrum, therefore, is not to treat consumption as illusion, but to make nameable the practical shift of the criterion of performance. When equivalence is stabilized by signs of delivery, such as label, class, rarity frame, displayable position and internal commensurability, public continuity can function as a substitute for the preservation of the relevant enjoyment, so that restoring comes to mean conserving recognizable symptoms of persistence, even if restrictions, effects, modes of access and performance conditions that constituted the acquired position are altered from within. The legal problem is not the symbolic, but the practical neutralization of the proof of the nexus between promise and enjoyment, which requires criteria of traceability and substantive control of equivalence.
