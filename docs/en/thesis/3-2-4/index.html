3.2.4 The mutation of immaterial competitive dynamics through intellectual property

p. 232

The engineering of scarcity and risk described does not operate in a vacuum, since it reorganizes the very grammar of competition. When the economic object ceases to be a material good, delivered and circulable as a thing, and becomes a continuous immaterial good, mediated by platform, account, catalog, interface and access rules, what is contested among economic agents changes in nature. Instead of competing only on productive efficiency, on price or on the intrinsic quality of a product, what comes to matter is controlling access, that is, defining who enters, where they enter, under what conditions they remain and at what cost they manage to leave.

The competitive mutation described manifests itself clearly in interactive entertainment operated by platform because competitive value is not concentrated in the “quality” of an isolated good, but in the capacity to keep the consumer bound to a proprietary grammar of belonging, progress and distinction. In the ecosystem of an online game, cosmetic items and reworks do not function as peripheral “accessories”, but as pieces of an economy of identity that depends on social visibility, comparison and recognition within the environment itself. Economic rivalry, in this framework, shifts to the administration of the gateways to these layers of meaning, to the distribution circuits (rotating shops, events, passes, availability windows) and to the raising of migration costs, since the investment is not transferable outside the technical fief. What is contested, therefore, is the infrastructure that makes the promise consumable, and not merely the promise in the abstract.

For this reason, competition in platform economies tends to assume an asymmetric design, shifting from the contest over products to the contest over a position of gatekeeping over technical and symbolic ecosystems. When economic power is concentrated in the capacity to intermediate flows of attention, data, reputation, digital libraries and routines of use, competition ceases to function as a simple alternation of consumer preferences and comes to function as competition for entrapment by design. “Market power” exceeds the field of share in sales, becoming command over the very infrastructure in which the consumer's “choice” becomes possible.

p. 233 Competition in markets for material goods tends to preserve structural frictions that limit the consolidation of a fully stable dominance, even if they do not prevent it entirely. Installed capacity, logistics, marginal costs, access to inputs and productive dispersion function, as a rule, as brakes that make leadership relevant, but more contestable over time. Even where there are concentration and durable advantages, the contest remains anchored in physical and organizational constraints that impose real costs of expansion and of maintaining command.

In platform-mediated immaterial goods and services, the dynamics reorganize because part of these frictions is replaced by concentration mechanisms peculiar to digital infrastructure. Non-rivalry and reproducibility at reduced marginal cost do not eliminate competition, but shift the terrain of the contest toward network effects, lock-in, standardization of interfaces and protocols, economies of informational scale and accumulation of data. The dominant position thus tends to stabilize less through efficiency in an isolated product and more through control of the channel, of access and of the conditions of enjoyment, which reconfigures the very competitive grammar.

Technology giants, such as Amazon in certain online retail markets and cloud services, or Mercado Livre in the intermediation of electronic commerce in specific regions, not only lead, but in many cases define and control the very infrastructure on which competition (or its appearance) takes place. This dominance does not rest only on the productive efficiency of a specific good, but on the strategic control of networks, data, algorithms and, above all, of attention and of access to the consumer, elements that characterize the informational domination and digital rentierism previously discussed. The capacity of a single entity to control the entirety of an immaterial good or its main access route reconfigures the economic order with profound sociopolitical implications.

If immaterial competition tends to produce structural winners, it becomes necessary to explain why this victory proves not merely contingent, but stabilizable. In material goods, domination faces physical and social frictions (installed capacity, logistics, sources of input, productive dispersion). In digital ecosystems, however, the very access route to the good coincides with the private infrastructure that organizes it. In this scenario, economic power is consolidated not only through efficiency, but through governance, since controlling the gate amounts to controlling the market, insofar as the gate becomes the market itself.

The scenario ceases to be merely an example and reveals itself as a typical case of competition by enclosure. The “rare” cosmetic item is not rare by technical nature, since its reproduction is p. 234 trivial, but because the platform administers scarcity, prestige and circulation as internal variables of the market itself, converting technical abundance into economic scarcity. The unilateral alteration of the item's appearance, effects or place in the internal economy, as in the scenario described, operates as an exercise of competitive power because it redefines ex post the meaning and utility of the good within the proprietary language, without the consumer having an equivalent route of restoration outside the ecosystem. Competition thus comes to function as a contest for sovereignty over signs and for the capacity to rewrite, over time, what “is worth” and what “delivers”, shifting consumer protection to a terrain in which control of the market is inseparable from control of the semantics of consumption itself.

In practical terms, this persistence arises from the combination of network effects, lock-in, standardization of interfaces and protocols, economies of informational scale and accumulation of data, which convert preference into dependence and make spontaneous competitive contestation structurally improbable.

It is here that intellectual property rights cease to appear as a peripheral legal title and come to operate as a material component of the architecture of closure. The decisive economic point is simple and direct, since, in the market for immaterial goods, reproducibility would tend to dissolve price, so that rent depends on reintroducing scarcity by institutional decision. When this decision is coupled with license contracts, accounts, DRM251, catalogs and authentication mechanisms, the incentive to create gives way to a technology of blocking, so that circulation comes to be treated as infringement and enjoyment is converted into authorization. In this context, competition ceases to be only a contest over products and becomes a contest over enclosures.

If competition, in the immaterial, tends to produce structural winners through capture of the channel, what remains to be explained is the legal mechanism that stabilizes this victory over time. It is here that intellectual property, coupled with infrastructure and contracts, ceases to be only a title and comes to function as a competitive technology of enclosure.

The stabilization of competitive victory in immaterial markets requires converting technical abundance into socially constructed scarcity, because the reproducibility of the immaterial would tend to dissolve price and, with it, the possibility of rent, if there is no institutional decision to reinstall barriers. Intellectual property ceases to operate only as an incentive and becomes part of an arrangement of enclosure, functioning as a true p. 235 “legal latifundium”252 that immobilizes accumulated technologies and knowledge under titles of prolonged exclusivity Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 44). When this latifundium is coupled with license contracts, accounts, DRM, catalogs and authentication mechanisms, the incentive to create gives way to a technology of blocking and the objective of copyright comes to operate as a mechanism for closing access253, transforming access into a conditional license and enjoyment into a precarious authorization, continually reconfigurable, insofar as circulation comes to be treated as infringement Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 44).

The consequence, for consumption, is that what gives concreteness to the consumer's right is not the possession of bits, but the legal materiality of the object254, understood as stability and predictability of the practical conditions that make enjoyment possible over time, in an environment in which economic control shifts from the good to the process and utility depends on continuous governance under private dominion Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 51-52). It is an institutional concreteness, since the infrastructure and the mechanisms that sustain access, circulation and continuity remain under the control of platforms and their governance systems Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 8; p. 51-52). The decisive economic point is that, in the immaterial, reproducibility would tend to dissolve price, and this inversion is particularly sensitive in the networked environment, because copying is not a deviation, but an ordinary technical condition of p. 236 enjoyment itself, insofar as copying is part of transmission, storage, caching and, in a certain sense, reading itself Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 51). By legally requalifying this constitutive gesture, intellectual property ceases to operate only as an incentive and comes to function as a technology of blocking, since it transforms circulation into infringement and enjoyment into authorization, allowing the supplier to govern the experience of the IOC as precarious and modulable access Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 51).

This logic of enclosure and informational toll finds critical and paradigmatic application in the examination of contractual clauses that grant developers the automatic appropriation of derivative works and user-generated content. The phenomenon is not an isolated topic of intellectual property, since it constitutes a concrete manifestation of artificial scarcity applied to cultural platforms, as the supplier uses its infrastructure to capture the symbolic surplus255, that is, the value generated in the very use and circulation of signs in the ecosystem, such as reputation, belonging, styles, narratives and visibility. The economic centrality of this surplus is described by Dowbor, following Gorz, in the analysis of the brand, of advertising and of symbolic values as the basis of a new formation of value Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 23). Produced by users under the rule of standard clauses, this cultural and collaborative flow is recast as a private infrastructure of rent, which reinforces the supplier's command over circulation and enjoyment in the ecosystem.

When the platform already occupies a gatekeeper position, intellectual property and license contracts cease to be a legal detail and come to operate as a device of economic government of the ecosystem. The competitive effect is direct, because it is not enough to offer a better product when the dominant operator defines what counts as legitimate use, which practices are tolerated, who may circulate signs and on what terms. Thus, intellectual p. 237 property does not act only as exclusivity over an asset, since it functions as a rule of entry and permanence in an environment in which enjoyment itself depends on continuous authorization. What is consolidated is an economy of cumulative permissions, in which each portion of access functions as a key, and the accumulation of keys is converted into market power.

Enclosure becomes particularly visible when one observes the point at which consumption is converted into production within the ecosystem. On cultural platforms and, paradigmatically, in game ecosystems, value lies not only in what is sold to the consumer, but also in what the consumer circulates, such as derivative works, content, styles, tutorials, mods, narratives, reputation and prestige. When standard clauses transform this flow into automatic appropriation by the platform, competition ceases to be only a contest over supply and becomes a contest over the capture of the cultural surplus produced within the market itself.

This logic of enclosure and informational toll finds paradigmatic application in the examination of contractual clauses that grant developers the automatic appropriation of derivative works and user-generated content (UGC). Although UGC models may require licenses functionally necessary for the circulation of content in the ecosystem, the legal problem emerges when the standard authorization expands beyond what is necessary and is converted into automatic appropriation of the content generated by the user, transferring to the platform the ownership of creative interactions and derivative works produced in the course of use.

At this point, the supplier not only charges for access, but captures symbolic surplus and creative labor from the consumer, reinforcing the toll economy over the cultural base of the ecosystem, without proportional reciprocity and under the unilateral governance of the rightholder256. It is not, therefore, a simple reservation of rights, but an architecture that recasts collaborative effort and social participation as a private infrastructure for generating rent.

From the competitive point of view, what is at stake here is not only “who has the best content”, but who controls the conditions under which content can exist, circulate and be reused. When the platform captures UGC and derivative works by default, it is not p. 238 only appropriating cultural value produced within the ecosystem: it is expanding its gatekeeping power over the very horizon of creation and circulation of signs in that market. Intellectual Property, then, does not operate only as one-off exclusivity, but as an infrastructure for stabilizing victory in the competitive “tournament”. It fixes the advantage over time by transforming access and reuse into a regime of continuous authorization. The duration and elasticity of these rights cease to be a technical detail and become part of the economic core of enclosure. Extending terms and expanding scopes means prolonging the toll and reducing the space for contestation through spontaneous competitive mechanisms.

Dowbor associates the problem of Intellectual Property not only with the existence of the right itself, but with its historical extension under organized pressure, observing that “we will only have digital access to a work 70 years after the author's death” Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 43) and questioning whether very long copyrights and “indefinitely extended” rights are disseminating culture or, on the contrary, hindering the social process Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 46). The argument is one of political economy, since, if the realization of profit is concentrated mostly in the first years of commercial exploitation Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 43), maintaining exclusivity for decades operates as rent without a proportional incentivizing function257 and imposes a massive social cost on research and creative reuse to sustain concentrated private flows. In this context, time becomes a deliberate mechanism of rentierism, because extending protection terms means, in practice, prolonging the toll on the common heritage of humanity.

A critical point of Dowbor's argumentative axis lies in the distinction between the figure of the creator and that of the rentier. The author, as subject of intellectual creation, has little to do with this dynamic, since the rights almost always belong to intermediaries Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 44). The finding shifts the focus of the discussion. It is not, strictly speaking, a matter of ensuring the artist's remuneration, but of identifying a chain of intermediation that converts copyright into a financializable asset and administers access to the good in a structurally captive market. From this perspective, intellectual property reveals itself as a legal platform of extraction, p. 239 because whoever holds the title holds, at the same time, the turnstile of access. In this arrangement, the moral discourse of theft comes to function as a tool of legitimation. Through guilt and criminalization, it sustains charging for the circulation of goods whose use by one individual does not reduce availability to another.

Dowbor makes explicit that the proliferation and overlapping of IP rights can produce prohibitive transaction costs and block subsequent innovations by “placing multiple obstacles” Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 47), configuring a tragedy of the anticommons258. The fragmentation of exclusivities distributes veto powers and makes recombination legally improbable or economically unviable, even if the resource remains technically available. This is not only an ethical critique, but a technical-institutional diagnosis. When an innovation depends on the articulation of multiple protected components, the creative process comes to require fragmented legal negotiation, with licenses, rights regularization (clearance) and constant risks of litigation. Structural friction favors large actors endowed with robust legal capacity and excludes smaller innovators, resulting in an ecosystem in which creativity remains subordinated to proprietary governance and to control over what has already been created.

The thesis is anchored in examples that make explicit the social materiality of this blocking, citing the “disaster of access to vaccines during Covid-19” as evidence of the patent latifundium259 Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 36). Mention is also made of the attempt to patent the “one-click” mechanism as a deliberate gesture to prevent the diffusion of a basic organizational improvement Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 22-23). Such cases function as concrete demonstrations of a p. 240 single operative principle: when IP is mobilized to prevent the generalization of a technique, it ceases to reward creation and comes to administer deliberate scarcity in contexts in which diffusion would be socially desirable. The argumentative axis maintains, therefore, that the distinctive mark of rentier IP is not the recognition of the invention, but the strategic blocking of the learning and collective fertilization that the invention could generate.

The effect of rentier IP is not exhausted in the binary turnstile of “having” or “not having” access. When exclusivity multiplies and overlaps in layers, it ceases to function only as a gate and comes to operate as an engineering of friction. It distributes veto powers, makes recombinations more expensive and transforms incremental innovation into permanent negotiation. The consequence is competitive, by creating barriers to entry and favoring incumbents, and also distributive, by reproducing the technological gap as dependence.

The notion of authorization points becomes particularly visible in IOCs because enjoyment is not sustained as a unit exhaustible upon delivery, but as insertion in a governed process. In this process, availability, compatibility and continuity depend on external controls under the supplier's dominion Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 40; p. 51-52). Within the scope adopted here, this process manifests itself through the overlapping of layers of control bearing on the same experience. One may cite, for example, software and code, content protected by copyright, distinctive signs, design and contractual license regimes, so that each layer can operate as a basis for restricting, modulating or charging for uses and circulations.

The architecture of cumulative permissions comes close to the diagnosis that tangles of legal restrictions and transaction costs can produce underuse and blockages through an excess of vetoes, to the detriment of recombination and subsequent innovation Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 44; p. 47), and helps to explain why economic activity comes to depend on persistent legal mediations even in trivial operations of production and circulation Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 45-46). For consumption, the relevant effect is that the sign of “product” can conceal an enjoyment governed by partial authorizations, reinforcing the need to make verifiable, in terms of auditability, the restrictions actually bearing on the promised utility when governance by titles, licenses and access controls degrades the functional value of the experience.

The theoretical consequence is an inversion. What appears as a product in commercial discourse, in practice, comes to function as a bundle of fragmented, revocable and conditional authorizations. When the experience depends on successive keys (accounts, licenses, DRMs, authentications, terms, sanctions etc.), the market ceases to revolve around property and p. 241 circulation and comes to revolve around governance and control. Thus, competition is no longer only a contest over supply, it becomes a contest over who can impose the most effective package of dependencies without losing social legitimacy.

It is precisely at this point that the discussion ceases to be whether we are “against or in favor of intellectual property” and becomes “how can we calibrate intellectual property teleologically”. Because, if the real economic function of enclosure is to reinstall scarcity where technique would dissolve price, the relevant legal problem becomes measuring up to what point exclusivity is still an incentive, and from what moment it becomes a structural toll. The question is not moral; it is one of institutional design, especially when the platform already dominates access and manages to convert any legal friction into competitive advantage.

Dowbor's propositional axis emerges when he gathers explicit alternatives for confronting this regime. Socializing innovation and preserving access does not depend on denying the remuneration of creative labor, but on disarticulating the normative equivalence between payment and blocking. For this reason, he reaffirms public access to knowledge as a structuring vector, by maintaining that knowledge generated with public resources must be socially accessible and serve the common good, and that access to knowledge and connectivity today constitutes a fundamental human right Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 62). In the same vein, he asserts that copyright must be limited, since the objective of copyright is to ensure reasonable remuneration for the author, and not to guarantee permanent rent for the intermediary, which is why he proposes a temporal limitation, even on the order of five years Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 43).

Exclusivity, in this framework, is not justified as prolonged retention, but as a functional and temporary measure. Intermediaries and chains of exploitation remain remunerable, but capture must occur through mechanisms compatible with circulation and access, and not through permanent tolls Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 64). The legal meaning of the inflection is to break the naturalization of enclosure as an ordinary technique of monetization. It is a matter of remunerating without hindering260 and financing without blocking, rebuilding the balance between incentive and circulation in a regime marked by the non-rivalry of knowledge.

p. 242 Dowbor does not offer a simplistic critique of intellectual property. On the contrary, he recognizes the historical legitimacy of the incentive, understood as privileged remuneration of the inventor or author, so as to stimulate scientific and cultural advances261 Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 36). The turning point, however, occurs when the instrument loses social validity by turning into rentier charging for access. In this key, patents and copyrights cease to operate predominantly as a temporary technique of stimulus and come to function as a mechanism of blocking and toll on knowledge, including when effective ownership shifts to intermediaries and economic groups Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 36; p. 43; p. 46).

The critique, therefore, does not deny IP, but brings its legitimacy back to its purposive foundation. Intellectual property rights are not based on a natural right of property, but on their potential to stimulate future creativity Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 46). It follows that the hypertrophy of duration and scope produces teleological maladjustment and requires proportionality control when exclusivity becomes autonomous from the incentive and comes to operate as a fence Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 43). Within the present scope, this brings back the need to define criteria of loyalty, material transparency and preservation of the functional minimum legitimately expected in consumption environments mediated by platforms.

At this point, it becomes evident that the competitive mutation of immaterial markets does not stem from a “natural evolution” of the market, but from the convergence between technical infrastructure and legal infrastructure. The platform concentrates the mediation of access, the contract disciplines everyday use, and intellectual property provides the grammar of blocking that transforms circulation into exception and authorization into rule. The result is a regime in which market power is measured less by the capacity to produce goods and more by the capacity to p. 243 administer gates, conditionalities and revocations, that is, to govern consumption as a permanently controllable process.

Having established the competitive mutation and the role of intellectual property as a component of enclosure coupled with infrastructure, it becomes possible to understand the reasons why, in continuous immaterial objects, the stability of value and the contestability of changes cease to be market noise and become part of the core of the legal problem under discussion.

Dowbor recognizes the historical justification of intellectual protection as a technique of incentive — privileged remuneration of the inventor and the author to stimulate scientific and cultural advances —, but maintains that, in the contemporary configuration, patents and copyrights tend to operate as a rentier mechanism of charging for access and blocking the diffusion of knowledge Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 36). The author exemplifies the dysfunction by the temporal inadequacy of the patent in the face of the technological pace, pointing to the experience of the Covid-19 pandemic as a case of socially disastrous restriction of access, and by the expansion of copyright with economic concentration in publishers, not in authors, illustrating with the horizon of open access to the works of Paulo Freire only in 2067 Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 36). In this diagnosis, the “war to hinder access” becomes a central axis of the knowledge economy, while mechanisms of openness (Open Access, Creative Commons and the like) figure as an institutional alternative capable of radically expanding worldwide access to knowledge without depending on retention as the ordinary form of remuneration Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 36). ↩
