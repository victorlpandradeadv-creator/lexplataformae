4.2.5 The consumer-citizen under the Lex Plataformae

See this section in the PDF

p. 379

When operating, a Lex Plataformae does not reorganize only a one-off contractual exchange. It institutes a regime of participation. When access, permanence, visibility, reputation and symbolic circulation depend on internal criteria and on sanctions executed by design, the enjoyment of the immaterial object ceases to be realized as an isolated delivery and comes to exist as a position sustained in the ecosystem, subject to unilateral reconfigurations presented as technical normality.

The subject of this relationship is not the abstract constitutional citizen, nor the neutral acquirer of an isolated exchange. It is a participant whose enjoyment depends on internal conditions of belonging and continuity, exposed to decisions that operate from within the ecosystem, by rule and by enforcement. Normativity qualifies positions and makes relationships operable.

Described as practical normativity in operation, the Lex Plataformae does not allow the norm to be treated as a simple “portrait” incapable of ordering relationships. In Grau, law appears as part of the very functioning of the social: “Law […] is not a simple representation of social reality, external to it, but rather a functional level of the social whole. Thus, as a level of reality itself, it is a constitutive element of the mode of social production” Grau(Grau, 2008, p. 19-20). From this interpretive key, private normativity can be treated as a constitutive component of the arrangement of the ecosystem, capable of assigning practical conditions of presence, visibility and continuity to participants, including in the form of restriction or exclusion.

The consumer-citizen is the subject who acquires IOCs whose utility is realized as functional permanence in an ecosystem, and not as the isolated delivery of a transferred good. He is a consumer because there is acquisition and a legitimate expectation of enjoyment. He is a “citizen” only in the p. 380 analytical sense that his presence in the environment depends on conditions of belonging and continuity set by private governance, with collective effects on recognition and symbolic circulation. Citizenship here does not concern nationality, sovereignty or classic constitutional status. It designates practical conditions of participation and permanence in an environment governed by internal rules, architecture and enforcement, in which the unilateral alteration of these conditions reconfigures, in practice, the very object enjoyed. With this, the relevant vulnerability is not exhausted in contractual informational asymmetry. It appears as dependence on a private order that administers, by design and by sanction, the conditions of access, expression, commerce and permanence, producing effects that are not neutralized by simple individual choice. This characterization blocks the generic constitutionalization of the dispute and avoids the statist leap.

To delimit what is at stake without inflating the argument, the concept of digital citizenship is mobilized as a parameter, and not as a primer398. Ribble and Bailey (2007, p. 11) maintain that digital citizenship should not be treated as a rigid set of rules, but as a conceptual lens for understanding the challenges faced by users of technology. Instead of fixing ideas as if they were definitive, the authors defend a guiding approach, aimed at offering criteria and modes of action that help the subject navigate a digital environment that is in constant change. Their formulation makes it possible to treat digital citizenship as an operational grid of what can be governed in the connected environment, without turning the discussion into user blame.

The nine dimensions proposed by Ribble and Bailey (2007, p. 10) function as a minimal map of what makes up participation in the digital realm when enjoyment depends on stable insertion in the ecosystem. They are: digital access (digital access), digital commerce (digital commerce), digital communication (digital communication), digital literacy (digital literacy), digital etiquette (digital etiquette), digital law (digital law), digital rights and responsibilities (digital rights and responsibilities), digital health and wellness (digital health and wellness) and digital p. 381 security (digital security). In environments governed by Lex Plataformae, the dimensions cease to appear as competences of the subject and come to be affected by internal rules, architecture, curation and enforcement, becoming governed variables of enjoyment.

Access and literacy, in this framing, are neither a generic inclusion agenda nor a user education program. They are procedural preconditions of contestability within the ecosystem. When enjoyment depends on permanence and recognition, the relevant access is effective access to the functions that make it possible to understand the environment's regime of government, including internal rules, instruments of contestation, decision trails and practical consequences of sanctions or reclassifications. Along the same axis, literacy delimits how far the environment makes it possible to recognize what is happening, why it is happening and how to react, above all when criteria of visibility and of enforcement operate in an opaque or variable way. Without this floor, the informational asymmetry typical of consumption becomes an asymmetry of position. The rule change, the downgrading of reach or the suspension do not even become a verifiable and disputable object within the system itself.

When classifications, visibility criteria and eligibility standards operate without sufficient functional transparency, enjoyment turns into an administered result more than a contestable result. Cohen (2019, p. 43) describes this condition when stating that the operational secrecy typical of the platform environment prevents buyers of these services from easily monitoring the quality of what they have acquired and makes it harder, more broadly, for users to identify whether the company engages in undisclosed forms of preferential placement. The legal-institutional effect is not to impute fault to the supposedly uninformed user, but to recognize that contestability is compressed by design. Vulnerability ceases to be an individual deficit and comes to take the form of administered dependence.

Participating requires executable conditions of speech and conduct. Communication and etiquette function as governable dimensions, because they are mediated by infrastructure and by private rule that distributes reach, organizes attention and applies sanctions through moderation, filters, ranking and metrics. On platforms, communication is not mere exchange between individuals. It is a practice mediated by infrastructure and by private rule that distributes reach, organizes attention and ranks relevance through interface design, moderation, filters, ranking and engagement metrics. What appears as “speech” is speech under technical and normative conditions that operate at the same time as a legitimate form of participation and as an instrument of sanction. Etiquette, in the same sense, is not a manual of good manners. It is the p. 382 set of standards that the environment can convert into operational requirements of permanence and visibility, implemented by routines of detection, containment and penalization.

Ribble and Bailey capture the sociotechnical core of this dimension when noting that contemporary forms of mediated communication have created a new social structure that governs who interacts, in what way and at what moment Bailey(Ribble; Bailey, 2007, p. 19). The normative correlate appears when they define digital etiquette as the standards of conduct expected by other users of digital technologies399 Bailey(Ribble; Bailey, 2007, p. 10). Read within this framework, communication and etiquette cease to be merely diffuse cultural traits and come to operate as governable dimensions of participation, because they structure expectations, define limits of tolerance and delimit the legitimate form of presence in the environment. In these ecosystems, the platform converts patterns of interaction and conduct into technically executable parameters of ordering and sanction, both through explicit rules and through mechanisms of moderation, ranking and restriction of reach. The impact is direct on the enjoyment position of the consumer-citizen, which comes to depend on signals of conformity, reputation and acceptability produced and applied within the infrastructure itself, with effects on access, visibility, permanence and opportunities for participation.

Under the Lex Plataformae, the “law” does not operate as an external backdrop. It manifests itself as an internal rule that typifies conduct, defines infractions and executes consequences in the ecosystem's infrastructure. There is a structural mismatch. The platform demands conformity as a condition of participation and permanence, but concentrates the power to formulate the normative content, interpret its application and impose sanctions with low density of reasoning and low openness to contestation. In this key, the consumerist lens needs to reach a minimum rationality of enforcement and effective avenues of contestation, lest responsibility function as a technique of subjection.

Ribble and Bailey's own framing helps keep the focus away from a catalog of crimes and close to the social grammar of acceptability. The authors indicate that respecting laws related to digital technology is only one aspect of digital citizenship, without exhausting it Bailey(Ribble; Bailey, 2007, p. 131). The relevant core, for the reading mobilized here, lies in understanding the social reasons for adhering to these rules, critically assessing what is acceptable and considering how actions affect others in society Bailey(Ribble; Bailey, 2007, p. 131).

p. 383 In platform ecosystems, this framework fits because internal standards of conduct, moderation and sanction cease to be a contingent detail of use and come to operate as a condition of permanence under enforcement. Participation becomes a position conditioned by normative expectations that are translated into operational criteria, applied at scale and with effects that reach access, visibility and continuity of enjoyment. Thus, the debate shifts from the isolated unlawful act to the regime of acceptability that the infrastructure itself institutes, in which the platform defines the threshold of the tolerable, administers deviation and turns adherence to patterns of interaction into a requirement of eligibility.

In the same model, rights and responsibilities describe institutional belonging, and not abstract values. Ribble and Bailey treat digital citizenship as a condition of full membership, in which each user must have rights equitably ensured, while at the same time assuming responsibilities and agreeing to live according to parameters that are mutually agreed upon by the participants Bailey(Ribble; Bailey, 2007, p. 29). These limits may take the form of legal rules, regulations or acceptable use policies, and the legitimacy of the arrangement depends, ideally, on the cooperative construction of a framework of appropriate use, at the risk of rules being imposed from the outside in Bailey(Ribble; Bailey, 2007, p. 29).

When these parameters are produced, interpreted and executed by private governance, the legally sensitive point is not to demand “good conduct” from the user. It is to delimit what can count as a legitimate parameter and which minimum guarantees make this demand compatible with the institutional asymmetry of the environment.

Digital commerce, finally, is not merely the instant of the transaction. It functions as entry into and maintenance of an enjoyment position in an administered ecosystem, in which the value of the IOC depends on access, visibility, communicability, reputation and continuity. Buying, subscribing to, activating or linking a service of this kind is not equivalent to acquiring an isolated good, but to adhering to a private order that defines conditions of use, eligibility and permanence and can reconfigure these conditions by rule, design and enforcement. For this reason, the typical risk captured by the lens of the CDC is not exhausted in price, quantity, quality or defect in the classic sense. The structural point is the unilateral reconfiguration of the mode of enjoyment and of the criteria that sustain the acquired position, including relevant changes of rules, access standards, visibility parameters, forms of ranking and sanction routines. In IOCs, the transaction buys governed participation, and the degradation of this participation may amount, in practice, to the loss of the enjoyed object itself.

Ribble and Bailey's formulation makes it possible to frame this dimension as mediated economic belonging, without any moralism and without reducing the discussion to individual virtues. p. 384 In stating that anyone who works, plays or buys items online is part not only of a digital community, but also of an economic community Bailey(Ribble; Bailey, 2007, p. 17), the authors shift the gaze to the fact that presence in these environments involves, simultaneously, social interaction and insertion in circuits of value. For this framing, the point is to consolidate that the economic dimension is part of the very conditions of participation in the ecosystem, because enjoyment does not occur in a normative vacuum, but in a space of conditioned access, in which the platform defines the practical terms of permanence and circulation.

In IOCs, this dimension tends to materialize as maintenance of an enjoyment position in an administered environment, since internal rules, architecture and enforcement reconfigure not only the experience, but also the scope of what it is possible to do, see and continue doing. Economic belonging, in this sense, is not merely the making of a purchase, but dependence on an internal regime that administers eligibility, distributes visibility and decides the continuity of the bond. The consequence is that the legal discussion is not limited to the price or the content delivered, since it involves the daily governance of access and permanence, in which metrics, reputation, conduct and conformity with internal standards can directly influence the possibility of continuing to enjoy what was acquired.

The dimensions of health and wellness and of security complete the picture because, in platform ecosystems, they do not describe merely “risks of use”, but structural conditions of permanence in an environment governed by metrics of performance, comparability and exposure. Enjoyment depends on continuous and recognizable presence, and this presence is stabilized by technical incentives of repetition, by regimes of visibility and by mechanisms of sanction that modulate attention, conduct and self-restraint. In this key, wellness is an institutional effect of an architecture that produces intensity of engagement, pressure to update and functional risk of loss of access or reputational degradation. Likewise, security should not be read as mere individual prudence, but as self-protection in an environment in which informational asymmetry is the rule and in which the platform itself defines the standard of what counts as safe conduct, acceptable risk and incident response. In legal terms, this shifts the center of the problem to the configuration of risk by the supplier, through the design of flows, internal policies, authentication standards and routines of verification and contestation. The consequence is that integrity and identity cease to operate as strictly personal attributes and come to be administered as practical requirements of credibility and permanence.

The definitions proposed by Ribble and Bailey (2007) make it possible to sustain this framing with low noise, because they delimit “health and wellness” as the set of elements of physical and psychological p. 385 well-being linked to the use of digital technologies Bailey(Ribble; Bailey, 2007, p. 31). In the same movement, the authors treat “security” as a practice of self-protection that is not exhausted in the individual. Thus, protecting equipment and data is also a duty with community effects, and digital security includes preventing harm to oneself and to others in the face of potentially harmful external influences, going beyond mere care for the hardware Bailey(Ribble; Bailey, 2007, p. 33–34). Read under the Lex Plataformae, these dimensions cease to be advice to the user and come to operate as points at which the ecosystem administers risk, permanence and credibility.

Once the Lex Plataformae has been described as practical normativity in operation, the subject of immaterial consumption cannot be reduced to an acquirer. Enjoyment depends on conditions of access, permanence, visibility, communication and reputation administered by internal rules and by enforcement. The appropriate figure is that of the consumer-citizen, consumer because they acquire and expect enjoyment and “citizen” in the analytical sense that they participate under private normative conditions that organize belonging and continuity, with collective effects on recognition and symbolic circulation.

In this framework, Ribble and Bailey provide a minimal grid for naming what is at stake in this participation without moralizing individual competences. When these dimensions are governed by private architecture, curation and sanction, they become institutional variables of the product itself and, therefore, a matter of structural vulnerability. Hence the need for reinforced duties of the supplier compatible with the power exercised, aimed at making controllable rule changes, reconfigurations of access and enforcement measures that degrade the acquired enjoyment position.

The passage from consumption to governed participation shifts the problem to a plane in which vulnerability ceases to be a mere informational deficit and comes to take on institutional density, because enjoyment depends on conditions whose meaning and application take place within the ecosystem. It is this kind of mutation, when technical changes and market arrangements alter the mechanics of harm and render usual categories of protection insufficient, that authorizes legal reconstruction400.

When technical mediation converts interaction into screening and permanence into performance, coercion tends to operate as administered freedom. Han describes this p. 386 mechanism when maintaining that the digital society of control makes intensive use of freedom, precisely because it depends on voluntary self-exposure and self-transparency to function401 Han(Han, 2017, p. [13-14]). In this framework, exclusion and degradation of reach cease to be accidents and come to figure as a functional risk of a regime of governed participation, in which permanence depends on continuous alignment with operational and reputational standards.

In view of this, the next step does not require rhetorically broadening the critique nor nationalizing the platform. The necessary movement is to convert the diagnosis into operational normative criteria that bear precisely on the points at which private power reconfigures access, permanence, communication and sanction. For this reason, auditable and controllable parameters will be formulated below, with reinforced duties compatible with the asymmetry, such as qualified transparency, minimum stability of enjoyment conditions, restoration in the face of degrading changes or sanctions, open auditability and functional equivalence, articulated with the axes of integrity and identity.
