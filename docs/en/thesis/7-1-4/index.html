7.1.4 On digital citizenship and limits to the private power of platforms

See this section in the PDF

  • In practice · Digital citizenship and limits

p. 752

The incidence of the regime of the Immaterial Object of Consumption reveals, in the end, an institutional fact that cannot be treated as peripheral. In platform ecosystems, supply no longer falls only on economically appreciable utilities in the narrow sense. It increasingly bears on practical conditions of participation, visibility, reputation, permanence and contestation in environments that structure relevant portions of contemporary social life. This does not mean dissolving the consumer relationship into an abstract political category, nor replacing dogmatic analysis with grandiose sociological rhetoric. It means recognizing that, in long-lasting immaterial objects of consumption, governed enjoyment may be part of processes of biographical continuity, relational insertion and symbolic identity, so that unilateral sanctions, erasures, reconfigurations and degradations may reach dimensions of the experience that exceed what is residually patrimonial.

This point is especially important because it prevents an impoverished reading of the conflict. When enjoyment is long, cumulative and socially situated, the object consumed ceases to be merely a utility detachable from the user's trajectory. It may become an anchoring point of history, belonging, reputation, memory of use and recognition before third parties. In these cases, functional loss is not limited to a momentary unavailability of access nor to a one-off economic frustration. It may compromise continuity of presence, stability of bonds, intelligibility of the consumer's own trajectory in the environment and preservation of symbolic elements that, although economically mediated, take on practical existential p. 753 relevance. This is not a matter of inflating any digital discomfort to the condition of an identity drama. It is a matter of recognizing that certain environments of governed enjoyment operate on concrete dimensions of personal integrity in a functional sense.

The formulation of these limits, however, requires conceptual sobriety. This is not a matter of equating platform with State, nor of converting the CDC into a parallel constitution of digital life. This comparison, besides being crude, tends to produce more smoke than light. The premise is a different one, and far more defensible. Functional public relevance does not produce equivalence to the State, but neither does it authorize treating the platform as an institutionally neutral supplier. When private powers administer access, permanence, visibility, reputation and contestation in environments structurally relevant to social participation, the legal order already has legal matrices of limitation. The Constitution is one of them. The CDC is another, operating in reconciliation with the social function of property, with the discipline of intellectual property and with the general limits on the exercise of private positions in a constitutionally oriented economic order.

Under this key, the CDC appears with greater density than that of a mere microsystem devoted to the discipline of one-off patrimonial exchanges. In environments of governed participation, it also functions as one of the legal matrices for limiting private power, precisely because the supply regime comes to organize concrete conditions of presence, interaction, permanence and contestation. This does not alter its nature nor transform it into an instrument of total publicization of the consumer relationship. It only makes explicit its aptitude to control, in a constitutionally compatible key, forms of supply that are no longer exhausted in the simple circulation of goods or in the episodic provision of services, but structure relevant portions of the consumer's social experience.

This conclusion matters because it puts the category of the IOC back in its proper institutional reach. Control of the immaterial object of consumption is neither a cultural gesture nor a theoretical whim, nor is it reduced to an attempt to update the CDC terminologically. It is a dogmatically necessary response to environments that tend to produce opacity, mutability and private concentration of power as normal operating conditions. In them, the precariousness of enjoyment cannot be naturalized by the mere invocation of contract, license, innovation or the global scale of the service. The function of the regime is to reintroduce institutional controllability where the supplier seeks to pass off contingency as the user's inevitable destiny.

This reintroduction of controllability is particularly relevant when the supplier presents itself as a mere intermediary, as neutral technology or as a global provider whose private normativity would purport to replace, by self-reference, the local legal vocabulary. p. 754 In each of these figures there is a recurring attempt to shift the discussion outside the field of attribution. The intermediary will say that it merely connects. Neutral technology will say that it merely executes. The global provider will say that it merely operates according to its own universal terms. The IOC regime rejects these self-descriptions as a sufficient framing criterion. What matters is not how the platform names itself, but how it organizes access, permanence, visibility, contestation and enjoyment. Whenever this organization produces legal relevance under the discipline of supply, there will be room for the incidence of limits, duties and consequences.

This is also why the digital citizenship implied here should not be read as a loose, celebratory category or one disconnected from dogmatics. It appears as a functional problem of participation under private governance. The point is not to assert rights in the abstract before an idealized public sphere, but to recognize that, in certain ecosystems, the consumption experience directly touches the practical conditions of presence and agency of the individual. Digital citizenship, in this context, does not replace the IOC regime. It helps to illuminate why certain conflicts of governed enjoyment cannot be treated as mere low-density contractual annoyances. When the platform administers the entry doors, the criteria of permanence, the mechanisms of visibility and the means of contestation, it comes to affect dimensions of practical autonomy that Law cannot abandon to pure corporate self-legitimation.

The strategic consequence of this for typological application is clear. From this point on, the incidence of the IOC regime becomes recognizable in different families of platform without depending on the cultural sensitivity of the reader or the judge to perceive, intuitively, the seriousness of the environment. The argument no longer needs emotional adherence to the social importance of a given service and comes to operate through criteria of supply, governance and limitation of private power. This strengthens the transposability of the regime. Even when the platform presents itself under discourses of neutrality, innovation, globality or mere intermediation, the analysis can bring the case back to what really matters: who organizes enjoyment, who administers its intelligibility, who controls its permanence and who defines the conditions of its contestation.

The legal dispute in governed ecosystems is already born traversed by regimes of perception administered by the supplier itself. The consumer does not enter the conflict on neutral ground. They enter an environment whose language, memory, visibility and possibilities of reaction were previously designed by the party that also concentrates economic, technical and normative power over the relationship. The dogmatic response, therefore, needs to recover agency, p. 755 verifiability and controllability. Not to freeze the service, nor to deny the complexity of the environment, but to prevent precariousness, opacity and asymmetric mutability from being accepted as the natural state of things. Because when private power begins to govern participation as if it were weather, and not an institutionally controllable choice, Law is already far too late.
