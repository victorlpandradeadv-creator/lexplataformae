3.2.6 Regimes of value, production of meaning and the attention economy on platforms

See this section in the PDF

p. 247

So far, the analysis has established that the platform does not act as a simple “contractual model” aimed at a one-off purchase, since it is a mode of production that organizes enjoyment as administered permanence. Thus, extraction is not concentrated in the act of sale, since it tends to stabilize as recurrent rent through access and p. 248 tolls, supported by artificial scarcity, manufactured risk, competition by enclosure and architectural teleology, with intellectual property operating as a technology for blocking and closing off the field. In this arrangement, economic power does not derive only from clauses or prices, but from the continuous governance of the conditions of existence of the object and of the consumer bond itself, keeping the consumer bound to a territory in which utility, rarity and value can be reprogrammed by unilateral decision presented as technical routine.

If extraction under platforms depends on permanence and on governance of access, it needs to govern the field in which this permanence is decided in practice, that of attention and signification. The platform economy is sustained by regulating what gains salience and what becomes socially desirable, because it is on this plane that it is decided what appears as valuable, what seems inevitable, what deserves time and what yields belonging. The key is neither price nor initial delivery, but the capacity to organize the horizon of what counts as object, as choice and as value.

In the classical market, economic discipline operates above all through price variation and competitive pressure, the axis on which access, substitution and preference are resolved. On platforms, discipline is reconfigured, since everyday experience is mediated by discovery devices and visibility metrics that not only organize the offer, but construct the field of what seems to exist and to matter. Power is not limited to charging or prohibiting, since it is exercised by defining what is seen, what is comparable, what is desirable and what gains social traction, pushing certain alternatives toward “naturalness” and others toward practical invisibility. Economy and semiotics cease to be parallel layers, because regulating perception, comparison and visibility is regulating the decision field itself.

The utility of a good is not reduced to physical use, because of its function as an infrastructure of classification and communication262. Goods stabilize hierarchies, boundaries of p. 249 belonging and the intelligibility of the social world by providing marking services capable of organizing calendars, estimating relations and sustaining reciprocities Isherwood(Douglas; Isherwood, 1996, p. xxii; p. 40-41). From this perspective, the relevance lies in offering a vocabulary for describing the immaterial object of consumption as something whose social utility depends on social legibility and belonging within an administered ecosystem, which allows the exit cost to be read not only as patrimonial loss, but as a risk of isolation and weakened bonds.

The central consequence of this approach is that meaning does not reside in an isolated item, but in the relational grammar of the whole. Goods operate as the hardware and software of an informational system in which meaning emerges from the relations263 between things, and not from an essence stuck to each object Isherwood(Douglas; Isherwood, 1996, p. 49). In platform environments, this gives the problem operational precision. Regulating curation and windows of circulation is equivalent to regulating the grammar by which value and belonging are expressed, because it alters the relations that make the collection “say” something. When the good is inseparable from its visibility, comparability and recognition, the ecosystem that administers the conditions of appearance comes to administer the sign together with enjoyment. What is delivered is not just an item. The conditions of language that make this item exist as a socially legible difference within this ecosystem are maintained, or reordered.

Along the same line, “attention economy” ceases to be a metaphor. The decisive scarce resource is not the technical availability of the good, but the user's exposure time. Retention provides volume for observation, profiling, continuous testing of stimuli and calibration of triggers. It is this predictability that stabilizes extraction through subscription, pass, “unmissable event”, rotation of offer and successive tolls. Without captured and p. 250 governed attention, recurrent rent loses support, because access ceases to operate as a maintenance fee for one's place and tends to return to the appearance of an episodic purchase.

This transition calls for a language264 capable of treating value as a result of situated recognition, and not as an intrinsic attribute. In this key, circulation creates value and the link between exchange and value is political in a broad sense. The consequence is that the relevant question ceases to be “how much does it cost” and becomes “which frames make something appear as desirable, comparable and rare”, because value is not inherent in the object, but a judgment265 produced by subjects and endorsed by a real or imagined exchange Appadurai(Appadurai, 1986, p. 3-4). For this reason, the author prefers to speak of regimes of value, whose coherence266 may vary according to the situation and according to the commodity, without requiring full cultural consensus to operate Appadurai(Appadurai, 1986, p. 14-15).

p. 251 The notion of “politics of value”267 makes explicit the field of power that sustains these regimes. Ordinary exchange depends on agreements about what is desirable and also about who can exercise effective demand and under what circumstances Appadurai(Appadurai, 1986, p. 57). On platforms, this link thickens, because gatekeeping does not bear only on price or contract. It bears on the conditions of visibility that make a sign desirable and a good demandable, converting control of circulation into control of value. If value is an effect of situated recognition, administering catalog, rarity and rules of visibility (curation, windows, criteria) is administering the practical conditions of value itself.

This point becomes even more incisive when consumption is treated as a mechanism of belonging. Douglas and Isherwood (1996, p. xxiv) describe goods as devices of alignment and boundary. Social life is organized by signs of adherence and exclusion, and goods function as public marks that raise fences against the unwanted, consolidating solidarities and demarcating “keep out” signs. In this framework, the correct measure of poverty is not possession, but social involvement, because participation in the informational circuit of consumption determines access to support, predictability and capacity to act Isherwood(Douglas; Isherwood, 1996, p. xiv; p. xxii). Being out of reach of this system is not a subjective discomfort. It is a risk of practical silencing268, reduction of voice and of future options, with direct impact on the very rationality of choice Isherwood(Douglas; Isherwood, 1996, p. 63-65).

p. 252 From this follows a functional thesis for the immaterial object of consumption. When the good depends on appearing, being recognized and circulating in order to “be worth” something, the governance of belonging is converted into a technique of permanence. Rentierism in the immaterial is only sustained when technical abundance is translated into social scarcity. The platform installs a regime of comparison of rarity, status and opportunity, which makes access seem a condition of the existence of value, and not a whim of the gatekeeper. Without this regime, charging for access appears as irritation and permanence loses meaning. With it, the toll becomes a maintenance fee for one's place, because what is lost on leaving is not only functional utility, but the very social intelligibility of the collection and of the signs it emits.

In terms of the structure of the object, this reconnects the category of the Immaterial Object of Consumption to its semiotic condition of existence. The IOC does not behave as a “good” only through an isolated functional utility, but as a functional and semiotic bundle, in which enjoyment depends on operations of public legibility and collective recognition. Thus, governing signs, regimes of appearance and collective recognition is not a gesture external to supply, but a way of governing the object itself and its utility over time, because it is in this field that what “counts” as relevant difference, socially perceived rarity and belonging is formed.

The discussion gains an additional tool for describing mutations of value over time without resorting to morality or psychology. In this sense, the perspective of the culturally informed economic p. 253 biography269 treats270 the object as a culturally constructed entity, endowed with specific meanings and classified and reclassified into socially constituted categories Kopytoff(Kopytoff, 1986, p. 68). The fact that an object is bought or exchanged says nothing about its later status, since it can be withdrawn from the sphere of exchange and still retain commodity potential and latent exchange value271 Kopytoff(Kopytoff, 1986, p. 75-76).

For the immaterial object of consumption, this describes precisely why reworks, relaunches, trivializations and repositionings alter value and status without the item “disappearing”. The platform operates as a practical institution of singularization, asserting power through the competence to reclassify, that is, to insist on the right to singularize272 a class of objects and to control public institutions273 of singularization (Kopytoff, 1986, p. p. 254 73; p. 81). On platforms, this singularization takes the form of catalog retirement, changes in rarity, restrictions on obtaining items, event-based repositioning, recoding by meta and rearrangement of display criteria. The user's nominal ownership is maintained, but the cultural biography of the item is materially reordered, and it is this biography that sustains social utility, prestige and equivalence between promise and delivery.

When the efficient risk is losing timing, status and belonging, the discipline of presence installs itself as a technique of governing time. Absence ceases to be a mere interruption and comes to produce measurable decline, progressive erasure and practical discontinuity, making permanence the rational way of avoiding loss. The risk is semiotic and temporal by structure. It is semiotic because it depends on the social meaning of what is lost, and temporal because it functions through windows, calendars, cycles and rhythms that convert delay into objective disadvantage even when the immaterial object remains technically accessible.

At this point, consumption reveals itself as ritual274 in a technical sense, a mechanism that fixes public meanings and stabilizes classifications. Douglas and Isherwood (1996, p. 43) formulate this by treating goods as ritual adjuncts and consumption as a ritual process whose function is to produce meaning in the unstable flow of events, containing the drift of meanings through visible public conventions. Consumption makes judgments firm and visible in the continuous classification of persons and events, which allows demand to be understood as a social, relational and active phenomenon, and not as a private and atomic act (Douglas; Isherwood, p. 255 1996, p. 45; p. viii-ix). This definition also connects rite and bond operationally by treating goods as providers of marking services in consumption rituals, capable of drawing a cartography of social integration through the standardized flow of goods Isherwood(Douglas; Isherwood, 1996, p. xxii).

In platform ecosystems, routines such as daily login, missions, limited-time events, seasons and passes can be read as operationalized ritualization. Attendance is organized as a condition of participation in a public system of marking in which value, reputation and belonging are continually confirmed. The typical punishment need not present itself as a direct sanction, because the administered rite already produces the effect of loss of position and loss of voice. Absence is equivalent to being outside the informational system that allows one to hear and be heard, with harm to the very capacity of choice Isherwood(Douglas; Isherwood, 1996, p. 63-65).

Symbolic charging emerges when consumption operates as a social obligation coupled to the bond. It is a system of reciprocal rituals that implies expenditures to mark occasions, guests and hosts, or the community in general. When consumption is the “normal” mark of friendship, falling out of step with the rhythm of the circuit produces weakened access to information and support, surprise in the face of crises and less readiness to deal with the situation Isherwood(Douglas; Isherwood, 1996, p. xxii).

On platforms, the question paves the way for the discussion of the identity cost of exit. Permanence is not sustained only by money already spent, but because withdrawal implies discontinuity of biography and loss of social legibility. Repertoire, history, network, collection and public signs accumulated as proof of trajectory and belonging are lost. The platform transforms invested time into a backing of socially objectified identity by organizing these elements as verifiable symbolic capital.

This circuit, however, requires a constant renewal of meaning275. Cultural meaning flows incessantly from the culturally constituted world to goods and from goods p. 256 to the consumer, through instruments of transfer and consumption rituals McCracken(McCracken, 1986, p. 71). Advertising works as a conduit through which meaning is continually poured into goods, informing the public of the current stock of available meanings and operating as a lexicon276 of prevailing cultural senses McCracken(McCracken, 1986, p. 75-76). Moreover, culture operates as lens and as blueprint277 of action, determining the coordinates of social action and specifying the behaviors and objects that enter it as legitimate references McCracken(McCracken, 1986, p. 72).

Renewal is not optional because part of meaning is perishable278. If certain symbolic attributes “spoil” over time, the consumer is led to draw meaning from p. 257 their possessions repeatedly, resorting to rituals that extract these special and perishable properties from possessions, making them come alive, even if for a brief interval, in the individual's experience McCracken(McCracken, 1986, p. 79). On platforms, this describes the pressure not to fall behind as a ritualized renewal of perishable signs. Updating one's repertoire becomes a discipline of presence, and the discipline of presence sustains the toll by converting the renewal of signals into a practical obligation of attendance under administered visibility.

From this perspective, control of access is not exhausted in price or contract when demand itself is organized before it presents itself as preference. In ecosystems governed by curation, ranking and windows of appearance, the relevant competition migrates to the dispute over control of discovery. Whoever controls the paths of visibility controls the practical formation of desire, since they decide what enters the horizon of comparison and what remains invisible. Power ceases to be merely selling, and becomes defining what appears as worthy of investment, which trajectories are rewarded, which signals become necessary for belonging and which paths of enjoyment impose themselves as the default route.

Here, Appadurai's contribution closes the mechanism by treating demand as the economic expression of the political logic of consumption and by insisting that consumption is social, relational and active. Demand279 is a socially regulated and generated impulse, and not an artifact of individual whims Appadurai(Appadurai, 1986, p. 31-32).

This point puts choice back in its place. The abstraction of the isolated rational individual is unworkable when millions share consumption and reciprocally transform the field of meanings. The intelligibility of the social world depends on visible markings and on public p. 258 categories fixed by conventions, and consumption is precisely the mechanism that makes judgments firm and visible by classifying people and events, requiring “complaisant” peers for public categories to be maintained or changed Isherwood(Douglas; Isherwood, 1996, p. viii-ix; p. 43; p. 45). On platforms, curation, metrics and windows take on this function of public fixation, converting visibility into performed consensus and consensus into support for the toll.

If the platform governs regimes of value and scripts of choice through rules of visibility and continuous updating of the desirable repertoire, it also governs the way in which the immaterial object is promised, perceived and enjoyed. The promise of the good includes expectations of social utility that depend on public visibility and collective validation in the ecosystem. Unilateral changes that reconfigure criteria of display, circulation, rarity and grammars of comparison are not neutral “cosmetics”, since they bear upon the semiotic core that makes up the social utility of the IOC and structures the consumer's decision. In this framework, governance of signification ceases to be a backdrop and becomes part of the governance of the good itself, since it alters the conditions under which the item exists as a relevant difference, as socially perceived rarity and as a public marker of position.

In this framing, what is usually perceived as individual preference comes to be described as a discipline of perception and permanence, operated by curation and regimes of appearance that define the horizon of the desirable and the grammar of belonging. In markets of immaterials, economic stability depends not only on price and clause, but on continued control of the field in which permanence is decided in practice, through attention and visibility.

The conversion of technical abundance into socially perceived scarcity is anchored in three coupled mechanisms. First, by validation through consensus, in which metrics, display and circulation produce performed recognition and make value “seem” natural. Second, by discipline of presence, in which the perishability of meaning and the timing of enjoyment turn the updating of repertoire into a practical obligation of attendance. And third, by an exit cost enlarged by reputational and belonging losses, such that the act of leaving the ecosystem implies not only losing utility, but losing social legibility and recognizable position. The result is an identity captivity, in which the toll stabilizes as a fee for maintaining one's place, not as the one-off price of an item.

The shift to the semiotic key is thus ripe. The mechanism described explains how visibility, updating and belonging stabilize recurring revenue and enlarge the exit cost. What remains is to advance, with greater granularity, to the concrete operations of interface, curation and circulation through which the platform produces meaning and discipline of p. 259 presence, and to the legal qualification of these arrangements when they cease to be ordinary management of the service and come to constitute abusive teleology, by reordering promise, enjoyment and the minimum preservation of legitimately expected value over time.

  1. This analytical perspective finds a structural, non-psychologizing formulation in The World of Goods: Towards an Anthropology of Consumption, by Mary Douglas and Baron Isherwood (1996), originally published in 1979. Instead of treating consumption as a moral caricature or as a sum of individual impulses, the work reinserts goods into the social process and describes consumption as language and as everyday ritual. The methodological inversion proposed by Douglas and Isherwood appears as an explicit command. The target is the impoverished reading of consumption as irrationality, excess or mere satisfaction of material needs. The central move consists in suspending, for long enough, the vocabulary of biological utility, in order to observe what goods do as operators of thought, classification and communication. The reference to Lévi-Strauss works as a theoretical framing, since, just as certain animals are “good to think” in the logic of totemism, commodities can also be “good to think” when treated as a medium of symbolic work. “Forget the idea of consumer irrationality. Forget that commodities are good for eating, clothing, and shelter; forget their usefulness and try instead the idea that commodities are good for thinking; treat them as a nonverbal medium for the human creative faculty” Isherwood(Douglas; Isherwood, 1996, p. 40-41). The conceptual consequence is significant, since goods cease to be read as things that merely “serve” and come to be understood as a nonverbal medium of cultural elaboration. This opens room for understanding consumption as everyday cognitive infrastructure, in which choosing, using and displaying goods is not moral noise in rationality, but one of the ways in which social categories become practicable. The “creative faculty”, in this register, is not reduced to artistic creativity, but designates the ordinary capacity to make the world intelligible through materialized signs. ↩
  2. Douglas and Isherwood go further by formulating the perspective of goods as an information system. The point is not merely that “objects communicate”, but that this communication is structural and relational, and depends on the whole. The isolated item is semantically incomplete, since meaning emerges from the reciprocal position among goods, as happens in a grammar or in a musical harmony. At this point, the authors consolidate the argument with two images that organize the reasoning, namely, the idea of a system that “monitors its own performance” and the analogy with music, in which the minimal unit is not the note, but the relation between notes. “The goods are both the hardware and the software, so to speak, of an information system whose principal concern is to monitor its own performance. […] The counterargument proposed here is that all goods carry meaning, but none by itself. […] The meaning is in the relations between all the goods, just as music is in the relations marked out by the sounds and not in any one note” Isherwood(Douglas; Isherwood, 1996, p. 49). This redefines what “value” is on the anthropological plane. Value comes to function as an effect of insertion in a series, and not as a physical attribute of the object. A good is “worth” something because it occupies an intelligible place in an arrangement of differences, complementarities and hierarchies. Consequently, any alteration in the arrangement, whether in what appears together, in what is comparable, in what is rare, in what is seen as a “peer”, modifies what the good “says”. The unit of analysis ceases to be the item and becomes the grammar of the collection. ↩
  3. In The Social Life of Things (1986), Arjun Appadurai proposes understanding the commodity as a situated and transitory condition, defined by the social arenas of exchange, and not as a fixed category restricted to capitalism. Titled Introduction: Commodities and the Politics of Value, the essay opens the volume edited by Appadurai, which gathers essays in anthropology and history aimed at shifting the gaze from exchange as abstraction to the things themselves in circulation, treating commodities as objects endowed with a “social life” and with trajectories that vary according to cultural and historical contexts. Instead of presupposing value as an inherent attribute of the object or as a simple mechanical effect of the market, the volume emphasizes that value is produced and stabilized in social regimes of recognition, circulation and classification. ↩
  4. Appadurai begins with an operation of conceptual cleansing. Before dealing with circulation, commodity and regimes, he redefines what is understood by value, precisely to prevent the analysis from falling back into an ontology of the object. The starting point is Simmel. Value is not an inherent property of the thing, but a situated judgment, formulated by subjects and made effective by conditions of demand and exchange. The economic object, therefore, does not possess absolute value as a mechanical effect of desire. It is demand, as the basis of a real or imagined exchange, that invests the thing with value. Next comes the decisive inversion. It is not utility or scarcity that explain exchange, but exchange that defines the practical parameters of utility and scarcity. “Value, for Simmel, is never an inherent property of objects, but is a judgment made about them by subjects. […] Thus, the economic object does not have an absolute value as a result of the demand for it, but the demand, as the basis of a real or imagined exchange, endows the object with value. It is exchange that sets the parameters of utility and scarcity, rather than the other way round, and exchange that is the source of value…” Appadurai(Appadurai, 1986, p. 3-4). With this shift, “commodity” ceases to be a moral or natural category and comes to designate a relational and historical condition. Value becomes intelligible as an effect of practices, expectations and arenas of exchange. The analysis of the economic, consequently, shifts to the frameworks that make exchange possible, that is, to mechanisms that stabilize judgments of value, instead of explaining value “from inside” the object. ↩
  5. Having set aside the idea of value as essence, Appadurai formulates a concept to face a recurring problem in economic anthropology. Exchanges occur even when there is no dense and shared cultural consensus about “what is worth how much”. Cross-cultural trade, exchanges under necessity and circulation between heterogeneous and asymmetrical groups require an analytical language that depends neither on cultural homogeneity nor on fully shared assumptions. In this context, Appadurai prefers to speak of regimes of value, not to presuppose a unified cultural system, but to describe variable degrees of evaluative coherence, sufficient to enable equivalences and hierarchies in diverse situations. The formulation is explicit: “I prefer to use the term regimes of value, which does not imply that every act of commodity exchange presupposes a complete cultural sharing of assumptions, but rather that the degree of value coherence may be highly variable from situation to situation, and from commodity to commodity. A regime of value, in this sense, is consistent with both very high and very low sharing of standards by the parties to a particular commodity exchange” Appadurai(Appadurai, 1986, p. 14-15). The concept offers a tool for seeing value as an operational grammar, composed of standards and criteria that need not be fully consensual in order to work, provided they produce enough practical coherence to guide equivalences, distinctions and disputes. Regime of value, thus, does not describe cultural harmony, but the capacity of an arena of exchange to make value “operate” under friction, asymmetry and disagreement. ↩
  6. In the conclusion, Appadurai names the structural link between value and exchange, which in everyday life tends to remain invisible, since custom takes on an appearance of neutrality. This link is politics, understood in a broad sense, as relations, assumptions and contests tied to power. Routine exchange only seems “natural” because it depends on agreements about the desirable, about what counts as a reasonable sacrifice in a transaction and, above all, about who is authorized to exercise effective demand in given circumstances. For this reason, value and exchange are not connected automatically by price or preference, since they are connected by an architecture of permissions, conventions and privileges that sustains what can circulate and with what efficacy. The passage is long and central: “Politics (in the broad sense of relations, assumptions, and contests pertaining to power) is what links value and exchange in the social life of commodities. In the mundane, day-to-day, small-scale exchanges of things in ordinary life, this fact is not visible, for exchange has the routine and conventionalized look of all customary behavior. But these many ordinary dealings would not be possible were it not for a broad set of agreements concerning what is desirable, what a reasonable ‘exchange of sacrifices’ comprises, and who is permitted to exercise what kind of effective demand in what circumstances. What is political about this process is not just the fact that it signifies and constitutes relations of privilege and social control. What is political about it is the constant tension between the existing frameworks (of price, bargaining, and so forth) and the tendency of commodities to breach these frameworks” Appadurai(Appadurai, 1986, p. 57). In this register, “politics of value” is not a normative addendum, but a description of the engine that makes economic life possible and unstable at the same time. The ordinary economy appears as a field of continuous tension between frames that attempt to stabilize exchange, such as price, bargaining and rules, and the tendency of the flows and disputes themselves to break such frames. Value thus becomes an effect dependent on socially situated agreements and on structures that authorize, block and modulate demand. ↩
  7. When, in the work, the authors reach the theme of exclusion, the tone hardens, because the argument depends on treating consumption as access to information and as a practical condition of insertion in networks of support, predictability and opportunity. Exclusion does not appear as a simple lack of goods, but as being out of reach of an informational flow in which one hears and is heard. This loss reconfigures the subject's possible future. The passage formulates this incisively, by directly connecting information, earning potential and “future choosing”. In the original: “To be in control of this sort of information may be vital for getting and keeping a high earning potential. To be outside its range altogether, for the individual who can neither hear it nor make his voice heard, is to risk being treated like a stone, trodden underfoot and kicked aside—a limit to future choosing and the exercise of rational choice. […] One way to keep off the slide from temporary unemployment to unemployability, which leads to chronic isolation, deviance for his children, even criminality, is to be more closely involved with people. And this takes money” Isherwood(Douglas; Isherwood, 1996, p. 63-65). What is at stake is a theory of the social cost of “being outside”, insofar as non-participation in certain circuits reduces agency by restricting access to signals, alliances and support. The image of the individual “treated like a stone” synthesizes the effect of social illegibility, while the passage ties this effect to a cumulative dynamic, in which exclusion tends to turn into chronic isolation and progressive degradation of opportunities. In this framing, consumption ceases to be read as whim and comes to be read as infrastructure of social survival, since the capacity to maintain bonds and circulate information depends on participating in shared practices that require money. ↩
  8. Kopytoff proposes a direct methodological turn. Instead of treating objects as stable units, endowed with a fixed economic “nature”, he suggests tracing trajectories. It is not a matter of writing biography out of literary exoticism, but of showing that the social identity of a thing is constructed and continually reassigned. What makes the biography “cultural” is not the theme, but the perspective, since the object is seen as a culturally constructed entity, invested with specific meanings and subject to successive reclassifications into socially produced categories. The passage formulates this unequivocally: “What would make a biography cultural is not what it deals with, but how and from what perspective. A culturally informed economic biography of an object would look at it as a culturally constructed entity, endowed with culturally specific meanings, and classified and reclassified into culturally constituted categories” Kopytoff(Kopytoff, 1986, p. 68). The reach of the argument lies in shifting the question. Instead of “what is this object?”, the analysis comes to ask through which categories it has passed, who inserted it into them and what kinds of value and prohibition were coupled to each phase. The object ceases to be a neutral support of value and becomes a node of historical classifications, in which “commodity” is only one possibility among others, often a temporary phase. ↩
  9. In the chapter The Cultural Biography of Things: Commoditization as Process, part of the volume The Social Life of Things: Commodities in Cultural Perspective, edited by Arjun Appadurai (1986), Igor Kopytoff (1986) shifts commoditization from a state to a process. Things enter and leave the status of commodity, alternating moments of homogenization and of singularization. ↩
  10. One of the most productive points on which Kopytoff's text insists is that the initial exchange does not fix the destiny of the object. Buying, selling or exchanging does not exhaust the subsequent social status of the thing, nor does it guarantee that it remains a commodity in the full sense. Kopytoff describes commoditization as a reversible process full of zones of latency. Things can be withdrawn from the sphere of exchange, “deactivated” as commodities and, even so, retain exchange potential and latent exchange value. The passage is explicit: “In the same way, the fact that an object is bought or exchanged says nothing about its subsequent status and whether it will remain a commodity or not. But unless formally decommoditized, commoditized things remain potential commodities — they continue to have an exchange value, even if they have been effectively withdrawn from their exchange sphere and deactivated, so to speak, as commodities. This deactivation leaves them open not only to the various kinds of singularization I have mentioned so far, but also to individual, as opposed to collective, redefinitions” Kopytoff(Kopytoff, 1986, p. 75-76). The conceptual force lies in breaking the automatic link between “having been a commodity” and “being a commodity”. The thing may remain nominally “possessed” and, at the same time, lose its practical place in the circuit of comparison, equivalence and recognition. The focus ceases to be the moment of purchase and shifts to the maintenance, suspension or reconfiguration of the exchange status and of the kind of value the thing carries at each stage of its trajectory. ↩
  11. In discussing singularization, Kopytoff links value and power explicitly. In every society, there are things that are publicly precluded from becoming commodities. Part of these prohibitions is cultural and collectively upheld. In state societies, many are the “handwork of the state”, in a typical combination of what “serves society”, what serves the state and what serves the groups in control. In this scenario, singularization appears as a gesture of authority, since power asserts itself symbolically by insisting on the right to singularize objects, that is, to withdraw them from the common and protect them from the undifferentiated flow of exchange. In the passage: “In every society, there are things that are publicly precluded from being commoditized. Some of the prohibitions are cultural and upheld collectively. In state societies, many of these prohibitions are the handwork of the state, with the usual intertwining between what serves the society at large, what serves the state, and what serves the specific groups in control. […] Power often asserts itself symbolically precisely by insisting on its right to singularize an object, or a set or class of objects” Kopytoff(Kopytoff, 1986, p. 73). The concept of singularization, therefore, is not limited to “making special”. It designates governing the borders between the comparable and the incomparable, between the exchangeable and the safeguarded, between the common and the exceptional. Singularity is a social status with distributive effects, since whoever can singularize can also exclude, hierarchize and stabilize prestige. ↩
  12. Kopytoff closes the arc by showing that singularity is not an intimate whim. When singularization is carried out by groups, it carries collective approval, channels the individual drive and gains the weight of “cultural sacredness”. For this reason, disputes over taste, aesthetics and value may conceal conflicts of class, identity and power. At this point, Kopytoff names the institutional core of the process, which is the struggle for control of the “public institutions of singularization”, that is, public arenas capable of certifying and imposing which objects will be treated as singular and which will remain in the common. The passage formulates it precisely: “Singularization of objects by groups within the society poses a special problem. Because it is done by groups, it bears the stamp of collective approval, channels the individual drive for singularization, and takes on the weight of cultural sacredness. […] Behind the extraordinarily vehement assertions of aesthetic values may stand conflicts of culture, class, and ethnic identity, and the struggle over the power of what one might label the ‘public institutions of singularization’” Kopytoff(Kopytoff, 1986, p. 81). The notion of “public institutions of singularization” makes it possible to read value as something that requires certification. Singularity is not “born” of the object. It is produced, validated and disputed in arenas capable of imposing public recognition. For this reason, the cultural biography of a thing changes when these arenas change, since what changes is who holds the power to say what is rare, exceptional, collectible, worthy of reverence or, on the contrary, ordinary and replaceable. ↩
  13. “Ritual”, here, is not equivalent to solemnity. It is a social technology for stabilizing meanings. The initial diagnosis is that social life faces a basic problem, since meanings drift, categories dissolve and interpretations slide. Without public conventions, there is no minimum consensual basis for acting. The authors describe rituals as devices that contain this drift and establish visible public definitions. Goods enter as material adjuncts of this process, since they function as a practical support capable of anchoring conventions. The chain appears clearly: “The main problem of social life is to pin down meanings so that they stay still for a little time. Without some conventional ways of selecting and fixing agreed meanings, the minimum consensual basis of society is missing. […] As for tribal society, so too for us: rituals serve to contain the drift of meanings. Rituals are conventions that set up visible public definitions. […] Goods, in this perspective, are ritual adjuncts; consumption is a ritual process whose primary function is to make sense of the inchoate flux of events” Isherwood(Douglas; Isherwood, 1996, p. 43). Next, consumption is defined in lapidary form as a ritual activity, precisely because it turns fluid judgments into stable and publicized markings: “Consumption uses goods to make firm and visible a particular set of judgments in the fluid processes of classifying persons and events. We have now defined it as a ritual activity” Isherwood(Douglas; Isherwood, 1996, p. 45). The interpretive key, therefore, is that consuming is not limited to acquiring, but implies participating in a system of marking that fixes classifications. Ritual “makes world” by stabilizing scales of value and recognizable positions. For this reason, repetition is neither noise nor mere habit, since it is part of the mechanism that keeps categories standing over time. ↩
  14. It is exactly this point that Grant McCracken systematizes in Culture and Consumption: A Theoretical Account of the Structure and Movement of the Cultural Meaning of Consumer Goods (1986). McCracken begins by pointing to the limitation he considers structural in the dominant approaches of that period. These approaches treated the cultural meaning of goods as something fixed, “adhered” to the object, as if it were stored in it and the consumer only had to buy it to appropriate it. The proposal is to put meaning back in motion. Cultural meaning, in a consumer society, is not a motionless stock, but continuous transit between relatively stable instances and “stations”. For this reason, McCracken speaks of a usual trajectory and of instruments responsible for this displacement. “Cultural meaning in a consumer society moves ceaselessly from one location to another. In the usual trajectory, cultural meaning moves first from the culturally constituted world to consumer goods and then from these goods to the individual consumer. Several instruments are responsible for this movement: advertising, the fashion system, and four consumption rituals” McCracken(McCracken, 1986, p. 71). Thus, in the first place, “good” and “consumer” cease to be origins of meaning and become points of passage, with specific functions in symbolic circulation. In the second place, the circuit is not merely metaphorical, since there are identifiable instruments that transfer meaning, such as advertising, the fashion system and rituals. With this, culture does not appear as a diffuse backdrop, but as a mechanism that must be operated for meaning to move and remain functional. ↩
  15. In dealing with the instruments that transfer meaning from the “culturally constituted world” to goods, McCracken assigns to advertising a stronger role than that of “persuading”. Advertising is described as a conduit, that is, a channel through which meaning continually “pours”. It couples meanings to goods, removes old meanings and attaches new ones. The viewer or reader enters as an active participant in the process, because they must decode and complete the transfer. It follows that advertising continually informs the public of the “present state” of the cultural meaning available in goods, as if it updated the symbolic inventory of the material world. The passage is entirely designed for this effect: “Advertising is a conduit through which meaning constantly pours from the culturally constituted world to consumer goods. Through advertising, old and new goods continually give up old meanings and take on new ones. As active participants in this process, the viewer/reader is kept informed of the present state and stock of cultural meaning that exists in consumer goods. To this extent, advertising serves as a lexicon of current cultural meanings” McCracken(McCracken, 1986, p. 75-76). The vocabulary employed is revealing, since it points to updating, and not to a mere message. Advertising, in this key, functions as a mechanism of symbolic synchronization. It keeps the public aligned with the prevailing repertoire and reorganizes the legibility of what goods “mean” at a given moment. ↩
  16. Before examining the instruments, McCracken defines the place where meaning originally resides. He locates it in the “culturally constituted world” and does so through a double definition of culture, crucial for refusing the idea of culture as a mere subjective filter. Culture appears as lens and as blueprint. It functions as lens because it guides how the phenomenon is seen. It functions as blueprint because it prescribes coordinates of action and production, by specifying behaviors and objects that become legitimate references. The passage makes this double function explicit: “Culture constitutes the phenomenal world in two ways. First, culture is the ‘lens’ through which the individual views phenomena […]. Second, culture is the ‘blueprint’ of human activity, determining the co-ordinates of social action and productive activity, and specifying the behaviors and objects that issue from both. […] In short, culture constitutes the world by supplying it with meaning. This meaning can be characterized in terms of two concepts: cultural categories and cultural principles” McCracken(McCracken, 1986, p. 72). cultural configure the map of the intelligible, by ordering divisions of time, space, person, status, gender, class and appropriateness. It is not a matter of individual opinion, but of a matrix that guides perception and, at the same time, defines a repertoire of actions and objects that can count as socially relevant. ↩
  17. The theory of the transit of meaning contains a decisive point. Part of meaning is perishable. McCracken uses this notion to explain why symbolic appropriation is not exhausted at purchase and why consumption rituals must be repeated. If meaning “expires”, it must be periodically extracted from possessions, since symbolic life requires reactivation. For this reason, he links perishability to the need for a continuous process of transfer from the good to the consumer and, consequently, to the tendency to resort to specific rituals, such as grooming rituals. These rituals involve care and “work” to make symbolic properties “live”, even if only for a short time. The passage is long and very clear: “It is clear that some of the cultural meaning drawn from goods has a perishable nature. As a result, the consumer must draw cultural meaning out of his/her possessions on a repeated basis. When a continual process of meaning transfer from goods to consumer is necessary, the consumer will likely resort to a grooming ritual. The purpose of this ritual is to take the special pains necessary to insure that the special, perishable properties […] are, as it were, ‘coaxed’ out of their resident goods and made to live, however briefly and precariously, in the life of the individual consumer” McCracken(McCracken, 1986, p. 79). The image of “coaxed” is central, since it indicates that meaning does not emerge on its own. It must be activated and cultivated. The very precariousness expressed in “however briefly and precariously” reinforces the logic of continuous maintenance. Ritualized consumption thus appears as regular symbolic work, and not as whim. Repetition, in this framing, is the ordinary way of preventing fragile cultural attributes from collapsing through obsolescence, disuse or replacement by new arrangements of meaning. ↩
  18. The dismantling of “demand” as a private impulse appears when Appadurai contrasts mechanisms that manufacture taste and regulate appropriateness of consumption in different societies. The target is the idea that desiring and demanding would be spontaneous movements of the individual, like a psychological energy that the economy would merely account for. Appadurai describes demand as a socially regulated and socially generated impulse, produced by taste-making mechanisms and by rules that organize the appropriate, the available and the prestigious. Even when the mechanisms vary, whether by fashion, media, informal norms or sumptuary laws, the structure remains. Demand is born within devices of social regulation and production, and not as an artifact of individual whims or needs. In the passage: “The demand for commodities is critically regulated by this variety of taste-making mechanisms, whose social origin is more clearly understood (both by consumers and by analysts) in our own society than in those distant from us. […] In both cases, however, demand is a socially regulated and generated impulse, not an artifact of individual whims or needs” Appadurai(Appadurai, 1986, p. 32). In this way, demand ceases to be a natural given and becomes an institutional and cultural variable. What counts as desirable, comparable and legitimate is not merely perceived, but produced and stabilized by mechanisms that organize visibility, appropriateness, distinction and belonging. The economy of consumption becomes legible as a process of manufacturing criteria, and not as a sum of autonomous wills. ↩
