7.1.1.2 On the parameters of semiotic vulnerability as a mechanism

See this section in the PDF

  • Semiotic vulnerability

p. 719

The vulnerability relevant to the application of the regime of the Immaterial Object of Consumption cannot be understood as a generic attribute of the digital, nor be reduced to economic hyposufficiency or to the consumer's lesser individual ability to understand technically complex environments. This reading is insufficient because it shifts the problem onto the person of the user and obscures the power structure that shapes the relationship. What is decisive is to recognize that, in platform-governed ecosystems, expectation, consent, perception of value, perception of risk and the possibility of contestation depend on signs, interfaces, informational flows and experience trails controlled by the supplier itself. Vulnerability, in this context, emerges as a structural effect of the way the environment organizes meaning.

That is why the vulnerability relevant here must be qualified as semiotic vulnerability. The term does not designate naivety, credulity or cultural deficiency of the consumer. It designates a mechanism of power inscribed in the architecture of p. 720 supply, through which the supplier administers the modes of presentation of the object, the criteria of visibility of relevant information, the routes of comprehension of the experience, the perceived equivalences and the practical possibilities of resistance or reaction. Instead of operating only on conscious and one-off choices, this form of power bears on the very intelligibility of the relationship, conditioning how the object is perceived, how its promise is decoded and how its later alteration may or may not be recognized as legally relevant.

This qualification is important because it prevents the analysis from falling into moralism about the consumer's conduct. It is not a matter of asking whether the user was sufficiently attentive, distrustful or technically informed. Nor is it a matter of presupposing absolute incapacity in the face of the digital. The question is objective. Whenever the consumption experience is mediated by signs and interfaces whose design, hierarchy, salience and mutability are controlled by the supplier, there will be a field of semiotic asymmetry potentially relevant to the Law. The consumer depends, in these cases, on an environment whose vocabulary, rhythm, emphases and criteria of legibility are not neutral. They are produced, selected and reorganized by whoever holds the power of governance of the ecosystem.

What is distinctive about this mechanism is that the asymmetry of meaning tends to be accompanied by an asymmetry of evidence. Whoever controls the way the object is made intelligible frequently also controls the records by which that intelligibility can be reconstructed in case of conflict. Logs, interface histories, screen versions, curation criteria, alteration records, visibility parameters and decision traces remain under the supplier's control. The result is a double concentration of power. On one side, the capacity to shape the ordinary reading of enjoyment is concentrated. On the other, the capacity to narrate retrospectively, in institutionally acceptable terms, what occurred in the relationship is concentrated. In environments of this nature, semiotic vulnerability is not an accidental layer of the relationship. It is a recurring part of the way it operates.

This finding has a relevant dogmatic consequence. Semiotic vulnerability does not operate only as a sociological description of the environment, but as a category of attribution and control. Its usefulness lies in tracing duties, burdens and legal limits back to the concrete structure of the governance of meaning. It makes it possible to understand why the duty to inform, in these contexts, is not satisfied by textual volume; why advertising must be read as management of expectation and not only as a promotional statement; why evidence cannot be organized while ignoring the private concentration of records; and why the very p. 721 mutability of the environment can convert seemingly minor alterations into legally relevant forms of capture, obscuring or displacement of enjoyment.

The parameter also has a delimiting function. It prevents every difficulty experienced in a digital environment from being automatically converted into legally qualified vulnerability. It is not enough that the case involves an interface, an algorithm or an online environment. It is necessary that the governance of meaning act structurally on the comprehension, comparability, predictability or contestation of enjoyment. What is controlled, therefore, is not the mere existence of technological mediation, but the configuration of an arrangement in which signs, paths and records are asymmetrically organized by whoever also holds the power of alteration, sanction or modulation of the relationship. Semiotic vulnerability gains legal relevance precisely when it becomes an objective condition of the consumer relationship and interferes with the institutional legibility of the conflict.

Read this way, the category rules out two equally inadequate reductionisms. The first reduces vulnerability to informational poverty in a quantitative sense, as if it sufficed to measure how much the consumer knew or failed to know. The second dissolves it into a cultural abstraction about the “digital society”, losing sight of the concrete mechanisms by which the relationship is organized. The parameter proposed here refuses both. Semiotic vulnerability is relational, structural and functional. Relational, because it arises from the position the parties occupy in the production and administration of meaning. Structural, because it stems from the design of the environment and not only from isolated episodes. Functional, because it only acquires dogmatic relevance when it interferes with the formation of expectation, the reading of utility, the assessment of risk or the consumer's possibility of institutional reaction.

With this formulation, the application of the IOC regime keeps its focus on the contemporary exercise of private power in consumption. The center of the analysis is no longer a supposed personal insufficiency of the user but the governance of meaning as a technology for organizing enjoyment. This avoids both the romanticization of consumer autonomy in structurally asymmetric environments and the abstract infantilization of the consumer's legal position. What is asserted is more precise. On platforms that control signs, flows, records and criteria of legibility, vulnerability takes a semiotic form because it is through it that private power administers perception, predictability and contestation. Recognizing it as a mechanism is a condition for legal protection to bear on the real structure of the relationship, and not on moral caricatures of its participants.
