5.2.3 On the Legal Framework for Electronic Games and preliminary framing

See this section in the PDF

  • Dogmatics · Sections of chapter 5

p. 493

At this point, the normative and institutional framing of the electronic games sector has a strict and limited function. The criteria-oriented typology has already stabilized the lenses through which platform-governed enjoyment can be observed and compared. What remains is to situate, in the Brazilian context, how the sector has been recognized and around which focal points it tends to be problematized, so that the study advances on institutionally intelligible ground and so that recurring tensions do not look like idiosyncratic contingencies of an isolated case.

The aim is neither to compile a legislative inventory nor to reconstruct the regulatory debate in detail. What matters is to record the minimum context in which games operated as a service come to concentrate public and legal attention, highlighting vectors that reappear when enjoyment depends on an environment of continuous operation with intense private governance. Against this background, a twofold movement can be observed. On the one hand, there is an effort to institutionalize games as a relevant economic and cultural activity. On the other, concerns keep recurring about the protection of vulnerable audiences, monetization through microtransactions and random mechanisms, the processing of personal data, and market and advertising practices built into the very experience of enjoyment.

The institutionalization of the sector matters because it reinforces, on the documentary record, the reading that games (above all under models operated as a service) are not limited to isolated products delivered in a single event. The offer is realized as an ecosystem sustained by continuous operation, permanent updating and enforcement of internal rules that form part of the material content of supply. A sufficient landmark for recording this fact is Law 14,852/2024 Brasil(Brasil, [2024]b), taken here as a reference point for the institutional recognition of the electronic games industry as an economic and cultural activity with its own relevance. The central point, for framing purposes, is the premise the sectoral statute expresses, namely that there is a sector operating in Brazil that is consolidated as a legitimate object of state attention and of public policy design, with the typical language of promotion, development and organization of a production chain. This dispels any impression that the locus studied is exceptional and brings it closer to other mass digital markets in which enjoyment unfolds over time under variable rules.

Recognition, however, does not eliminate legal tensions, it only tends to make them more predictable. In ecosystems of continued enjoyment, expectations are formed over time, under criteria open to unilateral modulation, and relevant losses may occur without formal interruption of access, whether through gradual degradation of utility or through p. 494 disorganization of the symbolic value incorporated into what was acquired. For this reason, institutional attention is organized around vectors of friction that stem from the structure of the ecosystem, and not from isolated episodes.

The protection of children and adolescents is one of these recurring vectors. Its centrality derives not only from concerns about content, but from the combination of interactivity, the permanence of the bond and exposure to mechanisms of acquisition and retention built into the interfaces and rules of the environment. In games operated as a service, consumption decisions tend to be fragmented over time, mediated by return loops, urgency cues, promises of progression, social comparison and incentives embedded in the very design of enjoyment. Focusing on minors thus works as an institutional trigger that makes visible asymmetries of understanding and of capacity to contest in environments governed by architecture.

Within this framework, the public policy of advisory age rating plays a useful contextual role. Without functioning as prior authorization, it acts as a guidance instrument aimed at informing age suitability and relevant content descriptors, and, by reaching digital products distributed through platforms, it makes explicit an important institutional premise: the experience of enjoyment is not exhausted by audiovisual “content”, since it includes conditions of interaction and circulation that can produce relevant exposures and asymmetries, above all for children and adolescents. What matters here is not the detail of the instrument, but what it signals about the sector. In a significant share of cases, the risk lies in the way the environment of enjoyment itself functions.

Another predictable focal point is monetization through microtransactions. In games as a service, acquisition tends to occur continuously and in segments, embedded in the very experience of enjoyment. This is not merely a payment method. Monetization is built into the architecture of the environment and influences the pace of use, progression, access to conveniences and the very perception of the value of what is acquired. The legal conflict thus shifts from the price of an isolated product to the governance of a system of offers and incentives that renews itself over time, under variable rules and with structurally pronounced informational asymmetries.

The point of greatest sensitivity arises when monetization relies on random mechanisms for obtaining items, often referred to in public debate as loot boxes. In these cases, the utility and value of what is acquired come to depend on internal distribution regimes, availability criteria and limits defined by the platform, and are also tied to scarcity cues, time windows and rarity regimes that make decisions more dependent on anticipation, urgency and expectation. The institutional discussion p. 495 then tends to focus on minimum transparency of the conditions of acquisition, protection of vulnerable audiences and control of practices that induce consumption, not out of moralizing about the sector, but because the supplier holds the internal criteria on availability, rotation, rarity and, where applicable, probability, under conditions of low external reconstructibility.

In this context, Law No. 15,211/2025 Brasil(Brasil, [2025]c) offers sufficient normative anchoring to situate the focal point, as it regulates, from the standpoint of protecting children and adolescents, the loot box feature as a mechanism for obtaining items at random upon payment. The statute's interest here is evidential. Certain mechanics of the ecosystem have already been identified as structurally sensitive and therefore open to treatment by criteria. Under the IOC regime, such mechanisms condense elements such as instituted rarity, anticipation, criterial opacity and unilateral modulation, with effects that may manifest themselves as material erosion of utility and reconfiguration of value, even if access remains formally available.

A further institutional vector stems from the fact that games operated as a service function as informational platforms. Enjoyment is realized through an identified account, inventories, usage records, performance metrics, purchase history, interaction patterns and reputational mechanisms that depend on the continuous collection and processing of data. These data are not merely administrative traces. They sustain the personalization of the experience, the offering of items, rankings, validations, fraud detection, moderation and the enforcement of internal rules. The relevant contextual consequence is the intensification of structural information asymmetries. The supplier holds the records and parameters that are decisive for reconstructing changes and governance decisions, while the consumer has only partial visibility, mediated by interfaces.

In this scenario, the Brazilian personal data protection regime operates as a normative backdrop, reinforcing the institutional sensitivity of the sector in environments of continued enjoyment, especially when children and adolescents are involved. The point here is not to turn the framing into a compliance audit, but to record that the environment of enjoyment is also an informational environment and that, for this reason, a minimum reconstruction of what changed, to what extent, and with what effects on utility and value becomes a relevant component of any discussion of controllability, without requiring full technical transparency or the disclosure of trade secrets.

Beyond minors, monetization and data, concerns associated with market and advertising practices built into the experience of enjoyment can be expected to arise. In models operated as a service, the consumer relationship is not exhausted at the initial entry. The offer p. 496 is renewed through events, seasons, promotions, bundles and purchase incentives built into use, so that advertising also operates as internal communication designed through storefronts, urgency messages, recommendations and highlighting mechanisms. This integration reinforces the continuous and mass character of supply and tends to trigger classic consumer protection instruments aimed at adequate information, transparency and the control of abusive practices.

Within this framework, the Consumer Protection Code functions as a general grammar of protection in mass relationships marked by informational asymmetry and by concentration of the power to organize the offer, without thereby anticipating, at this point, the specific dogmatic reconstruction of the IOC regime. What matters for the minimum framing is to recognize that ecosystems of this kind coexist with repeated patterns of complaints and large-scale conflicts, in which the conditions of acquisition, the criteria of the offer and the effects of internal changes to the environment become central.

Finally, intellectual property appears as a latent source of friction because enjoyment is mediated by protected assets and by licensing regimes that delimit admissible uses, forms of access and the possibilities of circulation of what was acquired. In platform-governed ecosystems, terms of use, moderation policies, transfer limits and revocation conditions are usually drafted under a logic of intangible asset management, which can intensify asymmetries of agency and reduce the alternatives for reconstituting what was acquired outside the ecosystem. The usefulness of this observation here is merely situational: consumer conflicts in games as a service often cut across the interface between consumer protection and control prerogatives derived from licenses and intellectual property, leaving the analysis of the consequences to the proper stage of legal reconstruction.

The minimum institutional framework outlined here makes it possible to understand why the electronic games sector, especially under models operated as a service, concentrates recurring legal tensions. The recognition of the sector as a relevant economic and cultural activity coexists with predictable focal points concerning the protection of children and adolescents, monetization through microtransactions and random mechanisms, the processing of personal data, and market and advertising practices built into the environment of enjoyment. These vectors appear not as episodic accidents, but as typical effects of ecosystems in which utility is realized over time, under private governance of the environment and through rules open to unilateral modulation, with structural asymmetries of intelligibility and agency.
