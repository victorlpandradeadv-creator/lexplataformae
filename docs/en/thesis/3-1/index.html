3.1 Technique, media and everyday life

See this section in the PDF

This section in the layers of the site

  • Foundations · Sections of chapter 3

p. 85

Consumption in digital environments does not take place over a neutral medium, nor is it exhausted in contracting for an isolated good or service. The technical-media order designates, here, the regime of technical and communicational mediations that comes to operate as the everyday infrastructure of experience. Interfaces, recommendation systems, metrics, protocols and display formats organize what appears as accessible, desirable, relevant or normal. In this context, the consumption experience tends to stop being a one-off event and comes to take place in ecosystems of continuous mediation, in which enjoyment itself depends on architecture, curation and regimes of signification.

The first layer of this reconstruction starts from Jacques Ellul, whose reading makes it possible to understand the tendency toward autonomization of technical rationality guided by efficiency, predictability and control. Neil Postman shifts the problem to media as a form of life, showing that means of communication alter criteria of relevance, intelligibility p. 86 and socially practicable truth. Willem H. Vanderburg adds the critique of desymbolization, making explicit how signs, needs and cultural bonds can be reorganized by advertising and by the technical rationality of consumption. Together, these diagnoses describe an environment in which technique and media cease to operate as external tools and come to constitute structural conditions of everyday life.

Andrew Feenberg's critique prevents this diagnosis from being received as fatalism. If technology is a social construction, concrete mediations matter legally: interfaces, frictions, permissions, incentives and punishments materialize social and economic purposes in artifacts and platforms. The practical normativity of the environment does not derive only from texts, contracts or explicit commands, but from the design and governance choices that organize the field of the possible and the probable. For this reason, technical architecture is not a mere support for the consumer relationship; it is part of the very form by which conduct is guided, expectations are stabilized and losses can be produced.

In this scenario, it becomes possible to understand why, in digital ecosystems, the value of the consumed object is not separated from the mediations that make it enjoyable, accessible and stable over time. Jacques Ellul (1968) describes technique as a system that ceases to operate as a simple instrument and comes to present itself as a reality of its own, no longer limited to being a means, but capable of imposing its logic as an object in itself Ellul(Ellul, 1968, p. 65). This dynamic is guided by efficiency and tends to select automatically the most effective method, so that, once the criterion of efficiency has been measured and established, technique consolidates and expands by its own force Ellul(Ellul, 1968, p. 82-83).

The movement is cumulative and feeds on its own self-growth. Technique reaches a point at which it comes to transform itself and progress with little decisive intervention by man Ellul(Ellul, 1968, p. 88-89). For this reason, its effects go beyond the productive plane and assume a civilizational dimension, insofar as technique expands into all domains, tends to reduce man to the condition of an object and imposes itself as the substance of the social world, and can be described as an entire civilization Ellul(Ellul, 1968, p. 5; p. 128). In this scenario, values and purposes lose centrality because technical rationality operates as a machine of instrumentalization, converting everything into a means and shifting the criterion of decision to the performance of the method and to what works best Ellul(Ellul, 1968, p. 145).

Ellul (1968) insists that the technical phenomenon40 cannot be treated as a particular sector of modernity, because it tends to cut across the very elements of civilization. The p. 87 decisive point is that, in whatever domain it is applied, technique presents itself as a rational process, which shifts the social problem into a problem of method Ellul(Ellul, 1968, p. 81). Alternatives come to be filtered by what can be rationalized, measured and operationalized, and the debate on ends tends to be compressed by the grammar of efficiency.

The consequence is a process of standardization and adaptation of the human being to the demands of the technical system, with decision-making power concentrated in bureaucratic structures and substantive margins of self-determination weakened. The effect is a reorganization of modern life in which work, administration and culture come to be shaped by demands of performance, standardization and control, relegating to the background other rationalities (ethical, political and symbolic) that do not translate into efficiency. From this perspective, the technical-media order is neither neutral nor merely instrumental, because it constitutes the practical horizon from which digital experience becomes possible and intelligible and, at the same time, produces the social and cultural conditions that naturalize certain routines, perceptions and consumption behaviors.

Ellul (1968) links the obsession with efficiency and productivity to the production of an inhuman atmosphere, in which work is dehumanized and man distances himself from nature Ellul(Ellul, 1968, p. 3). On the political-economic plane, he maintains that technique favors the formation of an aristocracy of technicians, capable of concentrating knowledge and decisions in opaque circuits that are scarcely accessible to the lay public Ellul(Ellul, 1968, p. 165). When this picture is articulated with the State, democracy is weakened because public debate tends to be replaced by management and by mechanisms of persuasion, with corrosive effects on the citizen's faculty of discernment Ellul(Ellul, 1968, p. 281).

The reference to Ellul matters here as a structural diagnosis of a rationality guided by efficiency, predictability and control, capable of expanding and stabilizing itself as an environment. The analytical gain lies in making visible that, when technique presents itself as “necessity” and as an automatic criterion of decision, purposes and values tend to be compressed by the grammar of what works “best”. This compression is relevant to p. 88 digital consumption because in it choices of design and governance are naturalized as if they were mere technical requirements, shifting conflicts outside the field of deliberation and accountability.

It should, however, be made explicit from the outset that drawing on Ellul does not amount here to adherence to technological determinism, but serves as a key to understanding a structural tendency whereby technical rationality, guided by efficiency, predictability and control, tends to spill over specific domains and to reorganize social practices, including consumption. To say that technique acquires “autonomy” does not mean postulating a single, inevitable or monolithic causality, but recognizing that, in certain sociotechnical arrangements, institutional and economic incentives turn innovation and the implementation of systems into a partially self-fueling dynamic, often detached from public deliberation proportional to their effects. It is precisely in this space, between systemic tendency and the possibility of democratic governance, that the legal problem at issue lies. If technical architecture becomes a vector for organizing everyday life, it also becomes a locus of attribution and of assessment of legitimacy, above all when it comes to structure choices, expectations and access regimes that cannot be audited by their addressees or even by legitimate authorities.

Precisely for this reason, the reading of Ellul needs to be articulated with that of Feenberg. The systemic force of technique does not eliminate the fact that artifacts, infrastructures and platforms assume concrete forms through socially situated choices, traversed by interests, disputes and purposes. This passage is decisive for the thesis: if technical architecture organizes the consumption experience, it cannot be treated as nature. It must be read as design, and design is susceptible to critique, control and reorientation.

With Feenberg, the problem shifts from the technical phenomenon in general to specific mediations. Interfaces, frictions, permissions, prohibitions, incentives and punishments are not a simple operational layer; they express a socially situated rationality. In the context of this research, this means that the appearance of inevitability of platforms must also be read as the result of consolidated choices, which come to operate as a naturalized environment. It is this mechanism of naturalization that makes the architecture of platforms legally relevant: it conceals purposes, hinders contestation and shifts conflicts outside the ordinary sphere of accountability.

In continuous digital services, especially in interactive entertainment, the modulation of what can be enjoyed is usually treated by the supplier as an ordinary operation of the system, while the consumer stabilizes expectations of enjoyment and belonging on the basis of p. 89 what was promised, acquired and experienced. This gap between regimes of meaning produces qualificatory opacity: it makes it difficult to name the injury, identify the affected object and bring the loss back within the traditional categories of suitability, quality, defect and information. For this reason, architecture and governance must be treated as a material dimension of supply.

The contribution of Neil Postman (2006), influenced by Ellul41, shifts the technical diagnosis to the plane of media as the dominant cultural form. In Amusing Ourselves to Death, the author analyzes how television, by replacing the printed word as the main means of public communication, reorganizes the form of discourse and, with it, reconfigures cultural content itself. Politics, religion and education come to operate under the grammar of entertainment, in a society that approaches the condition of “amusing ourselves to death” Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 16]).

In the political field, visual logic shifts the weight of argument to appearance, performance and impression management, reducing the centrality of programs, ideologies and substantive confrontations of ideas Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 16]). In the religious domain, television tends to convert the experience of transcendence into spectacle, emptying ritual density and symbolic depth Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 79]). In education, a pedagogical philosophy guided by immediate accessibility and contentment is consolidated, to the detriment of cognitive effort and more demanding intellectual formation Postman(Postman, 2006, [p. 96]). The common point is the reconfiguration of the social criteria of attention, relevance and truth.

In the contemporary digital environment, this contribution gains additional specificity. The epistemological reconfiguration no longer stems only from the predominance of image or entertainment, but from the passage to interactive, personalized and metrified ecosystems, in which the circulation of statements is modulated by recommendation, ranking and differential distribution of visibility. If on television entertainment colonizes public discourse through a relatively uniform schedule, on platforms it is coupled to the capture of attention based on continuous feedback, permanent testing and interface micro-optimizations. This shift is decisive for the thesis because the problem is no longer only the superficialization of content, but the production of a regime of reality in which signs, promises and p. 90 performances of belonging guide consumer conduct and form part of the semiotic materiality of the IOC.

Drawing on Feenberg, this epistemological reconfiguration can also be read as politics built into technical design. Personalization, ranking and metrification are not mere functionalities, since they materialize criteria of value and visibility that guide behavior at scale. The passage from television to platforms, therefore, is not a simple change of medium, but a change of architecture: entertainment and attention become operationalizable by technical structures that organize the field of the possible and the probable. For the IOC, this means that the offer cannot be reduced to the final content, since it includes the designed mediations that make this content consumable and carry identifiable teleologies, even if they are not openly presented.

The work of Willem H. Vanderburg (2011) deepens this critique by examining advertising as an interested catalyst of cultural desymbolization42, the process by which artificial needs are produced and excessive consumption is normalized. For the author, companies mobilize cultural symbols to attribute to material products the illusory capacity to satisfy emotional and social needs, which is particularly serious when this dynamic targets children, who are less able to distinguish programming from advertising. Advertising, in this framework, manipulates vulnerabilities, weakens cultural symbols and demands constant strategic reinvention to remain effective.

Vanderburg also problematizes the absence of substantive information in advertisements, whose aim is no longer to inform but to steer consumer conduct. From a transnational perspective, he points to obstacles to the democratic regulation of excessive advertising, especially when corporate rights prevail over the rights of communities. Advertising does not merely shape individual behavior; it perpetuates a cycle of consumption harmful to social well-being and establishes itself as a significant tool of the technical order.

p. 91 In digital ecosystems, Vanderburg's critique of advertising gains new reach: desymbolization does not act only in advertising discourse, but is coupled to technical architectures that administer visibility, repetition and reward. With Feenberg (2017), the advertisement ceases to be a separate event and becomes a component of a designed environment, in which the circulation of signs is programmed to produce need, adherence and permanence. Semiotic manipulation, therefore, does not occur only through the content of messages, but through the form of their distribution and through the interface logic that defines when, how and for whom certain signs appear. This intensifies the problem of auditability and shifts the debate from persuasion to the governance of the field of experience.

The mention of transnational arenas, such as international trade, does not function here as a conclusive statement about a specific body, but as an indication of a structural problem: the delocalization and multilevel complexification of the conditions for regulating advertising and consumption in digital ecosystems. The desymbolization described by Vanderburg matters because it reveals a pattern of governance of consumption through signs that often exceeds the addressee's horizon of control and, not infrequently, also the immediate reach of traditional regulatory responses.

On platforms, this semiotic governance is reinforced by contracts, private moderation policies and proprietary infrastructures for the circulation of content. In this layer, intellectual property rights appear not only as protection of creations, but as a legal technology for the control of signs, the delimitation of uses and the stabilization of expectations of enjoyment. The point is not to shift the axis to a macroinstitutional debate, but to make visible that contemporary semiotic manipulation operates in ecosystems in which normative and technical layers overlap, aggravating the asymmetry between those who design and those who consume.

The characterization of digital consumption as mediated and continued enjoyment finds support in Brazilian positive law, which already recognizes, in specific disciplines linked to software and related services, duties of functional continuity and technical support tied to the economic exploitation of the object. For the purposes of this section, the reference does not anticipate the later dogmatic construction; it only shows that dependence on maintenance, updating and governance over time is not an accident external to the consumer relationship, but an attributable feature of supply in digital environments.

In this environment, consumer vulnerability tends to manifest itself as the distance between the promised, curated and displayed experience and the actual conditions of enjoyment, modification, continuity and loss. Protection concerning suitability, quality, defect and information comes to depend p. 92 on the possibility of auditing and controlling mediations that organize the field of the possible and the probable. For this reason, before formulating the legal regime of the IOC, it is necessary to understand how technique becomes autonomous, how media reorganize the intelligibility of consumption and how design transforms economic choices into an everyday environment.

  1. For Jacques Ellul (1968), “technique” is not equivalent to machines, nor to the mechanical apparatus taken in isolation: “Whoever says technique immediately thinks of machine”, but this identification is a recurring error Ellul(Ellul, 1968, p. 1). This is because technique “has now assumed almost complete autonomy in relation to the machine”, surpassing the status of a simple instrument and coming to operate as a phenomenon of its own Ellul(Ellul, 1968, p. 2). In this key, technique presents itself as rationalization guided by efficiency, insofar as the “satisfactory” and “efficient” method tends to impose itself by itself — the “the one best way” mentioned earlier — and, with this, the technical decision comes to have the form of an objective necessity: “there is no choice between two technical methods: one imposes itself fatally because its results are counted, measured, patent and indisputable” Ellul(Ellul, 1968, p. 83). By penetrating “into all domains and into man himself” and tending to encompass the maximum of domains in order to be effective, technique ceases to be merely an object for man and “becomes his very substance”, acquiring totalizing reach Ellul(Ellul, 1968, p. 5). In this generalization, its performance criteria are converted into a practical measure of social decision, and the autonomy of the technical system restricts the possibility of human deliberation on ends: “the autonomy of technique therefore prevents man, at present, from choosing his destiny” Ellul(Ellul, 1968, p. 143). ↩
  2. Influenced by the work of Jacques Ellul, Postman treats technology not as a neutral tool, but as a rationality that tends to impose itself on institutions and culture through criteria of efficiency, standardization and control. On this basis, he shifts the diagnosis to media and education and maintains that media and their technical forms reorganize what counts as truth, relevance and attention, with effects of impoverishment of public debate and reduction of symbolic experience. In this framing, the critique is not an aversion to technology itself, but the defense of limits and of human criteria of judgment, such as purpose, meaning and responsibility, when technical rationality comes to operate as the dominant parameter. ↩
  3. According to Willem H. Vanderburg (2016), cultural desymbolization is the process by which the human capacity to attribute meaning to experiences is weakened, resulting in a fragmented and impoverished understanding of life as a whole. The process occurs when social, cultural and physical constraints limit people's participation, freedom and commitment, forcing them to navigate a fragmented world dominated by narrow, one-off objectives and by technological connectivity. Although desymbolization is not a new phenomenon, Vanderburg argues that, in modern societies, it takes on graver dimensions owing to the growing separation between knowledge and experience, fostering a dichotomy between "reality" and a "symbolically reduced universe." Desymbolization profoundly affects the way people experience the world, which he represents through an analogy with "mental maps". In traditional societies, these maps are densely connected, offering a rich network of meanings. In modern societies, however, as desymbolization intensifies, these maps become simplified, reducing symbolic connections and the capacity to understand life in its complexity. For Vanderburg, this process, exacerbated by reliance on disciplinary approaches disconnected from culture, results in a disjointed and divided world. ↩
