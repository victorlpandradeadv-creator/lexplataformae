7.1.1.9 On the parameters for reconciling the CDC and intellectual property rights in light of the primacy of the social function and Brazilian constitutional limits

p. 737

Reconciling the regime of the Immaterial Object of Consumption with intellectual property rights requires dispelling, from the outset, a recurring confusion. The existence of legally protected exclusivity over content, signs, code, systems, digital assets or platform structures does not amount to an unrestricted legitimation of the way this exclusivity is exercised within the consumer relationship. The protection of intellectual property remains intact on its own plane, but this does not allow its invocation to function as a general clause of immunization against review of adequacy, transparency, loyalty, functional equivalence and protection of enjoyment. In IOC ecosystems, exclusivity explains the legal organization of the environment, but does not neutralize the limits that bear on the way it is exercised.

This point is central because the relationship between intellectual property and continuous digital consumption is usually contaminated by a false alternative. Either one asserts the almost sovereign intangibility of exclusivity, treating any consumer-law interference as an undue threat to the economic architecture of the ecosystem, or one imagines that consumer protection would require reducing intellectual property to a secondary obstacle, devoid of legitimacy of its own. Neither of these positions is adequate. The first turns exclusivity into an unlimited power to govern enjoyment. The second impoverishes the understanding of the environment itself, whose organization frequently depends, legitimately, on exclusive structures of creation, licensing, technical control and economic exploitation. The legal problem lies not in choosing between one pole and the other, but in regulating the coexistence of both under Brazilian constitutional limits.

p. 738 From this standpoint, intellectual property must be read in light of the same constitutional rationality that shapes the exercise of property in general. Exclusivity is protected, but its exercise is not an absolute prerogative, detached from the social function, from social interest and from commitment to the constitutional objectives that subordinate private positions to an economic order guided by social justice, consumer protection and national development. This holds with particular force when intellectual property ceases to operate merely as an instrument for protecting creation or innovation and comes to structure environments of captive, continuous and governed enjoyment. In these contexts, exclusivity is not only a legal title over an asset. It is also an infrastructure of private power over access to, permanence in, modulation, updating and contestation of the consumer's experience. The holder of exclusivity consequently retains its economic and technical prerogatives, including the power to organize the ecosystem, protect its assets and preserve internal operating elements. What it does not retain is the possibility of invoking this position to rule out, a priori, review of the way supply is carried out.

Putting this reconciliation into operation runs through the filters already built into the regime. Open auditability is fully compatible with intellectual property because it does not demand full disclosure of trade secrets or total exposure of the technological architecture, but only minimal institutional intelligibility about whatever interferes with enjoyment in a legally relevant way. Functional value equivalence does not deny the prerogative to update, adapt or evolve the environment, but prevents this prerogative from being exercised as a license to hollow out, from within, the legitimately acquired experience. The reconciliation parameter also prevents the debate from being hijacked by an excessively proprietarian grammar, in which every controversy ends up being brought back to the question of who holds the asset or what the abstract extent of the contractual license granted to the user is. The relevant conflict often lies not in the direct violation of a proprietary or authorial position, but in the way exclusive ownership is instrumentalized to govern enjoyment, redistribute risks, concentrate the power of alteration and hinder contestation.

This does not mean that intellectual property is absorbed by the CDC, nor that its regime is reduced to consumer-law logic. It means, rather, a systematic reading compatible with the Constitution. The CDC does not eliminate intellectual property. Intellectual property does not suspend the CDC. What is required is a coexistence governed by criteria of material harmonization, in which exclusivity and consumer protection are read as institutes that p. 739 share the same constitutional space and, for that reason, cannot be interpreted as cancelling each other out. In ecosystems of governed enjoyment, this harmonization requires recognizing that the exercise of exclusivity becomes legally more intense, more structural and more consequential for the consumer's experience. Precisely for that reason, it also becomes more subject to review as to its effects.

The parameter, therefore, affirms a thesis that is simple in its formulation and severe in its consequences. Intellectual property protects the ecosystem, but does not absolve its governance. Where there is conflict between the concrete way exclusivity is exercised and the duties of loyalty, transparency, equivalence and restoration proper to the IOC regime, the solution must be sought through the functional reconciliation of the institutes, under the primacy of Brazilian constitutional limits and of the social function that shapes the legitimate use of proprietary positions. This is how exclusivity remains protected without turning into private sovereignty over the consumption experience.
