6.2.3 On the Immaterial Object of Consumption as a concretization of the immaterial product of Article 3, paragraph 1 of the Consumer Protection Code

See this section in the PDF

Concepts in this section

  • Immaterial Object of Consumption (IOC)

  • Dogmatics · Provisions cited

p. 574

Art. 3, paragraph 1, of the CDC already offers a sufficient basis for understanding the IOC as an immaterial product. The law does not make the product conditional on corporeality, nor does it tie it to the image of a tangible thing delivered once and for all. By expressly admitting material and immaterial goods, the system is already open enough to encompass objects of consumption whose utility is organized in a digital environment and realized through continued enjoyment. Reading the IOC this way, therefore, does not project a foreign category onto the Code. It makes explicit a dogmatic possibility already contained in the legal text itself, and dispels the idea that digital consumption would always depend on precarious analogies with traditional material media (Nunes, 2018, [p. 96; p. 160]638; Nery Jr., 2011, p. 544-546639).

This legal openness matters because, in digital ecosystems, the object of consumption is not exhausted by abstract availability or by formally completed delivery. Its legal existence, as an object of consumption, depends on governed conditions of enjoyment. What the consumer acquires is not merely nominal access to an item or to an isolated utility, but a position of enjoyment whose functionality depends on maintenance, updating, modulation, intelligibility and continuity. The tradition of the CDC itself already allows this reading, because the p. 575 core of the product category lies not in tangibility, but in utility economically organized to satisfy human needs Filomeno(Filomeno, 2018, [p. 108-109; p. 111- 113])640. Consequently, the legal standing of the IOC does not arise from an exercise in fiction. It follows from the very normative structure of the immaterial product.

That is also why the contractual form of the license is not enough to dissolve the legal density of the acquired enjoyment. The Terms of Service of Riot Games expressly recognize a core of “Virtual Content” associated with the account, made up of in-game currency, skins, emotes, accessories and other items, and admit that this content may be formed through purchase, earning, gifting or other internal dynamics of the ecosystem. This shows that the supplier itself already works with an identifiable virtual object functionally integrated into the consumption environment. The IOC category starts from this fact, but puts it back on the right ground. What matters is not merely the existence of a virtual asset associated with the account, but the fact that its utility only stabilizes through the environment that makes it enjoyable. The clause helps prove the existence of the object. It does not exhaust its regime.

The proposed reconstruction, then, is functional. The CDC protects not only an isolated “object”, but also the conditions of enjoyment, access, maintenance and intelligibility that make it effectively consumable. In contemporary consumer relationships, what is offered to the consumer is a functional bundle of utilities, qualities, modes of performance, expectations and annexes that make up the very legal experience of the performance. The contractual object cannot remain tied to the image of the isolated material good, because protection already applies also to quality, functionality, information, status and security ensured in the relationship Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 180; p. 183)641. It is exactly in this key that the IOC becomes p. 576 intelligible. It legally names an object whose relevance lies not in a static existence, but in the enjoyment concretely organized and maintained over time.

This does not require a break with the grammar of the CDC. On the contrary, it stems from its evolutionary and functional use in the face of contemporary forms of supply. The system does not need to be replaced or remade. It needs to be read in close fit with the real problem it faces. The contemporary consumer also accumulates immaterial goods linked to leisure, security, information, status and well-being, and consumer law already has instruments to offer reasonable answers to these cases without abandoning its own protective matrix Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 70-71; p. 104)642. The IOC, thus, is not a heterodox graft. It is an internal development of the system itself, built on the immaterial product the law already provides for.

The doctrine of digital assets helps to confirm this starting point, because it blocks any relapse into a dogmatics dependent on physical tangibility. Information, provided it carries appreciable utility, may be treated as a legal good and as an immaterial good. This recognition is important for the thesis because it rules out denying the legal relevance of the techno-digital object and reinscribes its legal status on the plane of consumption Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 52; p. 55; p. 71)643. But the inflection of the IOC begins precisely where the civil-law framework no longer suffices. The debate no longer centers on the existence or availability of the asset and turns instead to the way this object is offered, governed and enjoyed in continuous consumer relationships.

This shift is decisive because certain digital assets already admit, in their very nature, multiple enjoyment and complex legal relationships, which weakens any entirely static understanding of the problem. Information need not be thought of on the basis p. 577 of a rigid model of exclusivity, and the very idea of property can be read as a relationship conditioned by social function and by internal limits of legitimacy Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 50; p. 70)644. The IOC takes advantage of this opening, but shifts it to the field that matters. The question ceases to be only what exists legally and becomes how the enjoyment of this object is preserved, degraded, restored or made opaque to the consumer.

It is at this point that offer, quality, adequacy and trust come to operate in an especially important way. The CDC does not separate the offer from the product as if the former were an irrelevant promotional accessory and the latter an autonomous entity already closed in itself. Nonconformity with the offer and with advertising suffices to characterize consumer-law inadequacy, and quality defects by inadequacy are always measured in accordance with the consumer's legitimate expectation Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 252; p. 326-327])645. In IOCs, this means that the promised enjoyment cannot be reduced to the mere formal permanence of access. The immaterial object is also measured by the functional correspondence between promised utility and utility actually maintained in the course of the relationship.

The very breadth of the concept of product reinforces this reading. The CDC adopted a contemporary and broad vocabulary, directly linked to the results of production and circulation in the market, and not to the narrow category of the corporeal thing. The application of the consumer-law regime has always been explained by the placing of products and services on the market and by the consumption pole, not by a requirement of physical materiality of the supplied object (Nunes, 2018, [p. p. 578 89; p. 99])646. The interpretive tradition of the Code, therefore, already admitted incorporeal utilities and complex technological systems, including in contexts in which the acquirer's technical vulnerability justified legal protection Filomeno(Filomeno, 2018, [p. 131; p. 267; p. 660])647. The IOC category merely makes this potentiality more precise for cases of governed enjoyment.

This precision also appears when one observes the link between immaterial product, trust and functional adequacy. Organized information can be understood as digital goods offered as products in the consumer market, and protection is anchored less in the static existence of the object than in the utility the consumer relies on and legitimately expects Miragem(Miragem, 2020, [p. 78; p. 152])648. In products that depend on continuous services, the offer becomes the main criterion for measuring the legitimate expectation of use, and the duty to inform becomes decisive for assessing defect and adequacy Miragem(Miragem, 2020, [p. 87; p. 523])649. The IOC comes into focus precisely because it concretizes the immaterial product in a key oriented by the functional integrity of the promised enjoyment.

p. 579 For this reason, the continuity of the bond and architectural opacity increase the weight of duties of predictability, reasoning and minimum verifiability. In a continuous consumption environment, protection applies not only to an isolated object, but to the conditions that make it consumable, because it is in them that the mechanisms of asymmetry and the points of possible relevant frustration are concentrated.

The IOC makes it possible to translate the phenomenon into manageable dogmatic language. It is not a matter of creating a “new CDC”, but of making explicit how the system already offers tools to control unilateral reconfigurations and practices of private governance when they impact adequacy, trust and restoration. It is precisely this reconstruction that will open space, in the following sections, for open auditability and functional value equivalence as standards for controlling administered mutability.

The methodological consequence of the item, then, is simple. The IOC does not replace the immaterial product of art. 3, paragraph 1. It concretizes it in a context of continuous and governed consumption. This prevents the object from being reduced to a technically granted and legally disposable permission, and also keeps the debate from being held captive by a static ontology of the digital asset. What the CDC protects here is not only the abstract existence of the object, but the acquired enjoyment in its functional, informational and relational integrity. This is the point that will later make it possible to deal with updating, modulation, downgrading and restoration without leaving the grammar of the system itself.

Notes

  1. The incorporation of immaterial goods into the CDC's field of application is a possibility already inscribed in the legal framework of art. 3, paragraph 1, itself, whose wording consciously abandoned the narrower civil-law vocabulary of the “thing” to adopt the contemporary category of “product”, directly linked to the results of production and circulation in the consumer market. The CDC, following the contemporary concept, employs the term “product” and, in its definition, includes “any good”, designating it as “movable or immovable” and also “material or immaterial” Nunes(Nunes, 2018, [p. 96]). This doctrinal record is relevant because it shows that the recognition of immaterial objects of consumption does not represent a graft foreign to the system, but a coherent development of a possibility already expressly admitted by the law. The author himself observes that “product” already admits the categories “movable, immovable, material and immaterial” provided for in art. 3, §1 Nunes(Nunes, 2018, [p. 160]). ↩
  2. Nery Jr. (2011, p. 544) also observes that art. 3, paragraph 1, of the CDC allows the product to be read beyond corporeal materiality by conferring a legal regime of their own on products, defined as “any good, movable or immovable, material or immaterial”, which he considers a deliberately broader notion than that of “good” or “thing” in the Civil Code. The CDC does not make the configuration of the product conditional on physicality, and works with a category capable of encompassing incorporeal utilities placed on the consumer market. This openness takes concrete form, for example, in treating bank credit as an immaterial product subject to the elements of the consumer relationship Jr.(Nery Jr., 2011, p. 546). The CDC was not guided by the criteria of the Civil Code, but created its own system in which both materially consumable goods and legally consumable goods may form part of relationships subject to the Code Jr.(Nery Jr., 2011, p. 545). Reading the IOC as a concretization of the immaterial product makes dogmatically explicit a potentiality contained in the normative vocabulary of the system itself. ↩
  3. Just as what qualifies the object of consumer-law protection is not its corporeality, but its appropriable economic utility intended for the satisfaction of human needs, the doctrinal tradition understands the economic good on the basis of its aptitude to form part of relationships of circulation in the market, shifting the focus from tangibility to economic function Filomeno(Filomeno, 2018, [p. 108-109]). This openness is reflected in the recognition of immaterial utilities, such as bank credit, as legitimate products within the scope of the CDC Filomeno(Filomeno, 2018, [p. 111-113]). The reading of the IOC as a concretization of the immaterial product does not break with the classic ground of doctrine, it merely carries forward an openness that doctrine itself already recognized. ↩
  4. The identification of the contractual object of consumption within the scope of the CDC cannot remain tied to the traditional image of the isolated material good, as if legal protection applied only to a static thing detachable from its context of performance. In contemporary consumer relationships, what is offered to the consumer is a functional bundle of utilities, qualities, expectations and modes of performance that make up the very legal experience of the performance. Marques summarizes this shift by stating that, in the consumer contract, "what becomes important is the nexus of the performances and their balance (synalagma), not the performance itself, but its annexes, its quality, its functionality, the information that accompanies it, the status it ensures, the speed and the security of its rendering." Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 180). The CDC system, supported by the theory of the social function of the contract, concentrates on the effect of the contract, so that the service rendered must be adequate for the purposes reasonably expected of it Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 183). This reinforces that the IOC can be understood as a contractual object of consumption precisely because its legal status emerges from the concrete way in which the performance is organized, made available, maintained and enjoyed. ↩
  5. The construction of the IOC as a concretization of the immaterial product provided for in art. 3, paragraph 1, of the CDC requires neither a rupture with the consumer-law grammar nor an artificial importation of categories foreign to the system. On the contrary, it stems from an evolutionary and functional reading of the microsystem itself, oriented by the need to understand contemporary forms of supply without abdicating the legal instruments already available for consumer protection. It is in this direction that Marques states that the adequate answer lies not in "proclaiming the uselessness of the traditional scientific instruments of law", but in the "use of the legal instruments we have, in particular the new Consumer Protection Code and the constitutional lines, so as to give reasonable and just answers to concrete cases" Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 70-71). The contemporary consumer tends to become an accumulator also of services, doings and "immaterial goods" linked to well-being, security, leisure, status, information and credit, which requires the law to keep pace with this change Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 104). In this key, the IOC appears as an internal and coherent development of the system itself. ↩
  6. The recognition of the legal status of the digital asset constitutes an important starting point for the construction of the IOC, because it guards against relapses into a myopic dogmatics that would only grant legal density to the corporeal. Information, provided it carries appreciable utilities, may be treated as a legal good and, by meeting the conditions for the recognition of a new category of immaterial goods, blocks the denial of its relevance in a digital environment Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 52; p. 55; p. 71). This methodological continuity allows the thesis to maintain the legal recognition of the immaterial object while shifting the focus of the debate: instead of centering on the ownership or availability of the good, the problem turns to the way this object is offered, governed and enjoyed in continuous consumer relationships. The shift to the IOC does not deny the legal status of the digital asset, but reinscribes it in a key of its own linked to art. 3, paragraph 1, of the CDC, and becomes necessary when the debate reaches the functional integrity and the functional restoration of the consumed object. ↩
  7. The doctrine of digital assets itself offers elements that help shift the debate beyond the pure grammar of ownership by recognizing that certain goods admit multiple enjoyment. Information, as a good of plural enjoyment, makes it possible to overcome the classic view according to which a good could only be thought of on the basis of the exclusive enjoyment of a single subject Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 50). The same holds for the understanding of property as a complex legal relationship involving duties that condition the legitimacy of the exercise of the right, among them the social function Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 70). This inflection shows that the passage to the IOC does not depend on denying the doctrine of digital assets, but on carrying it to the point at which the pure language of ownership already proves insufficient. When the focus falls on how enjoyment is governed and altered in continuous consumer relationships, the IOC category is justified in order to shift the debate from the existence of the asset to the functional integrity of the consumed experience. ↩
  8. In the CDC, product, offer, quality and adequacy make up a single protective logic, which reinforces the dogmatic plausibility of treating the IOC as a concretization of the immaterial product. The Manual shows this when commenting on art. 18 and highlighting that the system recognizes not only defects that render the product unfit for consumption or diminish its value, but also defects arising from the disparity between its real characteristics and those conveyed in the offer and in advertising Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 326]). The dogmatic consequence is decisive: the offer does not hover outside the product as an irrelevant promotional detail, but forms part of the very legal criterion for assessing its quality and adequacy. The Manual further observes that, in this third kind of defect, nonconformity with the offer suffices to characterize consumer-law inadequacy, even if there is no intrinsic defect in the good and no immediate material reduction of its value Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 326-327]). Reinforcing this, quality defects by inadequacy are described as nonconformities relating to the performance of the product or service, to the fulfillment of its purpose and also to its durability, always “in accordance with the consumer's legitimate expectation” Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 252]). ↩
  9. The legal framework of the CDC proves broad enough to admit non-corporeal objects of consumption without any need for interpretive rupture. The application of the consumer-law regime is explained by the consumption pole and the placing of products and services on the market, and not by a requirement that the supplied object be physically material. The system regulates the products and services offered and sold on the market, a logic that holds both for corporeal goods and for supplied water and electricity or for money lent by a bank Nunes(Nunes, 2018, [p. 89]). Along the same lines, commenting on the legal enumeration of banking and financial services, Nunes notes that the express designation of these activities serves to prevent certain economic agents from escaping the scope of application of the CDC, and does not signal doubt about the immaterial nature of this type of service Nunes(Nunes, 2018, [p. 99]). This helps to show that the thesis does not inject a foreign body into the CDC; it merely develops a possibility contained in it. ↩
  10. The reading of the product in the CDC is not limited to corporeal goods, since the system itself recognizes that consumer relationships encompass movable and immovable, material and immaterial goods Filomeno(Filomeno, 2018, [p. 267]). The openness of art. 3 allows protection to apply to economically organized incorporeal utilities, as can be seen in the application of the Code to complex technological systems and software maintenance, in which the acquirer's technical vulnerability justifies legal protection Filomeno(Filomeno, 2018, [p. 131; 660]). Treating the IOC as a concretization of the immaterial product does not mean doing violence to the legal text, but making explicit a possibility already incorporated into the interpretive tradition of the consumer-law microsystem. ↩
  11. The reading of the IOC as a concretization of the immaterial product does not introduce a foreign body into the CDC, but makes explicit a normative potential already contained in art. 3, paragraph 1. Organized information can be understood as digital goods over which ownership and offer fall in the form of products that, under the terms of the CDC, may be immaterial goods Miragem(Miragem, 2020, [p. 78]). The importance of this breadth grows in the face of the economic growth of goods produced exclusively by means of computing, a scenario in which the legislator anticipated the regulation of the internet by making consumer protection norms fully applicable Miragem(Miragem, 2020, [p. 152]). The value of the approach lies in recognizing the functional breadth of the concept of product without imprisoning it in physical corporeality, allowing the CDC to treat techno-digital objects within a consumer relationship, shifting the focus from ownership to legally protected enjoyment. ↩
  12. By associating product, offer, trust and adequacy in a functional key, Miragem helps decisively to show that the IOC is not a foreign graft in the CDC, but an internal development of its protective logic. The author observes that, although distinct as to the specific duties imputed to the supplier, both liability for the accident and liability for the defect of the product or service are linked to the so-called theory of quality, which, in turn, is anchored in the protection of the consumer's trust. It is precisely this foundation that imposes on the supplier a general duty to offer products and services that are safe and adequate for the purposes intended for them Miragem(Miragem, 2020, [p. 523]). The notion of product in the CDC is not exhausted in the static identification of a good, but projects itself functionally onto the utility entrusted to and legitimately expected by the consumer. Miragem's reading becomes even more relevant when dealing with new products dependent on services provided by the supplier, continuous or not, under the assertion that, in these cases, the terms of the offer become the main criterion for gauging the consumer's legitimate expectations. According to the author, the fulfillment or non-fulfillment of the duty to provide correct, clear, precise and conspicuous information about the characteristics and qualities of the product is what will measure the consumer's legitimate expectation as to its use, including for purposes of contractual binding and of the supplier's liability under arts. 18 and 20 of the CDC Miragem(Miragem, 2020, [p. 87]). That is, the adequacy of the object is not read as an abstract quality enclosed in its materiality or in its nominal existence, but as a functional correspondence between the utility promised in the offer and the enjoyment actually delivered. It is precisely this perspective that makes the CDC speak of an immaterial product not only to open the doors of the system to incorporeal objects; but also so that its protection is organized on the basis of trust, offer, adequacy and restoration. Miragem thus helps to shift the debate from a civil-law ontology of the good to a functional reading of the object of consumption, in which what is relevant is not only the legal existence of the asset, but its aptitude to achieve, reliably and adequately, the purposes legitimately projected by the consumer. By concretizing the immaterial product of art. 3, paragraph 1, the IOC is consolidated through a consumer-law perspective oriented not by the mere ownership of the asset, but by the functional integrity of the promised enjoyment. ↩
