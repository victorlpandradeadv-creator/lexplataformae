6.1.3 On personal integrity, symbolic identity and biographical continuity as reference legal interests

p. 534

The continued enjoyment of the Immaterial Object of Consumption cannot be adequately grasped when reduced to the language of immediate utility or of the price paid. In platform-governed ecosystems, what the consumer maintains over time is not only functional access to a digital performance, but a position of enjoyment that incorporates history, memory, recognition, identity projection and practical continuity of presence in the environment. The corresponding legal protection, therefore, cannot be blind to the circumstance that certain losses, erasures, sanctions and unilateral reconfigurations reach not only the economic value of the acquisition, but also dimensions of personal integrity and symbolic identity that become objectified in the very course of the digital experience. The rereading of private law in light of human dignity long ago broke with the narrow view that confined the legal problem to the patrimonial value of the good affected, requiring attention to the existential projections of the person in the social world Schreiber(Schreiber, 2014, p. 10; p. 17)561.

p. 535 This does not mean dissolving the analysis into academic sentimentalism, an elegant shortcut for thinking ill of the one whose conduct is now being questioned. The question at hand is more sober. In platform environments, the consumer's personality is cut up into data, profiles, histories, metrics, reputations and trails of participation that condition concrete possibilities of permanence, recognition and self-determination. The digital experience may reach legally relevant dimensions of personal integrity precisely because data protection, informational self-determination and the free development of personality form part of the existential core of the person in an informational environment Laprano(Canavez; Andrade; Laprano, 2021, p. 139; p. 148; p. 155-156)562. That is why the administration of these elements cannot be treated as a simple “data issue” set apart from the consumer relation. It forms part of the very regime of enjoyment and interferes directly with the consumer's informational and biographical integrity.

This key makes it possible to formulate, in more rigorous terms, the idea of personal integrity in the consumption of IOCs. It is not exhausted in physical or psychological integrity in the classical sense, but reaches informational and biographical integrity, because the subject also comes to project themselves socially through their digital records, their accumulated trajectory and their recognizable position in the ecosystem. Data, records, profiles and histories are not only economically exploitable informational collections; they function as a direct representation of personality and, to a certain extent, as its extension in the digital environment Doneda(Doneda, 2020, p. 146-147; p. 149)563. In contemporary terms, this means recognizing that the so-called digital dossiers p. 536 constitute a new type of identity and that personal data are connected to the free exercise and development of the human personality Laprano(Canavez; Andrade; Laprano, 2021, p. 141; p. 153)564. Consequently, erasures, blocks, resets and suppressions of trajectory cannot automatically be reduced to mere patrimonial loss.

This perception has an older dogmatic basis than is sometimes admitted. The legal protection of the person is not exhausted in protection against physical aggression or economic loss, reaching also intellectual, emotional and biographical life. The old formulation of Warren and Brandeis is useful here precisely because it shows that the law, over time, had to recognize that thoughts, emotions, sensations and other immaterial forms of existence also call for legal protection, not on the exclusive basis of property, but on the broader idea of inviolate personality Brandeis(Warren; Brandeis, [201-?], [p. 2-3; p. 10])565. This historical p. 537 opening matters because it legitimizes the passage from the patrimonial to the existential without turning theoretical reflection into delirium. Thus, what is asserted is only that the integrity of the consumer may include the preservation of minimum conditions of biographical continuity and stability of symbolic insertion, and not only the refund of a frustrated purchase.

In continuous services, this takes on very concrete contours. The consumer's trajectory is not a mere passive “record” kept by the platform, nor an informational deposit without a subject. It is a biographical result produced by repeated choices, performance, investment of time, acquisition, cooperation, accumulation of signs of belonging and permanence in the ecosystem. Even though this construction takes place under the mediation of the Lex Plataformae, it remains attributable to the consumer as an expression of biographical continuity within the supply regime. For this reason, certain IOCs operate as trajectory assets and not merely as one-off utilities. What accumulates is a bundle of memory, identity and recognition, so that its unilateral suppression or reclassification cannot be treated as a simple irrelevant technical variation. Data protection, moreover, becomes insufficient when read in a purely patrimonial frame, because this subordinates the person to the market value of their own information and lowers the priority of the development of personality Doneda(Doneda, 2020, p. 145-146; p. 269; p. 288)566.

At this point, the value of the IOC must also be reconstructed as symbolic architecture. Certain objects and trajectory marks are worth less for what they “do” in utilitarian terms and more for what they mean in the ecosystem: status, rarity, belonging, memory of participation, distinction and social recognition. What is consumed, then, is not only functional performance, but also symbolic position. The civil protection of personality reaches precisely the way in which the person is projected, recognized and situated in the social milieu, p. 538 which is why reputation, honor and identity receive protection not because they are simple intimate feelings, but because they make up objective relational dimensions of the person's life Schreiber(Schreiber, 2014, p. 71; p. 211)567. In digital ecosystems, the loss of an account, of history, of accumulated signs and of continuity of trajectory may, therefore, transcend economic utility and touch legally cognizable reputational and identity dimensions.

The formulation is reinforced when one observes that persistent records and social classifications can compress the singularity of the person and affect the way they are apprehended and positioned in the social world. The protection of identity does not protect only formal signs, but the faithful presentation of the person in their singularity, preventing compressions, falsifications and fixations produced by third parties Schreiber(Schreiber, 2014, p. 155-156; p. 216)568. On platforms, this means that the history of enjoyment, the signs of belonging and the accumulated marks of participation are not merely technical remnants of the experience; they take part in the way the consumer is recognized within the environment and, in certain cases, outside it. The IOC comes to operate as a biographical and identity asset.

Here too the informational background matters. Data, traces and monitoring concern the personal integrity of the user in a digital environment because they take part in the way their social and individual identity is projected, organized and exposed. Data protection must consider the physical and electronic body of the subject, and not only the abstract circulation of information Costa(Costa, 2018, p. 41; p. 44; p. 47)569. For this reason digital traces cannot be p. 539 reduced to economically exploitable assets, since they interfere with social classification, autonomy and self-determination, and are more adequately understood as a dimension of personality itself Costa(Costa, 2018, p. 44; p. 47)570. The digital environment, in short, is not only a market; it is also a space of identity projection and of existential impact.

On the consumer-law plane, the argument must keep its feet on the ground. Contractual and consumer-law protection is not exhausted in the control of price or in the correction of narrow patrimonial imbalances. In relationships marked by vulnerability, dependence and continued trust, the rebalancing that can be required must extend to loyal and dignified treatment and protect legitimate expectations that go beyond the mere economic account Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 290; p. 1481)571. This is the limit that prevents the existential aspect from drifting away from consumer law. The CDC already offers sufficient grammar to recognize that the injury may reach quality of life, trust and the practical integrity of the consumption experience, and not only the consumer's pocket in the narrow sense.

This broadening of protection has direct repercussions on legal remedies. When the consumer relationship frustrates legitimate expectations as to the adequacy, utility, safety or p. 540 quality of the performance, the consumer-law system is not automatically oriented toward the residual monetization of the loss. It seeks, in the first place, to materially restore the consumer's position, satisfy their interests and avoid greater harm, including in a non-patrimonial dimension Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 1383; p. 856)572. Hence the importance of Article 35 of the CDC573, which allows one to demand forced performance of the obligation, accept an equivalent performance or rescind the contract with losses and damages, and of Article 84574, which enshrines specific relief of the obligation and a practical result equivalent to performance. In long-lasting IOCs, these provisions support the primacy of functional restoration, and not the automatic conversion of every loss into money. First one tries to restore the position of enjoyment; only afterwards is an alternative for reparation considered.

This point is crucial in view of the platform's own contractual frame. Clause 2.3 of Riot's Terms of Service provides that, once the account is terminated, the user loses access to the account, to the associated data, to the in-game currency and to virtual content, without refund in cases of just cause, with the further possibility of cancellation of other accounts and of access to other services575. The clause is valuable because it lays the problem bare: what is lost is not only an isolated item, p. 541 but the overall position of enjoyment, the history, the inventory, the trajectory and the practical continuity of insertion in the ecosystem. Exclusivity is exercised, here, as a power of complete erasure of the consumed experience, which prevents treating sanction, exclusion or rollback as mere internal adjustments without existential legal relevance.

It is precisely for this reason that the subsection works with the idea of a digital existential minimum. The expression does not serve to rhetorically inflate any online discomfort into the category of constitutional drama. Its function is more restrained: to designate a core of stability and continuity that can be required in long-lasting IOCs with a high identity load, such as account, history, progression, reputation and inventory, so as to prevent unilateral erasures and reconfigurations from being naturalized as “mere internal policy” immune to control. In qualified cases, the loss of an account, history or trajectory may constitute moral damages for injury to personality, especially when it affects in an objectifiable way biographical continuity, institutionalized belonging in the ecosystem or relevant anchors of reputation and memory, and not mere subjective annoyance. Here, moreover, the methodological parallel with aesthetic damage is useful: Brazilian law already knows movements of categorial refinement to prevent injuries to the person from being swallowed by overly generic headings, which legitimizes, by methodological analogy, greater analytical density for injuries to the digital trajectory and to biographical continuity Lopez(Lopez, 2021, p. 31-33; p. 78)576.

The institutional consequence of this reading falls on the platform's own governance. If it administers access, permanence, visibility, sanctions and paths of participation, then it also administers inclusion and exclusion in the digital social space. From this follows the need for minimum parameters of private due process: verifiable reasoning, decisional coherence, procedural predictability and intelligible avenues of challenge. This requirement does not derive from the company's benevolence, but from the public relevance of the effects produced by its governance on the memory, reputation, continuity of trajectory and symbolic p. 542 position of the consumer. The administration of someone else's digital biography cannot operate as a sovereign black box.

At this point, the LGPD provides important reinforcements. The use of data linked to the consumer's trajectory, history and digital biography must remain compatible with the stated purposes and with the context of the relationship577, must be intelligible to the data subject578, and does not eliminate the rights to confirmation of existence, access, correction, deletion or portability of processed data579. It also presupposes secure management of relevant records and trails580. These provisions do not turn the consumer into the absolute owner of everything the platform records, but they reinforce that their digital biography is not raw mass entirely unavailable to the data subject and that the administration of the memory of enjoyment must remain controllable, intelligible and minimally accessible.

There is also a systemic parallel especially useful for preventing simplistic objections. The Copyright Law recognizes, in Article 24, item VII, the author's moral right to access a single and rare copy of the work that is legitimately in the possession of another in order to preserve its memory581. The point is not to turn the consumer into an author, nor to transplant the regime of authorial moral rights into the consumer-law field. The point is narrower and more elegant: the Brazilian legal order already admits, in another domain, that the preservation of memory linked to an immaterial object under the control of a third party may justify minimum positive duties of access and of enabling a record. This suffices to dismiss the superficial objection according to which the absence of full ownership would, by itself, eliminate any legal relevance of interests of memory, preservation and continuity.

This parallel even helps to prepare the fit with the discussion of Intellectual Property developed further on. If the platform depends on the continuous participation of the consumer to produce economic value and vitality of the ecosystem, then there is a core of trajectory and memory produced in the relationship that cannot be treated as disposable residue p. 543 of supply. The consequence is not to make the consumer an author, but to recognize that erasures, resets, inventory exclusions, suppressions of history and sanctions that eliminate the biography built in the environment require verifiable reasoning, minimum preservation of records and, where appropriate, restoration through reinstatement of the position of enjoyment. What is lost, in these cases, is not only access, but biographical continuity objectified within the governed regime itself.

With this, the subsection fixes the reference legal interests that make the IOC legally visible beyond immediate exchange value. The protected core of enjoyment comes to include personal integrity and symbolic identity, objectified in history, reputation, memory, belonging and continuity of trajectory. The signs of relevant degradation also become clearer: reputational exclusion, loss of functional equivalence, access restrictions, erasure of history and unilateral mutability of structuring elements of the consumer's position. Protection ceases to operate under an institutional blindness that converts every conflict into price and residual compensation and comes to admit, on the basis of the system itself, remedies of functional restoration, preservation of trails, access to records, procedural correction and restoration of the position of enjoyment whenever this is possible and legally due. This is the basis that will allow, further on, the formulation of open auditability, the distribution of burdens and finer standards of restoration in the regime of the IOC.

The dispute over losses, blocks or suppressions in digital environments need not remain imprisoned in the language of patrimonial loss or of simple non-performance, because contemporary civil protection already recognizes that certain injuries reach the person in an existentially relevant dimension, and not only the things he possesses. The rereading of private law in light of human dignity breaks precisely with the excessively patrimonialist perspective that confined the legal problem to the economic value of the affected good, requiring attention also to the existential projections of the person in the social world Schreiber(Schreiber, 2014, p. 10). When records, signs, usage trajectories, digital historicity and symbolic belongings come to structure objectifiable forms of personal continuity, the corresponding impairment no longer fits comfortably within the narrow grammar of the lost price. This key becomes even sharper when the injury is understood not from the suffering subjectively experienced, but from the attribute of personality objectively affected. The advantage of this formulation lies precisely in preventing the protection of the person from depending on sentimental proof or on dramatization of the harm, concentrating instead on the legally protected projection that was violated Schreiber(Schreiber, 2014, p. 17). This is precisely what matters here: in continuous digital ecosystems, there may be a legally cognizable impairment of dimensions of personality mediated by records, positions of enjoyment, objectified memory and signs of insertion in the environment. ↩
  2. The digital experience may reach legally relevant dimensions of personal integrity that go beyond strict patrimonial interests, because data protection, informational self-determination and the free development of personality make up the existential core of the person in an informational environment. Along these lines, the specific protection of personal data appears as a means of guaranteeing fundamental rights already enshrined, such as liberty and privacy, as well as others developed more recently, among them informational self-determination and the free development of personality Laprano(Canavez; Andrade; Laprano, 2021, p. 139). This articulation deepens when it is affirmed that informational self-determination is directed precisely at the free development of the personality of the data subject, which is why any interpretation that distorts it, for example through unrestricted consent harmful to that development, must be considered unacceptable Laprano(Canavez; Andrade; Laprano, 2021, p. 148). In reinforcement, informational self-determination and the centrality of the data subject appear as means of enhancing the free determination and free development of the personality of the natural person, giving him instruments to control the processing of his data, including as to confirmation of the existence of processing, correction, modification and deletion Laprano(Canavez; Andrade; Laprano, 2021, p. 155-156). The integrity of the person, in digital environments, may thus involve not only patrimonial goods, but also informational integrity, biographical continuity and minimum conditions of personal self-determination. ↩
  3. Digital data, records, profiles and histories do not constitute only economically exploitable informational collections. By reason of the privileged bond between personal information and the subject to whom it refers, these elements function as a direct representation of his personality and, to that extent, as a true extension of it Doneda(Doneda, 2020, p. 146-147). When the digital environment operates on histories, traces, profiles and personal records, it is not merely manipulating neutral content or abstract utilities, but acting upon legally qualified projections of the person himself. This impact intensifies when the profile built from the data becomes the main visible aspect of the individual before third parties. In these cases, the electronic representation may function as a true virtual persona, merging, in certain circumstances, with the very person to whom it refers Doneda(Doneda, 2020, p. 149). The reference to the electronic body, to the digital persona or to the avatar expresses the mutation according to which the person comes to project himself in the digital environment through data, histories and records whose manipulation, blocking, distortion or suppression affects not only economic interests, but also identity, biographical continuity and personal position in the ecosystem Doneda(Doneda, 2020, p. 149). For this reason, in long-lasting immaterial objects of consumption, erasures, sanctions and losses of trajectory cannot automatically be reduced to mere patrimonial loss. ↩
  4. The digital environment cannot be read only as a space for the economic circulation of assets, contracts and data, because injuries that fall directly on the person in his existential dimension are also projected in it. The repersonalization of private law, in this context, places back at the center the tension between the demands of an impersonal, economically oriented market and the need to protect the fundamental rights of the natural person subjected to external influences Laprano(Canavez; Andrade; Laprano, 2021, p. 141). This reading gains density when it is recognized that data processing activities have a growing interference in people's lives and that the so-called digital dossiers constitute a “new type of identity”, which is why the correction of this information becomes essential for the identity of the data subject to be projected faithfully, dogmatically justifying the inclusion of personal data in the category of personality rights Laprano(Canavez; Andrade; Laprano, 2021, p. 153). In reinforcement, personal data are qualified as a “true extension of the free exercise and development of the human personality”, so that informational self-determination is not justified by mere ownership of data, but by its indissoluble relationship with the personality of the data subject Laprano(Canavez; Andrade; Laprano, 2021, p. 153). Thus, in governed digital environments, the injury may fall not only on patrimony or immediate economic utility, but on informational integrity, identity projection, biographical continuity and the development of personality. ↩
  5. Here, Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis offer a classical foundation that is particularly useful for showing that the legal protection of the person is not exhausted by protection against physical aggression or patrimonial loss. In reconstructing the historical evolution of rights, the authors show that the law progressively came to recognize the spiritual, intellectual and sensitive dimension of human existence, extending the reach of legal protection beyond corporeal things. As they state, there was recognition of man's “spiritual nature”, of his “feelings and his intellect”, so that the right to life itself came to mean also “the right to enjoy life”, while property expanded to encompass “intangible, as well as tangible” forms of possession Brandeis(Warren; Brandeis, [201-?], [p. 2]). This movement appears even more explicitly when they argue that the intensification of intellectual and emotional life made it evident that only part of the pain, pleasure and profit of life lies in physical things, which is why “thoughts, emotions, and sensations demanded legal recognition” Brandeis(Warren; Brandeis, [201-?], [p. 3]). In addition, in examining the protection of personal writings and productions, they state that the corresponding protective principle rests not, properly speaking, on private property, but on an “inviolate personality” Brandeis(Warren; Brandeis, [201-?], [p. 10]). The integrity of the consumer, in continuous digital ecosystems, may comprise the preservation of minimum conditions of biographical continuity, built trajectory, symbolic insertion and stability of enjoyment, without thereby dissolving the analysis into psychologism or depriving the CDC of its protective centrality. ↩
  6. The protection of personal data does not fit adequately within a purely patrimonial frame. Treating personal information as a simple economic good or as a circulable asset subject to a proprietary logic impoverishes protection precisely because it subordinates the person to the market value of his own data, when what is at stake is a much broader existential dimension Doneda(Doneda, 2020, p. 145-146). The insufficiency of this reading becomes clear once one observes that, in many contemporary social relationships, the individual is recognized only indirectly, through the representation of his personality that his personal data provide Doneda(Doneda, 2020, p. 269). The legal problem, therefore, is not only the economic use of information, but the projection of the person's identity through it. For this reason, a predominantly proprietary protection of personal data comes into tension with its qualification as a fundamental right, since it subjects the debate to paradigms that weaken the priority of the development of personality and reduce the person to the status of an object of informational circulation Doneda(Doneda, 2020, p. 288). As a consequence, histories, profiles, records and other digital traces must be protected not only because they have economic value or business utility, but because they make up the identity, biographical continuity and informational integrity of the subject in a digital environment. ↩
  7. The civil protection of personality reaches not only subjective interiorities, but also the way in which the person is projected, recognized and situated in the social milieu. Reputation and honor receive their own legal protection: not because they represent simple intimate feelings, but because they make up objective relational dimensions of the person's life, socially relevant and normatively protected Schreiber(Schreiber, 2014, p. 71). In digital environments, the loss of an account, of history, of accumulated signs, of relational position or of continuity of trajectory may, in certain cases, transcend the language of economic utility and touch legally cognizable dimensions of reputation and identity. This conclusion is reinforced when personal identity is treated not as merely formal data, but as protection of the right to be oneself in the social space, that is, of respect for the traits, experiences, convictions and marks that set the person apart before others Schreiber(Schreiber, 2014, p. 211). The point carries particular weight here because it allows us to understand that certain digitally mediated elements cease to be perceived only as isolated assets or functionalities and come to make up forms of the person's social presentation. ↩
  8. The persistence, organization and manipulation of socially relevant records may have direct repercussions on the dignity and existential integrity of the person, because informational standardization tends to reduce his singularity to profiles, classifications and framings built by third parties. When data and records come to be used to fix representations of the individual, there is a civilly relevant risk of compression of his singularity and of impairment of the way he will be apprehended, treated and positioned in the social world Schreiber(Schreiber, 2014, p. 155-156). The legal problem of digital records is not limited to their economic circulation, since it also involves the protection of the person against fixations, distortions or losses that affect his social presentation and his biographical continuity. This perception fits well with the dynamic dimension of personal identity. The protection of identity does not protect only formal signs, but the faithful presentation of the person in his singularity, preventing falsifications or undue compressions of the elements by which he is individualized in the social whole Schreiber(Schreiber, 2014, p. 216). ↩
  9. Data, traces and monitoring do not concern only the abstract circulation of information on the network, but the very personal integrity of the user in a digital environment. Data protection must consider the social and individual identity of the user, as well as his “physical and electronic body”, precisely so that adequate means of protection can be built Costa(Costa, 2018, p. 41). This formulation shifts the debate from the purely technical-informational plane to the plane of the person, showing that processed data and captured traces do not hover outside the subject, but take part in the way his identity is projected, organized and exposed in the digital environment. The same reading is reinforced when data protection appears as an expression of the very liberty and dignity of the person, so that data cannot serve the objectification of the individual nor his transformation into a target of constant surveillance Costa(Costa, 2018, p. 44). In addition, the creation of mechanisms of knowledge and control over personal data constitutes a direct expression of the protection of personality rights, since, when the object of the data is a subject of rights, the information must be understood as an attribute of personality Costa(Costa, 2018, p. 47). ↩
  10. Traces, monitoring and data processing legally affect the integrity of the person because they interfere with his social classification, his autonomy and his power of self-determination in a digital environment. The protection of personal data may be understood as a new personality right, and not as a simple offshoot of a supposed patrimonial dominion of the individual over his data, which is why it proves inadequate to treat the person as a mere owner of information about himself Costa(Costa, 2018, p. 44). The consequence is direct: digital traces cannot be reduced to economically exploitable informational assets, since they bear on existential projections of the person himself. This reading is reinforced when the recognition of the protection of personal data as an autonomous and fundamental right appears linked precisely to the risks that automated processing imposes on personality rights, being grounded in substantive equality, liberty, the dignity of the human person and privacy Costa(Costa, 2018, p. 47). ↩
  11. Consumer-law protection is not exhausted by the control of price nor by the restoration of strict patrimonial imbalances. In relationships marked by vulnerability, dependence and continued trust, the legally required rebalancing cannot be limited to the economic account, because contractual injury may also reach legitimate expectations, dignified treatment and the practical integrity of the consumption experience. It is in this direction that Marques warns that "focusing only on the economic imbalance of the consumer contract would be a limited view of the notion of contractual equity", since what is sought is "the total rebalancing of the relationship, including its level of loyal and dignified treatment", the only way to maintain and protect the legitimate expectations of the parties Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 290). This broadening is decisive if the existential item is to remain anchored in the dogmatic ground of consumer law. In the same vein, the author observes that the principle of trust leads the CDC system to protect the adequacy, quality and reasonable safety of products and services, its ultimate purpose being "to improve the quality of life of Brazilians" Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 1481). Consumer protection thus also reaches injuries that go beyond narrow economic loss. ↩
  12. The protection of trust and the application of objective good faith also bear on the design of the available legal remedies themselves, ruling out the idea that every relevant contractual injury must be converted solely into patrimonial compensation. When the consumer relationship frustrates legitimate expectations as to the adequacy, utility, safety or quality of the performance, the consumer-law system seeks, first and foremost, to restore the consumer's position materially. Marques, in dealing with the legal guarantee of adequacy, stresses that it aims to "satisfy the interests" of the consumer, whether by "perfect performance of the obligation", by "rebalancing between the performances rendered", or by avoiding greater harm and compensating that already suffered, grounding this guarantee in the trust legitimately aroused and in the mandatory good faith of the supplier in the market Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 1383). The author reinforces this reading by arguing that, without an effective legal reaction to the frustration of trust and to the breach of ancillary duties, such commands would become "words in the wind", which is why the law of damages in the CDC fulfills not only a compensatory function, but also a preventive and satisfactory one, reaching even non-patrimonial damages and negative interests Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 856). This supports the opening of the system to remedies of restoration that are functionally richer than the mere monetization of the loss. ↩
