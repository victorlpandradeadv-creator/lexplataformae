Epigraph

Lorde, The Love Club (2012). What it announces · Read the epigraph in the PDF.

p. 21

There is a kind of consumption in which the subject believes they are walking on their own ground, but only moves within ground that is granted, revocable and fully reconfigurable. Access, permanence and even the meaning of what was acquired depend on a continuous administration that presents itself not as management, but as technical normality. The object is not valued only for what it is. It is valued for what it allows one to do, for what it signals, for the place it occupies in the ecosystem and for the way this ecosystem recognizes or erases its bearer. When everything changes and the consumer no longer feels like themselves, this is not subjective drama or mere annoyance. It is the foreseeable consequence of a regime in which use value, symbolic value and the continuity of enjoyment are subject to the governance of third parties.

The perception that digital consumption operates under revocable and mutable conditions does not arise as a loose intuition. It stems from a research path that crossed the institutional dimension of industrial property and came into focus in interactive entertainment, where enjoyment depends on private decision-making centers and opaque infrastructures. The decisive point is not to note that the environment reconfigures the object, but to recognize that classical dogmatics tends to treat this fact as a post-contractual detail, when the real problem lies in the conditions of experience that define what counts as delivery and what counts as loss.

This thesis did not arise from a prior institutional agenda, nor from mere private curiosity turned into an academic pretext, but from a recurring discomfort that repeated itself for years, in the same form, albeit in different settings. Everyday life came to be permeated by technical layers that ceased to operate as simple tools and began to function as an environment. What was once a means became a condition of possibility for living, for consuming and, often, for belonging itself. Research was the way found to treat this discomfort seriously, keeping a distance from both romanticization and cynicism.

Throughout academic, teaching and professional practice, it became difficult to ignore that certain contemporary experiences, although trivial in the eyes of those who choose not to live them, are structurally decisive for those who devote a relevant part of their lives to them. In a country in which the digital environment is already routine infrastructure, how can one keep treating the consumption embedded in it as if it were a lesser, puerile or legally irrelevant version of traditional consumption? At what moment did it become natural that time, attention, reputation, collection, bond and patrimony can be captured and undone by design, without this being named with the weight it deserves and, above all, without generating the consequences it should generate?

p. 22 This difficulty in looking away was not merely theoretical. It also came from teaching and from the need to sustain Consumer Law as a language of protection, and not as mere ritual. The experience of teaching and following discussions on collective redress in consumer matters shifted the perspective beyond the contract as paper and into consumption as routine, expectation and frustration. There, a pattern repeated itself: contemporary injury rarely announces itself as an explicit scam. It usually comes as administered normality, as a promise of experience, as an incentive to stay, as a cost one learns to call inevitable.

Another part of this perception came from professional experience with digital advertising and community building. By contrast, it stripped away the naivety that sometimes contaminates legal discourse on communication and the market. Visibility, narrative, belonging and engagement are not ornaments. In practice, they are structures, techniques and design choices. Community is not born from nothing, nor is it maintained by chance. It is cultivated through language, symbolic reward, a sense of place and small frictions that push behavior wherever it serves someone's interest. Observing the machine at work confirmed that the contemporary consumer does not face only an asymmetry of data: they face an asymmetry of grammar. The supplier, under such a reading, would be controlling the very vocabulary of value, the signs of belonging and the triggers that make the consumed experience seem natural.

There is, however, a predictable social reflex whenever one mentions the aim of treating interactive entertainment as a technical, marketing, existential and patrimonial category. The reaction, at times, is laughter, puzzlement or disdain. This reflex is part of the very problem the thesis confronts, because social disdain is one of the mechanisms that makes injury easier, more acceptable and more invisible. When the activity of an entire economic sector is treated as harmless play, it becomes easier for abuses to be examined from the perspective of “it's just a game”, even when they involve money, time, attention, legitimate expectations and relevant losses.

There is, nevertheless, a relevant side effect. Because it is treated as play, interactive entertainment ends up exposing, with less modesty and less varnish, structures that in other sectors appear disguised as convenience, innovation or the mere organization of a service. In games, reward cycles, retention metrics, visibility management, friction engineering and behavior administration are acknowledged more openly. On platforms commonly taken seriously, the same set of techniques shows up under the guise of neutrality, personalization or efficiency. Studying games, in this sense, is not choosing a lesser object. It is choosing the place where the mechanisms appear most clearly.

p. 23 When this parallelism is recognized, the legal question changes scale without losing rigor. If the experience of interactive entertainment allows one to see, almost with the naked eye, the production of dependence, the fabrication of symbolic value, the conversion of routine into bond and the naturalization of asymmetries, then it offers a privileged laboratory for understanding what repeats itself in ecosystems of communication, work, learning, reputation and consumption in general. The difference is that, outside the game, the same effects tend to be presented as inevitable, and not as choices of architecture. It is precisely for this reason that a socially underestimated object can provide especially useful keys for treating seriously what hides behind polished interfaces and mature discourses.

Law is the language of earthly gods. It is the way in which the collectivity that organizes itself as a State transforms forces into forms, conflicts into criteria, desires into limits and powers into responsibilities. When it works, it prevents the will of the strongest from becoming normality by sheer capacity of imposition. It names and organizes what, without a name, becomes “bad luck”, “rule of the game” or “technical necessity”. In this setting, Consumer Law presents itself as a deliberately expandable technical idiom, built to recognize mutable asymmetries and capture abuses that do not announce themselves as deviations, preserving the promise of protection even when the market changes its skin.

The expandability of Consumer Law is not poetic license. It is consistent with the Brazilian constitutional order, in which free enterprise neither authorizes insurmountable asymmetries nor naturalizes losses as a cost of participation. In platform markets, this matrix takes on a decisive particularity. The relevant vulnerability is not reducible to missing information or a poorly drafted clause. It can be fabricated by architecture, by semiotic governance and by the private concentration of evidence and decision. For this reason, the dogmatic reconstruction proposed in this work is not meant merely to update concepts to keep pace with technology, but to make operational, under controllable criteria, a constitutional core for containing private power when it presents itself as technical normality.

This passage from the technological problem to the constitutional problem is not a methodological deviation, but a direct consequence of the very process of constitutionalization of law. If fundamental rights are no longer understood only as barriers opposed to the State and instead radiate effects over the various branches of the legal order, then the private relationship can no longer be treated as a space immune to the incidence of constitutional values, above all when powers capable of conditioning, restricting or degrading relevant existential positions of the subjects involved are concentrated in it Clemente(Freitas; Clemente, 2011, p. 160-161). p. 24 In other words, when the platform unilaterally organizes access, permanence, visibility, evidence and sanction, what is at stake is no longer simple freedom of negotiation in a neutral environment, but the private exercise of power with concrete aptitude to affect the consumer's fundamental rights.

It is precisely for this reason that platform governance is not taken here as a mere contractual or technological backdrop. The key lies in recognizing that, in contemporary society, relevant violations of fundamental rights do not come only from the State, but also from private subjects endowed with economic, informational and organizational power, whose action requires a dogmatic reconstruction capable of reconciling effective protection and private autonomy without romanticizing the latter Clemente(Freitas; Clemente, 2011, p. 159-161; 166-167).

In the environment of continuous digital consumption, this finding gains its own density: the asymmetry does not stem only from abstract patrimonial superiority, but from the infrastructural capacity to fabricate the experience, define what counts as regular conduct, administer the intelligibility of loss and impose, under the appearance of technical normality, reconfigurations that affect the user's enjoyment, identity and patrimony. The incidence of fundamental rights in private relationships, here, does not appear as a theoretical ornament, but as a presupposition of the intelligibility of the problem itself.

If Law operates through this transformation of force into form, then the decisive conflict in platform consumption is not only “what happened”, but “how what happened can be said” without being downgraded to noise. In ecosystems governed by architecture, the dispute is filtered by recording formats, evidentiary criteria and limitations on access to information, which pushes the debate onto the terrain of legal intelligibility: what is nameable, demonstrable and attributable. Consumer protection can only act when what today appears as “the functioning of the system” comes to be seen again as a controllable decision and, therefore, as responsibility.

It must be recognized, however, that this expandability of Brazilian Consumer Law has been held back by a strictly material ontology, which insists on reducing the product to the delivery of a physical medium or of a discrete, completable performance. Article 3, §11, of the Consumer Protection Code already contains the normative opening needed to recognize goods that circulate without a stable corporeal medium and whose enjoyment depends on continuous technical and organizational conditions. The Immaterial Object of Consumption (IOC) presents itself, in this sense, as the definitive subdivision of a plot that the legislator of p. 25 1990 had already demarcated, although contemporary doctrine has left it a vacant lot. By conferring legal density on what was only a residual label, the IOC prevents immateriality from serving as an alibi for the technical irresponsibility of platforms.

Keeping this lot vacant is not merely a theoretical problem. It produces foreseeable institutional effects, because every stable dogmatic gap tends to be converted into a business model. When categories capable of making certain losses legally visible, of naming, demonstrating and attributing them, are lacking, the space of irresponsibility does not remain neutral and begins to function as a margin for optimization. What is not captured as legal risk tends to be treated as an ordinary component of design. When oversight is rare and sanction unlikely, risk ceases to act as a brake and gets priced in, as if it were merely an operating cost. Dogmatics, by omission, may end up becoming part of the very arrangement that naturalizes the transfer of losses to the consumer.

This reconstruction also requires an explicit reconciliation with intellectual property rights, since a relevant part of platform governance is anchored, directly or indirectly, in exclusive rights and licensing regimes. The legal protection of immaterial creations, however, does not operate as a safe-conduct for the arbitrary administration of consumption experiences. The Brazilian Constitution does not treat exclusivity as immunity and conditions the tolerability of exclusive prerogatives on socially justifiable purposes, of development and of public interest2, which prevents converting opacity, unilateral mutability and sanctions destructive of enjoyment into so-called “inevitable” effects of the right to exclude third parties.

In this sense, the regime of the Immaterial Object of Consumption is not opposed to intellectual property as if it were a project for annulling exclusivities. It fixes the point of institutional tension. Under continuous consumption, the economic exploitation of the sign and of access does not eliminate minimum duties of stability, verifiable transparency and restoration when there is relevant degradation of enjoyment. Exclusivity may organize the market, but it cannot justify an environment in which the consumer bears systematic losses without an intelligible language of justification, without traceability and without conditions for contestation.

p. 26 This investigative chain produced an additional turning point, decisive for the economic reading of the problem. The analysis of the dispute between the United States and China at the WTO, at the end of the 2000s3, revealed a structural trait that would become key to understanding contemporary models of extraction. When the institutional design of the unlawful act operates through thresholds and metrics, the boundary between lawful and unlawful ceases to function as an effective prohibition and comes to operate as an optimization parameter of the business model itself, in which profit is realized not through a high margin on few units, but through scale and repetition below the punishable threshold.

The engine of many digital ecosystems is not only overt charging, but drainage through micro-events, micro-decisions and micro-losses, distributed at scale and diluted to the point of becoming almost invisible, although cumulatively relevant. The hypothesis taking shape is that a significant part of the “perfect business model” of contemporary Big Techs does not in reality minimize risks, but rather pulverizes and outsources them, converting costs, frictions and setbacks into noise that is acceptable per unit and, for that reason, hard to contest through individual perception. It is at this micro level that the architecture of the business becomes dogmatically relevant, because what presents itself as "technical normality" comes to operate as an economic method, actively transferring risks and losses to the consumer — a diagnosis that will be reconstructed under criteria of controllable attribution in the following sections.

What platforms do, in this context, is sell, as factual inevitability, a private language that in practice forms part of the very object they set out to sell. It is, rather, an institutional regime that reorganizes consumption through architecture, curation, sanction, opacity and unilateral change. By governing access, permanence and the intelligibility of what was acquired, the platform comes to exercise, in practice, regulatory functions internal to the p. 27 ecosystem, defining conditions of participation, administering visibility and reputation, deciding what counts as acceptable conduct and executing sanctions that can destroy the use value and the identity value of the object enjoyed. This normativity is not limited to the contractual clauses that formally govern the relationship, since it materializes in interface design, choice patterns, selective frictions and enforcement4 mechanisms capable of organizing what is possible, visible and desirable.

Lex Plataformae is an operative Latinization built by analogy with classical formulas for naming private orderings, especially the Lex Mercatoria, an expression used to designate an a-national body of rules and principles associated with the practice of international trade and its private mechanisms for settling disputes. The approximation is deliberate, because both expressions function as conceptual labels for normative phenomena that do not depend on state legislative production to operate socially and produce legally relevant effects, even though their nature, their sources and their limits are the subject of controversy. The distinction is equally relevant, because, while the Lex Mercatoria is usually described as an ordering emerging from diffuse practices and multiple actors, the Lex Plataformae5 designates a type of private ordering centered on a platform as the institutional core that defines rules, visibilities, sanctions and evidentiary regimes through continuous architecture and updating.

The expression Lex Plataformae therefore intends to name not a set of pre-drafted clauses, but a private ordering that is technically executable, economically oriented and semiotically naturalized. Its specificity lies in the fact that the platform not only enunciates rules, but materially conditions the field of the possible. It not only promises sanctions, but implements them. It not only interprets behaviors, but classifies, records, prioritizes, makes invisible, recommends, blocks, expels, restricts, monetizes and reconfigures the very environment in which the user tries to exercise the enjoyment of the acquired object. For this reason, the analogy with the Lex Mercatoria is useful as a point of departure, but insufficient as a point of arrival. The Lex Mercatoria refers, in its best-known formulation, to a transnational order associated with mercantile practices and private mechanisms for stabilizing expectations. The Lex p. 28 Plataformae, in turn, designates a private order whose center is not only mercantile circulation among actors, but control of the infrastructure of access, of the intelligibility of the experience and of the concrete conditions for exercising the user's position.

From this follows a methodologically sensitive difference. On the platform, the repetition of practices and solutions should not be read naively as a sufficient sign of normative legitimacy. Often, what presents itself as market habit or custom of the ecosystem is already the product of unilateral design, of structural informational asymmetry and of the naturalization of choices previously filtered by the architecture itself. In other words, reiteration, here, may express less a spontaneously formed normative consensus and more the sedimentation of a private power that fabricates the environment in which behaviors repeat themselves. The critique is relevant because it prevents romanticizing, under the language of custom, what may in truth correspond to the technical stabilization of private impositions.

The proposed expression, therefore, fulfills a specific analytical function. It allows one to designate the practical juridicity that emerges when the platform ceases to be a mere vehicle of circulation and comes to act as an internal instance of government of the ecosystem. In this condition, it produces express and implicit rules, defines regimes of adhesion, shapes expectations of conduct, establishes its own circuits of contestation, models the use value of the good or service accessed and interferes directly in the preservation, erosion or destruction of the identity and economic value associated with the immaterial object of consumption. The Lex Plataformae, thus understood, neither replaces state Law nor eliminates the incidence of the public legal order. It designates, rather, the private normative layer that interposes itself between the subject and enjoyment, organizing the concrete experience of consumption by means that Law cannot treat as a simple technological backdrop.

In this context, this is the technical sense that allows one to speak of governance by architecture, along the lines of what Julie E. Cohen6 describes as informational power exercised by p. 29 structural means, and not only by explicit command. In doing so, the platform occupies the place of the collectivity without authorization and occupies the place of the State without accepting, in the same measure, the duties that limit a power capable of organizing lives. It transforms political decision into operational detail and sells this usurpation as the simple functioning of the world. The legal problem begins when this continuous administration manages to redefine, by design and by enforcement, what the object is, what counts as delivery and who bears the losses when the environment “decides” to change. From here on, it proves decisive to separate two movements that should not be confused.

The first is descriptive. Identifying this regime of private governance means recognizing a form of power that precedes and conditions, in practice, economic and cultural life. In terms of legal theory, it is a matter of exposing a presupposed law that functions as the effective ground of conduct, even before posited law manages to name and discipline it. It is this mismatch between what operates as a norm in everyday life and what is recognized as a norm by state language that makes the phenomenon legally relevant.

The second movement is dogmatic and is not automatic. Abusive teleology does not name the mere fact that this regime governs. The category only makes sense if it can function as controllable attribution, capable of reconstructing a pattern and not merely reacting to an episode. What one seeks to qualify is the situation in which the exploitative purpose is inscribed in the very architecture of the service and reproduces itself as regularity, through choices of design7, curation and sanction that make enjoyment dependent on asymmetries the consumer can neither decode nor audit. This requirement of form and control is consistent with Miguel Reale's8 idea that Law only reaches reality when it converts facts and values into criteria, and with the p. 30 warning of Tércio Sampaio Ferraz Jr.9 that, without categories capable of capturing structure and recurrence, legal decision-making fragments into micro-events and loses the rationality of the whole.

A methodological requirement follows from this. Legal control of these ecosystems depends on a vocabulary capable of making nameable, demonstrable and attributable the decisions that today hide behind technical functioning. For this reason, the work is organized in linked movements, through which it describes the governance regime that administers access to, permanence of and meaning of the digital good, stabilizes categories of qualification, evidence and attribution under the Consumer Protection Code in reconciliation with the legislation on which intellectual property rights rest and, finally, tests these keys in illustrative applications with typical examples of continuous digital consumption, in which unilateral reconfigurations and platform sanctions shift what counts as delivery, alter enjoyment and redistribute losses.

The contemporary organization of economic, social and cultural life increasingly runs through a techno-media order that reconfigures the conditions of possibility of p. 31 consumption. This is not merely a quantitative increase in technologies applied to the production and circulation of goods, but the consolidation of permanent digital environments capable of shaping the perception, attention and behavior of individuals on a large scale. In this setting, legal protection no longer deals only with isolated failures of quality or safety and must come to see recurring structures of governance and inducement, in which the environment itself organizes expectations, enjoyment and losses as part of the business model.

The critical literature on technique and media, by highlighting the passage from technique-as-tool to technique-as-environment, allows one to see that contemporary economic rationality is not limited to producing isolated material objects, but architects contexts of experience in which the consumer is immersed. Instead of clearly delimited corporeal goods, immaterial arrangements of functionalities, signs and symbolic rewards emerge, continuously updated through proprietary code and confidential business decisions. Consumption comes to be mediated by algorithmic flows and graphical interfaces that organize the visible, the desirable and the possible, making the technical architecture, itself, a locus of legal dispute.

The interactive entertainment industry, especially that of electronic games in a platform environment, is a paradigmatic terrain of this transformation. Online games with millions of users, integrated into ecosystems of microtransactions, progression systems and elements of digital sociability, cease to be mere “leisure products” and assume the role of cultural and economic infrastructures. In them, the consumer's experience depends on constant interaction with a set of dynamic rules, visual and sound signs, avatars, skins10, virtual items and narratives that make up a true playable world. The traditional figure of the “buyer of a game” is replaced by the subject who enters and remains in a private normative environment, governed by adhesion contracts and unilateral policies.

From the legal point of view, this reconfiguration throws into crisis a material ontology still dominant in Brazilian Consumer Law, which tends to think of products and services as discrete, one-off performances exhausted in an act of acquisition and enjoyment. The consequence is a growing mismatch between the way consumption is actually organized, in digital environments of high complexity and continuous reconfiguration, and the traditional p. 32 categories mobilized for its protection, which end up treating as “after-sale” what, in practice, is the very core of the object.

The Brazilian Consumer Protection Code was tailored for a world in which risk shows its face and damage leaves direct traces on the body, the mind and patrimony. It was born to protect life as a condition of dignified existence when industry errs, when commerce fails, when the normality of the market produces degrading quality defects and when it gives rise to violent accidents. In the classical vocabulary, first come the structural weaknesses, the defects that seep into manufacturing and performance as routine and that Law tries to tame through information, quality and liability. Then come the shock, the accident and the explosion as events that break integrity and demand a response. It is a grammar made to capture error, negligence, what is dangerous, what materializes as a fall, a loss and mourning.

Yet, in continuous consumption governed by platforms, the central problem no longer behaves as error or accident. The “product” is not a stable thing, it is an administered position. Enjoyment depends on mutable rules, invisible curation, sanctions, updates and an authority that does not present itself as such. Everything seems to work and, even so, the feeling persists that something important is moving in the shadows. The CDC keeps looking through the lens of manufacturing and marketing while the object of the conflict lies in the conception, in the design of the environment, and not in an isolated event. Causality itself changes, because the injury is no longer the failure that escaped control, but the one that was converted into a “legitimate” business model.

This is how abusive teleology emerges as a decisive category of unlawful act. It is the worst of the design defects, because it intentionally precedes the damage and programs it. When deliberate, it ceases to be a failure and becomes a method. It is a defect of purpose, a sin driven into the root of the project. It is not that something “went wrong”, it is that something “was made to be this way”. Technique comes to operate as a manifest language, that is, fully operative. A language of architecture and engineering that defines what can be seen, proven, contested, repaired and even remembered. It fabricates the facts of consumption the way one would fabricate weather in a predictable sequence, with superficial justification and the appearance of strict normality. Damage ceases to be an occurrence, because it is part of the very routine of government. And the consumer is pushed to negotiate not with the object, but with the world the object inhabits. A world that is not their own. A world that, behind the masks, is nothing but a set built so that everything happens not in their favor, but in favor of whoever defines the rules of the game and, in this case, even beyond the game.

p. 33 The opening metaphor of Law as the “language of earthly gods” introduces an ethical perspective for reading the conflict. In the work of Dante Alighieri, the depth diagram of the Inferno11 is not a scale of the intensity of suffering, but a cartography of the degradation of the human bond. Seriousness increases when evil ceases to be impulse or force and comes to operate as guile, that is, when intelligence is used to produce an appearance of legitimacy and, above all, to violate trust.

The Consumer Protection Code operates comfortably when injury presents itself as a manifest failure, a disorganization of conduct or recognizable violence, situations in which the deviation can still be seen as a repairable event. Under the technofeudal dynamic, however, injury tends to reorganize itself as a program. Capture is designed through architecture and continuous governance, converting informational asymmetry into method and making the bond promised to the consumer an exploitable trust. In this horizon, the problem ceases to be only the one-off damage and comes to be the systematic production of conditions for trust to be broken without appearing broken, as if the revocability of what is offered were technical normality.

At the apex, betrayal appears. Not as literary drama, but as the logical consequence of a consumption that sells belonging only to reveal itself later as a revocable license. The unjust ban, the loss of a resource legitimately earned and acquired, the erasure of a history that concentrates expenditure of attention, time and money, as well as the pressure for forced reinvestment so that it becomes possible to “exist again” in the ecosystem are examples of how the ninth circle is updated in this regime.

It is in this context that the central problem is situated: how to reconstruct the consumer protection regime in light of the reality of digital goods and interactive entertainment platforms, without breaking with the normative framework of the Consumer Protection Code and the Constitution? And, at the same time, how to make it responsive to the new techno-media order? More specifically: in what way can Brazilian Consumer Law recognize and confront the risks derived from the continued exposure of individuals to digital objects that intensely mobilize their attention, their affectivity, their identity, and even their patrimony under the logic of business models oriented toward the maximum extraction of value?

p. 34 The hypothesis that guides the work is that this updating depends on the formulation of a legal-analytical category capable of capturing the specificity of digital goods in a platform environment. To this end, the thesis proposes the Immaterial Object of Consumption (IOC) as an infrastructure-concept that gives concrete form, at the dogmatic level, to the notion of “immaterial product” already enacted in the Brazilian legal order under Article 3, §1, of the CDC12, shifting the focus from the mere conformity of a corporeal object to the governance of functional and symbolic architectures that structure the consumer's experience. The IOC is not a new microsystem, but a new interpretive key, a way of reading the CDC itself and the Constitution, leading them to the centrality of flows of code, images, sounds and narratives in the configuration of digital consumer relationships.

From this perspective, it is argued that the consumer's factual vulnerability, expressly recognized in Article 4, I, of the CDC13, takes on in digital environments a form of its own, linked to mediation by signs, interfaces and private criteria of visibility and enjoyment. This vulnerability is intensified by practices of hypercuratorial anticipation, through which the platform projects images of performance, reward and future experience without offering, in the same measure, real conditions of verification and control by the consumer.

In this framework, the operational concept of abusive teleology makes it possible to qualify, under strict criteria, situations in which the architecture of the environment is designed to systematically exploit the consumer's semiotic vulnerability, instrumentalizing their attention and their symbolic identity in favor of revenue maximization. Consumer protection, then, needs to be reconstructed so as to protect not only physical or economic integrity, but equally the functional integrity of the goods acquired and the personal and symbolic identity of the user, both continuously projected onto Immaterial Objects of Consumption, which are responsible for structuring life in the digital ecosystem.

In this reading, La Divina Commedia offers no mere rhetorical ornament. In Alighieri's poem, the circles do not represent mere degrees of punishment, but rather a gradation of ethical deviation established in the development of the relationship between impulse, force and human p. 35 intelligence. What Dante narrates, on this understanding, is not a graduated definition of sin, but an understanding of what degrades human relationships. The greater the damage to the bond, the graver the sin. The first circles bear the mark of man's weakness: desire, lack of control, incontinence. This is where evil appears as a fall, as incapacity and, to a certain extent, as manifest and recognized chaos. Next, violence implies an active rupture of another's integrity, and sin takes on its most objective and also most destructive form.

In the last circles, however, the decisive point is fraud, because it mobilizes reason, language and calculation. Fraud touches what should elevate the human being, but which is instrumentalized for the shady capture of the other, exploiting their trust, their expectation and making any threshold of predictability impossible. For this reason, the Dantean apex is betrayal: fraud practiced against the most privileged bonds. It is not merely a perspective of material damage, but the full dissolution of the foundations of trust without which no common life is able to sustain itself. This logic, when transposed as an ethical-structural metaphor, makes it possible to organize the passage from contingent defect to deliberate defect, from isolated failure to method, from event to project, without losing the guiding logical thread that makes intelligible why certain forms of deviation seem deeper than others.

The last circle adds yet another detail decisive for this reading: it is not an environment marked by fire, but by ice. If the previous circles refer to deviations that present themselves as ostensive events, that is, as something that explodes, that burns, that imposes itself on the senses and creates uproar, the apex comes by the opposite route. Betrayal, ultimately, is cold, discreet, motionless, and precisely for this reason substantially more difficult to understand from common intuition.

Ice symbolizes an evil that does not need to display itself as a dispute, because it operates by withdrawal, erasure and de-escalation, not by shock. Beyond the circles, it is also the symbol of the apex of administered normality, where the structure does not need to shout in order to dominate, since it simply “freezes” the consumer's alternatives. It is not a damage that shouts, but a structure that silences, that sickens the bond by stiffening, that makes loss naturalized, and that induces the subject themselves to passively occupy the position in which they are harmed. In this regime, the metaphorical Inferno does not translate into combustion of the visible fact, but into an administered paralysis that makes the complaint inaudible and, at the same time, transforms the act of injustice into functional normality.

Therefore, the traditional product-caused accidents and quality defects described under the Brazilian consumer codification are not being abandoned. What is proposed is support for the view that, when technique is carried to its ultimate consequences, the protection of dignity requires a category capable p. 36 of seeing evil before the scream, the unlawful act before the event, which manifests itself under a design capable of making any cry for help inaudible. The platform disqualifies the consumer as a subject who deserves to be heard14, converting the search for justice into mere noise that the platform itself can manage.

This research falls within the field of consumer rights and intellectual property from the perspective of the Brazilian constitutional order, taking as its analytical axis the category of the Immaterial Object of Consumption (IOC). The thematic scope concentrates on consumer relationships mediated by interactive entertainment platforms, especially those structured around online electronic games and their ecosystems of digital goods. It is, therefore, a matter of investigating not the technological phenomenon in general, but the specific way in which playful digital environments, organized under a platform regime, condition the consumption experience and the projection of the symbolic identity of their users.

As to the normative delimitation, the starting point is the Consumer Protection Code, in dialogue with the 1988 Constitution of the Federative Republic of Brazil and with the comprehensive national legislation pertaining to intellectual property rights (copyright, industrial property rights, rights over personally or economically relevant immaterial creations in the lato sense), in order to reconstruct the concept of “immaterial product” in light of contemporary practices of economic exploitation of digital goods. Instead of proposing an autonomous regulatory microsystem for “electronic games” or “digital platforms”, what is being proposed is a demonstration that the Immaterial Object of Consumption is a form of interpretive updating that, in dogmatic terms, is already internal to the CDC itself and derived from the constitutional principles that inform it, with special attention to the guarantee of recognition of the consumer's vulnerability and to the social function of intellectual property.

At the level of illustrative application, the work takes as its privileged locus the ecosystem of products and services of the multinational Riot Games, with a focus on the game League of p. 37 Legends and its extensions on associated platforms. This scope does not seek to exhaust the diversity of the global interactive entertainment industry, but to offer a field of exemplification in which typical structures of semiotic governance, practices of hypercuratorial anticipation, monetization models based on digital goods and recurring patterns of friction between consumers and supplier become particularly clear. Although the IOC is built to engage with continuous digital consumption as a whole, the research limits its use of references to this universe to supporting intelligibility and anchoring the theoretical-dogmatic path.

In this effort of delimitation, a necessary terminological cleansing is required, which demands abandoning the descriptive generalization “digital vulnerability”. Although it presents itself as a seductive nomenclature, this expression suffers from a destructive polysemy that, by merely naming the setting, ends up obscuring the mechanism of power that operates in it. This research replaces the environmental label with the precision of semiotic vulnerability. The consumer is not vulnerable merely because they are on the internet, but because the platform governs the grammar, the meanings and the interfaces that define the very existence and value of enjoyment. It is a matter of shifting the debate from the lack of data to the asymmetry of world, converting what was previously academic noise into an objective criterion of evidence and decision.

By methodological choice, topics such as criminal liability, sectoral regulation of broadcasting, taxation of software or personal data protection regimes as autonomous normative axes fall outside the direct scope of the analysis. The focus falls on the articulation between consumption, intellectual property and platform architectures, with the guiding thread being the way the IOC organizes the consumer's experience and how semiotic vulnerability is exploited in business models oriented toward the continuous extraction of value.

The relevance of the research stems, first, from the growing centrality of digital consumption in everyday life, especially in the fields of entertainment, sociability and the construction of identities. Online gaming platforms have ceased to be peripheral spaces of youth leisure and have become environments in which significant economic values circulate around microtransactions, virtual goods, targeted advertising and the organization of competitive events of global reach. In this setting, Brazil presents itself simultaneously as a large consumer market and as a legal order that still operates, to a large extent, with categories conceived for an analog world, based on corporeal goods and one-off performances, even if repeated.

Second, a conspicuous theoretical-dogmatic gap persists. Although doctrine and case law have advanced on topics such as electronic commerce, distance p. 38 contracting and personal data protection, the way complex digital goods, especially those that structure persistent playful worlds and experiences, fit into the Consumer Protection Code's category of “immaterial product”, and what consequences follow for the liability of suppliers, remain little explored. The absence of a systematic treatment of the consumption of digital goods in a platform environment opens space for case-by-case solutions, often incapable of capturing the specific risks of consumers' prolonged exposure to techno-media architectures that exploit their semiotic vulnerability.

Third, the research is justified by its potential critical contribution to the debate on the social function of intellectual property and on the governance of digital platforms. By understanding intellectual property as an infrastructure for the governance of signs, and not only as a mechanism of reward for creativity, it becomes possible to show how exclusive rights are mobilized to structure consumption ecosystems in which access, permanence and the user's very experience are conditioned by unilateral decisions of the supplier. The category of the Immaterial Object of Consumption makes it possible to articulate, in a single framework, the economic, legal and symbolic dimensions of this process.

Finally, the choice of the Riot Games ecosystem, with a focus on League of Legends, adds applied and exemplary relevance on a global scale. It is an environment with a strong presence in the Brazilian market, a business model sophisticated in its monetization of digital goods and a wide availability of public documentary material and of public statements by consumer users. Together these provide sufficient grounding for rigorous illustrative applications designed to make typical mechanisms of governance by architecture, curation and sanction visible.

This context offers an especially clear locus for making visible recurring points of friction that the IOC regime intends to make adjudicable, including when such frictions stem from architecture and enforcement decisions specific to the service. From a social standpoint, the research responds to the need to improve the quality of the public and legal debate on the legitimate limits of the economic exploitation of users in playful digital environments and on the conditions for holding accountable private powers that reorganize expectations and losses at scale.

Starting from the general objective of reconstructing the Brazilian consumer protection regime through the category of the Immaterial Object of Consumption (IOC), the aim is to offer an interpretive key capable of making the law in force responsive to the demands of digital consumption, without p. 39 breaking with its normative framework. As specific objectives, the work seeks to identify, in light of the critical theory of technique and the sociology of platforms, the central characteristics of the techno-media order that structures contemporary digital consumption, with emphasis on interactive entertainment. It is also necessary to formulate the concept of the Immaterial Object of Consumption as an infrastructure-concept, delimiting its constitutive elements and its articulation with the Consumer Protection Code's traditional categories of product and service.

Next, the notion of semiotic vulnerability is developed as a contemporary form of factual vulnerability in environments mediated by digital signs, so as to allow the construction of the category of the unlawful act of abusive teleology, capable of qualifying technical-contractual arrangements whose economic purpose depends on the systematic exploitation of this vulnerability. Finally, these categories are employed in illustrative applications anchored in the Riot Games ecosystem, with a focus on League of Legends, to make explicit dogmatic consequences concerning commercial practices, contractual governance and liability regimes, without any claim to exhaustive empirical demonstration through an extended case study.

In operational terms, the aim is to select and analyze, for illustrative purposes and for their dogmatic pertinence, public statements by consumers related to the League of Legends ecosystem, as well as to examine terms of use, policies and interface mechanisms relevant to the governance of the service.

Drawing on this public documentary material and on typical examples, the aim is to make explicit how practices of hypercuratorial anticipation, curation and sanction interact with the legal regime of the IOC, so as to make recurring patterns of friction adjudicable without shifting the argument toward a claim to a systematic survey. Finally, criteria of open auditability and functional value equivalence are proposed, capable of guiding the action of consumer protection bodies, the Judiciary and public policy makers in protecting digital goods in a platform environment.

The research adopts a qualitative approach with a theoretical-dogmatic orientation, combining theoretical reconstruction and normative reconstruction with referenced illustrative applications. In dialogue with the proposal of reflexive science, it is assumed that the researcher's position does not by itself become evidence, but functions as a condition of intelligibility for the selection of typical examples and for the translation of micro-processes of governance of digital consumption into legally controllable categories. At the theoretical level, critical contributions on technique and media, from the sociology of technology and from the political economy of platform p. 40 capitalism are reread, with the objective of understanding the techno-media order that conditions contemporary digital consumption. This path is linked to the analysis of legislation, doctrine and legal institutions of Consumer Law and intellectual property, as well as to the constitutional reconstruction needed to stabilize criteria for controlling private powers in continuous digital environments.

Public documentary materials and institutional sources are employed to anchor and exemplify the dogmatic path, without constituting an exhaustive empirical corpus. This choice stems from the object itself, since the axis of the work is the dogmatic reconstruction of categories of qualification, evidence and attribution applicable to continuous digital consumption, and not the production of generalizations by sampling. Relevant contractual and informational documents are selected for their pertinence to the legal problem and to the proposed categories, included above all as evidence of recurring points of friction in the protection of digital goods in a platform environment. Public statements by consumers in accessible spaces, when used, are treated as a symptomatic record of conflict patterns and as support for the intelligibility of the mechanism described, and not as a basis for statistical inference.

The field references are articulated with the theoretical-dogmatic framework in a hermeneutic logic oriented by controllability, in which the validity of the argument stems from the normative reconstruction and the consistency of the proposed criteria, and not from demonstrative empirical testing. The illustrative applications aim to make visible typical mechanisms of governance by architecture and their consequences for the consumer's enjoyment, so that it can be set out rigorously when and why categories such as Immaterial Object of Consumption, semiotic vulnerability and abusive teleology are justified, and which control operators can guide decisions and remedies in the Brazilian legal order.

Like the cultural references mentioned earlier, the epigraphs that open the thesis and each of its primary sections are curated as a second plane of reading, a symptomatic record of the main text, with a precise methodological purpose of intelligibility and denaturalization. The epigraphs are intended to record, in culturally situated language, the structure of feeling that runs through the phenomenon investigated, whose object is transnational and widely operative and disseminated from an anglophone environment and culture, even though the dogmatic framing of this work is deliberately Brazilian.

For this reason, the predominant cultural and temporal scope of the epigraphs is deliberate. The curation frequently concentrates on popular works produced in the anglophone environment between the mid-2000s and the early 2010s because of the historical fit of the material to the p. 41 phenomenon investigated. It was precisely in this period that the participatory web, the attention economy and the first successful formats of continuous monetization in digital environments were consolidated with unprecedented intensity, with promises of freedom and belonging that walked side by side with new forms of capture, performative pressure, anxiety and symbolic dependence. It must be remembered, on this point, that the very game ecosystem taken as an illustrative locus , League of Legends, was originally launched on the market in 2009.

Thus, through the record of this repertoire, the thesis fixes a layer of cultural archaeology of the moment in which certain affective grammars of digital consumption were originally forged and normalized. The temporal mark, therefore, makes the genesis of the argument clear. Although the references are situated in specific cultural contexts, the tensions they dramatize have been incorporated as a structural trait of contemporary experience. Diffuse fear, disorientation, performative pressure and the feeling of living under invisible instructions do not belong to a decade, since they describe a way of life that was formed from transnational structures and consolidated itself as a global phenomenon.

In platform economies, the consumer relationship is not reduced to an exchange of performance for price. It is mediated by environment, sign, rhythm, belonging and performance, and it is in this symbolic regime that expectations are formed, conduct is modulated and vulnerability is produced. For this reason, the thesis operates in two complementary layers. One is dogmatic, in which categories, criteria and legal consequences are built. The other is cultural and expressive, in which the way the object itself presents itself and naturalizes its mechanisms becomes visible, without requiring recognition of the references, only recognition of the logic they condense. To ground this layer, an anthropology of consumption is mobilized in a structural key, taking goods, regimes of value and forms of belonging as organizing components of experience and, therefore, as part of the very field in which economic extraction is stabilized.

The discussion is organized in eight sections, including this introduction and the conclusions, so as to progressively articulate the methodological design, the theoretical reconstruction of the phenomenon and the dogmatic conversion of the proposed categories. After the presentation of the problem, the hypothesis, the delimitation, the objectives and the methodology in this introduction, the second section establishes the research design and makes explicit the status of the reference material used throughout the work, delimiting the role of the researcher's situated experience and of public sources as support for intelligibility and illustrative anchoring.

p. 42 The third section reconstructs the techno-economic order of platforms, describing how technical rationality and models of continuous extraction reorganize the conditions of possibility of contemporary digital consumption. The fourth section examines the semiotic and subjective dimension of immaterial consumption, making explicit the means through which signs, belonging, rhythm and performance modulate expectations and conducts, thus intensifying asymmetries relevant to consumer protection. Throughout these sections, occasional references to the interactive entertainment ecosystem are employed for illustrative purposes, in order to make visible typical mechanisms of governance by architecture.

The fifth section consolidates the regime of the Immaterial Object of Consumption as an infrastructure-concept and establishes the operational control concepts capable of guiding the identification and qualification of abusive practices in continuous digital environments, with emphasis on semiotic vulnerability, abusive teleology as a category of controllable attribution, and parameters such as open auditability and functional value equivalence. The sixth section carries out the dogmatic reconstruction, proposing an integrated reading of the 1988 Federal Constitution of the Federative Republic of Brazil, the Consumer Protection Code, and the Brazilian legislation on intellectual property rights based on the IOC regime, so as to make explicit criteria of legal qualification, correlative duties and possible institutional responses to conflicts on digital consumption platforms.

The seventh section concentrates illustrative applications and tests of fit of the proposed keys in typical contemporary situations of continuous digital consumption, using examples and public documentary material to make visible recurring patterns of friction, evidentiary requirements and blind spots in the translation of the conflict, without shifting the work toward an extended case study. At the end, the eighth section closes the path with the final considerations, in which the main arguments are revisited, their limits indicated and possible developments pointed out at the scientific, dogmatic and institutional levels.

Furthermore, it should be clarified that the text of this research carries no intent to establish avenues of accusation regarding any subjects cited or analyzed. The proposal has strictly scientific objectives of a theoretical-dogmatic order, directed at building categories and criteria for interpreting posited law for the pacification of conflicts in consumer relationships marked by structural asymmetry, aiming solely at promoting techniques apt to restore material balance and the controllability of performances in continuous digital environments.
