6.3 On open auditability and functional value equivalence as criteria of legitimacy for platform architectures

View this section in the PDF

This section in the layers of the site

  • Dogmatics · Sections of chapter 6

p. 635

The private governance of the IOC cannot be controlled only by the simplistic question about the abstract possibility of change. In platform ecosystems, changing is part of the very way of performing. The legal problem, therefore, does not lie in finding out whether the platform may alter the environment, but in establishing the criteria by which legitimate mutation is distinguished from mutation that breaks the balance of supply, degrades the promised enjoyment or makes the very possibility of control opaque. This shift is decisive because it prevents the controversy from being imprisoned between two equally bad extremes: on one side, the fantasy of the immobility of the digital object; on the other, the fantasy, symmetrically convenient to the supplier, that every ordinary mutation would be automatically legitimate because it derives from the “nature of the service”. In IOCs, mutability exists, but it does not hover above the Law.

After reconstructing semiotic vulnerability, the duty to inform in the key of adequacy, advertising as controllable production of expectation and abusiveness as a phenomenon that may reside in the entire arrangement of supply, it is no longer enough to assert generically that the platform must act with good faith, transparency and respect for the consumer. It is necessary to convert such requirements into operational criteria capable of guiding proof, attribution and remedy. The task is precisely to avoid this fate, offering standards capable of disciplining the legitimacy of platform architectures without depending either on the civil-law ontology of ownership of the digital good or on the scattered hunt for isolated clauses.

The first difficulty faced by the section stems from the object itself. In continuous digital services, a large part of the controversies does not arise from abrupt and conspicuous suppression, but from progressive alterations, functional reconfigurations, interface changes, redefinitions of equivalence, visibility adjustments, nerfs, resets, rule migrations and other moves that, taken in isolation, may seem trivial, but that, together or with sufficient intensity, restructure the acquired experience. The consumer remains formally “inside” the ecosystem, still has the account, still sees the object, can still access the platform. Even so, the utility enjoyed may be substantially downgraded, and the ability to prove this downgrading may be simultaneously eroded by the very way the environment is administered. It is precisely against this combination of normalized mutability and operational opacity that the section proposes two complementary criteria.

p. 636 The first of them is open auditability. The name must be read carefully so as not to produce misunderstanding. It is not a matter of requiring the opening of code, a total search of the infrastructure, full publicity of internal systems or theatrical laboratory transparency. Open auditability works on a more sober and more legal plane, since it designates the requirement of minimum institutional verifiability of supply. In other words, the platform cannot claim to alter the regime of enjoyment and, at the same time, keep in absolute secrecy what it actually altered, when it altered, why it altered, what relevant effects the change produces and which records allow the episode to be reconstructed in case of conflict. The standard does not require exposure of trade secrets as a purifying fetish. It does require a demonstrable minimum capable of preventing information, good faith and transparency from turning into empty performances.

Open auditability thus arises as a direct response to the functional opacity typical of IOCs. If the supplier controls the architecture, the criteria of alteration, the relevant logs, the timing of the change and the very means by which the modification will be perceived, it concentrates not only power of reconfiguration, but also power to narrate unilaterally what occurred. The legal risk there is obvious: without minimum trails, without minimally reconstructible reasoning and without the possibility of confronting promise and mutation, the consumer is reduced to the ridiculous position of feeling that he lost something important, but being unable to demonstrate institutionally what he lost, how he lost it, or why the loss exceeds the trivial annoyance inherent to networked life. Open auditability prevents exactly this erosion of control. It requires that the platform environment remain, at least to a minimum degree, reconstitutable.

This has quite concrete consequences. Open auditability extends to communications of alteration, history of relevant versions, confirmation screens, change notices, transaction records, logs sufficient to trace materially relevant interventions, minimum documentation of the supply flow and minimally intelligible explanation of the criteria invoked for relevant reconfigurations. It is neither necessary nor desirable to turn the supplier into a fully transparent oracle. What is required is something simpler and more serious: that the platform cannot benefit legally from the very opacity it produces. In terms of proof, this means that the supply regime needs to be institutionally reconstructible. In terms of legitimacy, it means that important mutations cannot rest only on the platform's naked authority. In terms of remedy, it means that the absence of trails and verifiability ceases to be an operational detail and becomes part of the unlawful act itself.

p. 637 The second standard is functional value equivalence. If open auditability controls the verifiability of the change, functional value equivalence controls the substance of the enjoyment preserved or degraded by the change. The core of the idea is simple, though dogmatically powerful: in IOCs, the integrity of what was acquired cannot be measured only by the nominal survival of the object nor by the formal permanence of access. The fact that something continues to exist inside the account, remains technically accessible or continues to bear the same name is not enough, by itself, to demonstrate that the acquisition was preserved in its legally relevant value. In a mutable environment, what matters is the functional equivalence of enjoyment, that is, the substantial preservation of the practical utility and the sense of use that justified consumption.

This criterion is necessary because the platform supplier tends to operate with a very comfortable defense: nothing was taken away, everything merely “evolved”; nothing was suppressed, merely “rebalanced”; nothing was lost, merely “readjusted to the ecosystem”. Functional value equivalence prevents this managerial vocabulary from serving as a free pass for relevant downgrading of the experience consumed. What is asked, from it, is not whether the object is still there in a nominal sense, but whether the change preserves, to a legally acceptable extent, the set of functions, advantages, possibilities of use, practical value and symbolic density that made up the legitimately expected enjoyment. When this equivalence is relevantly broken, the mutation ceases to be a simple ordinary variation of the service and begins to touch the very core of the acquisition.

This standard is particularly useful because it escapes two traps. The first is the temptation to reduce every conflict to proprietary language, as if only a strong notion of ownership could protect the consumer against unilateral alterations. The section refuses this shortcut. It is not necessary to demonstrate full dominion over the object to maintain that the acquired enjoyment cannot be arbitrarily downgraded. The second trap is the trivialization of mutability as a supposedly natural trait of digital services, an argument that usually serves as technological varnish for opportunistic changes. Functional value equivalence allows, precisely, a much more robust intermediate position: it is recognized that the platform may alter, but it is required that the alteration preserve, in substantial terms, the functional value of the acquired experience or, failing that, open room for restoration, correction, revision or another adequate remedy.

The force of the criterion appears clearly in cases such as removals of relevant functionalities, nerfs that empty acquired utility, interface changes that disorganize ordinary use, redefinitions of rules that downgrade the equivalence of what was promised, p. 638 alterations of currency or internal purchasing power, mutations in progression systems, reclassifications of rarity, erasure or compression of inventories, and changes in visibility patterns that affect the consumer's position in the ecosystem. In all these cases, the decisive question should not be whether the platform continued to offer “something” with the same label. The correct question is whether the thing legally acquired, as a position of enjoyment in a governed environment, retains sufficient functional equivalence with what was the object of the consumer's expectation, trust and investment.

It is important to note that functional value equivalence does not freeze the ecosystem. It does not require absolute identity between before and after, nor does it prohibit rebalancings, adjustments, corrections or evolutions of the environment. What it prevents is the use of mutability as a screen for the opportunistic destruction of value. The supplier is not obliged to fossilize the service, but neither may it take refuge in the idea of permanent evolution to legitimize any relevant reduction of the utility enjoyed. Instead of a simplistic opposition between immobility and total sovereignty of the platform, the standard introduces a more sophisticated test of legitimacy: the change may occur, provided it remains within margins compatible with the substantial preservation of enjoyment or is accompanied by adequate justification, verifiable transparency and fitting restoration when equivalence is broken.

It is there that the two criteria meet. Open auditability and functional value equivalence are not parallel standards that march without touching. They presuppose each other. Without auditability, functional equivalence is difficult to demonstrate, because the consumer cannot reconstruct what was altered, to what extent, with what motivation and at what moment. Without functional equivalence, auditability runs the risk of becoming transparency without material density, that is, a simple capacity to narrate changes without a criterion to assess their substantive legitimacy. One standard controls the how of the mutation; the other, the how much and the what of the mutation. One prevents institutional opacity; the other prevents functional downgrading disguised as ordinary management. Together, they offer something the debate lacked: a decision grammar capable of disciplining the legitimacy of platform architecture.

This complementarity makes it possible to reorganize the central question of controversies in IOCs. One no longer asks, in an impoverished way, whether or not the platform has the right to alter. One asks whether the alteration was accompanied by minimum conditions of verifiability and whether it preserved, in a substantially acceptable way, the functional equivalence of the acquired enjoyment. When one of these conditions fails, the legitimacy of the change already becomes legally suspect. When both fail at the same time, the case comes strongly close to a defect p. 639 of supply, an abusive practice, a breach of trust or even an architecture of structural opacity incompatible with the consumer-law regime. The section, thus, does not turn every alteration into an unlawful act. It only refuses to let alteration become an automatic alibi for irresponsibility.

Semiotic vulnerability had shown that the platform controls the means by which the consumer understands, compares and proves his own enjoyment. The duty of information, read in the key of adequacy, had shown that volume and performative transparency are not enough. Advertising, treated as market semiotics, had revealed how expectation is curated in advance and is legally binding. Hypercuratorial anticipation had demonstrated that architecture, interface and platform dynamics operate together on the decision. Now, all this needs to be converted into a criterion of legitimacy for ordinary mutations of supply. That is exactly the role of open auditability and functional value equivalence.

Under such standards, there no longer remain only bad dogmatic exits. It is no longer necessary to reduce the conflict to a civil-law ontology of ownership of the digital good, which would be narrow for governed consumption. Nor is there a need to remain trapped in the microscopic analysis of isolated clauses, as if the platform only produced unlawfulness through badly drafted contractual sentences. The focus comes to rest on the architecture of supply and on the material controllability of its mutations. In governed ecosystems, the relevant unlawful act is often not in a heroically villainous clause, but in the way the environment changes, justifies itself poorly, erases traces and preserves only the formal appearance of continuity.

When the controversy involves removals, rule alterations, interface changes, ecosystem rebalancings, compression of functionalities, redefinitions of equivalence or reconfigurations of value, the interpreter will have two central tests at hand. First, was there minimum institutional verifiability of the change, with reasoning, timing, effects and trails for reconstructing supply? Second, did the alteration preserve sufficient functional equivalence with the legitimately acquired enjoyment, or did it relevantly downgrade the practical and symbolic core of the object? From there, it already becomes possible to discipline proof, calibrate claims, structure remedies and control the privatist narrative according to which everything that occurs within the platform would be merely the neutral exercise of its freedom of management.
