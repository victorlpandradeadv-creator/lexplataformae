6.2.5 On information, advertising and abusive practices in contexts under hypercuratorial anticipation

See this section in the PDF

p. 629

In platform contexts, information, advertising and abusive practices cannot be examined as autonomous phenomena, isolated in watertight compartments, because, in the real process of consumption, they operate jointly in the formation of expectation, urgency, trust and tolerance to frustration. The legal problem is no longer exhausted in knowing whether a given clause existed, whether certain information was technically available or whether an advertising piece contained literal falsity. What needs to be controlled is the integrated decision arrangement through which the platform organizes the consumer's adhesion to and permanence in the p. 630 ecosystem, distributing visibility, scarcity, comparability and promise of value in a way functional to its own interests. In IOCs, this becomes even more sensitive because the consumed object does not stabilize as a thing delivered once and for all. The consumer's decision falls on a future governed experience, continuously modulated by architecture, curation and enforcement, so that the initial expectation comes to make up the very legal substance of enjoyment.

This finding requires an important methodological shift. Information cannot be read only as declaratory content; advertising cannot be treated as promotional ornament; and the abusive practice cannot be reduced to a marginal event subsequent to the contract. What exists, in many cases, is a single communicational and decisional environment in which promise, selective visibility, managed scarcity, symbolic rewards, choice architecture and rituals of acceptance act together to produce adhesion. Transparency, in this frame, is not a mere formal requirement of the final instant of contracting, but a presupposition that must run through the entire process of the consumer's approach to the object, from the capture of their attention to the settling of their expectation as to what will be obtained, lost or tolerated as admissible frustration718. For this reason, the legality of the relationship cannot be assessed only by the final text of the bond, but by the set of pre-contractual and contextual manifestations that shaped the will and the consumer's perception of risk.

It is precisely in this framework that hypercuratorial anticipation gains dogmatic relevance. The platform does not administer only the present experience of enjoyment. It administers, before it, the very horizon of expectation on the basis of which the consumer decides. Temporary events, selected shop windows, curated rarity, artificial urgency, promises of distinction, teaserization of utility, suggested equivalences and cycles of emotional intensification make up a grammar of anticipation that acts on the way the consumer learns to desire, to hope and to fear loss. The environment comes to govern not only access to the object, but the future interpretation of the value of this object. This p. 631 profoundly alters the reading of information, advertising and abusiveness, because the promise is no longer a lateral phenomenon. It becomes a structural component of the regime of governed enjoyment.

In these contexts, the platform produces something more sophisticated than simple ordinary commercial persuasion. It builds cycles of promise, intensification and partial delivery, in which the object is presented as a rare, prestigious, limited or virtually guaranteed opportunity, even though effective enjoyment depends on later mechanisms of randomness, mutability, internal rebalancing or unilaterally defined equivalences. The consumer is induced to adhere not only by a stated advantage, but by an architecture of expectation that teaches them to read that acquisition as a decisive moment, an imminent loss or a singular chance. The decision, thus, is formed within an environment that administers in advance the meaning of value. And it is precisely there that relevant omission, misleading advertising and abusiveness can arise in an articulated way, with no need for a frontal lie or for a complete absence of information.

Relevant omission appears when the essential is deliberately displaced out of the decisional focus. The supplier emphasizes urgency, benefit, rarity or prestige, but relegates to lateral conditions what really defines the substance of enjoyment, such as relevant limitations of use, ordinary mutability of the object, equivalence parameters, possible losses, restricted windows of use, additional requirements for the promise to materialize or specific conditions for maintaining the advertised value. Misleading advertising, in turn, manifests itself when the image of utility or equivalence built by the promotional environment finds no verifiable correspondence in the effective experience of enjoyment, either because the platform later downgrades the object, or because the initial promise already concealed, through ambiguity or an architecture of visibility, the real way in which utility would be delivered. Abusive advertising, finally, appears when the design of the environment systematically exploits the consumer's vulnerability, compressing their space for deliberation through artificial urgency, symbolic reward, ritualization of acceptance and fragmentation of the relevant conditions.

This reading prevents two symmetrical errors. The first would be to moralize every technique of commercial persuasion, as if all intense advertising, all interface curation or all architecture aimed at conversion were, in itself, unlawful. That is not it. The market can promote, persuade, dramatize and organize interest. What the CDC does not admit is that this organization of interest operate through functional opacity, capture of expectation and displacement of the essential out of the useful field of understanding. The second error would be to imagine that there is only a problem when the advertising piece, in isolation, contains literal falsity or when a certain p. 632 clause, in isolation, is abusive. In contexts of hypercuratorial anticipation, unlawfulness can reside precisely in the articulation between elements that, seen separately, seem neutral, but that, combined, govern the consumer's decision in a structurally asymmetrical way.

At this point, the role of advertising needs to be brought back to its correct dogmatic place. It is not external to the contract, nor simple cultural ambiance of the market. Marketing communication actively participates in manufacturing the disposition to contract, inflating needs, organizing desires and simultaneously presenting the path to their satisfaction719. Instead of a clean and compartmentalized sequence between advertisement, information and contracting, what exists is a communicational continuum in which the platform produces expectation, guides the reading of the object, administers the rhythm of the decision and only then stabilizes acceptance in documentary forms that frequently do not reflect, with the same emphasis, the promise that triggered the bond. This means that abusiveness cannot be located only in the final statement, because the pre-contractual environment itself already participates in the material constitution of consent.

This conclusion is reinforced when one observes that, on platforms, marketing communication frequently dissolves into hybrid formats. Recommendation, entertainment, tutorial, community narrative, demonstration of use, influencer content and advertising piece are confused, reducing the consumer's critical vigilance and scrambling the perception of the persuasive status of the message. In these cases, the legal problem lies not only in the content communicated, but in the very way it is inserted into the decision flow, diminishing the intelligibility that it is a commercial push and maximizing its aptitude to produce adhesion720. The abusive arrangement, then, does not depend on an p. 633 explicit banner or a classic advertisement. It may reside precisely in this dissolution of advertising into the environment, which makes it more effective as a technique for steering behavior and more opaque as a source of obligation and control.

This has direct consequences for the relationship between information, advertising and abusive practices. In IOCs, the supplier does not merely transmit messages about a previously given object. It administers signs and metrics that constitute the very perceived value of enjoyment. Advertising does not surround the object externally; it helps produce it as an expectation of performance, distinction, utility or permanence. Information does not serve only to describe this object; it defines to what extent the consumer can understand its real conditions of use and its ordinary margins of alteration. And the abusive practice does not appear only when there is brutal imposition or a scandalous clause; it can emerge from a decision ecosystem in which urgency, scarcity, performative transparency and elastic equivalences work together to induce adhesion in a context of materially defective understanding.

The duty to inform, in this context, can no longer be conceived as an obligation of textual deposit. When the platform operates under hypercuratorial anticipation, informing comes to mean making reconstructible, at the exact moment of decision, that which the anticipatory curation itself tries to obscure. It is not enough for text to exist. The essential content must be presented in a comparable, hierarchized, conspicuous and timely way. If the promotional architecture of the environment creates intensity and scarcity to hasten the decision, the duty to inform needs to function as a counterweight to this acceleration, and not as a bureaucratic ritual consummated in background documents. Likewise, advertising cannot be evaluated only by its microscopic statement, because its aptitude to deceive or abuse also depends on how it is inserted in the flow, what it highlights, what it omits, what equivalences it suggests and the way it administers the perception of time, risk and loss.

The most important methodological consequence of the item is this: consumer-law analysis must consider the whole of the communicational and decisional arrangement, and not only isolated fragments of its formal expression. The jurist needs to ask how the expectation was produced, at what moment the relevant conditions were revealed, with what emphasis they appeared, what was the effective sequence between promise and acceptance, and whether the consumer had real means to p. 634 understand and compare what was offered to them. This places choice architecture back within the field of the CDC without technological mysticism and without creating a parallel law of platforms. It is merely a matter of recognizing that, when information, advertising and design operate together to govern the decision, legal control also needs to reach them in an integrated way721.

With this shift, two operators are prepared. The first is open auditability, because, if abusiveness can reside in the communicational regime of the supply, it is necessary that this regime be institutionally reconstructible, with minimum preservation of screens, flows, messages, promotional pieces, interface alterations and decision trails. The second is functional value equivalence, because the expectation produced by hypercuratorial anticipation cannot be discarded as promotional smoke when it served precisely to induce the acquisition and shape the understanding of the object. If the platform promises a curated experience of value, rarity, performance or advantage, and later rebalances or downgrades it under opaque criteria, the frustration is not a mere annoyance. It may be a relevant inadequacy of the governed enjoyment itself.

In contexts under hypercuratorial anticipation, information, advertising and abusive practices belong to the same dogmatic problem. The platform does not merely offer an object; it organizes the semiotic field in which this object will be desired, understood and accepted. It is in this field that the consumer learns what counts as an advantage, what losses they should fear, what costs they can ignore and what frustrations they should bear as normal. When this organization of meaning is conducted so as to displace the essential, manipulate expectation and induce tolerance to loss under an appearance of freedom of choice, the CDC is not facing peripheral details of the digital experience. It is facing the very contemporary mode of exploitation of vulnerability in IOCs.

Notes

  1. The consumer-law discipline of the decision environment does not authorize artificially separating information, advertising and the formation of will as if each of these dimensions operated in an isolated compartment. In the real process of consumption, especially in contexts of hypercuratorial anticipation, these elements act in an integrated way to shape the consumer's expectation, urgency, trust and practical disposition to adhere to and remain in the bond. The meaning of the transparency mandate can be traced back to the search for the “true and free will of the consumer”, functioning as a “precondition” for them to freely express their will and realize their legitimate expectations, that is, those that led them, “duly informed about the product or service, aware of their future contractual rights and duties”, to choose a certain supplier as a contractual partner Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 818-819). This shows that transparency is not limited to the final instant of acceptance, but must characterize every pre-contractual manifestation of the supplier in the market. ↩
  2. Advertising and offer, in the consumer market, do not operate as watertight acts. Marketing communication participates in manufacturing the very disposition to contract by inflating needs, organizing desires and simultaneously presenting the path to their satisfaction, leading the consumer to the culminating moment of adhesion Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, [p. 91]). For this reason, the legal reading of the decision process cannot artificially separate, into autonomous compartments, advertising, information and contracting, as if each of these layers acted in isolation. This inseparability is accentuated because the marketing message usually brings together, in a single arrangement, concrete offers, fanciful promises and accessory advantages integrated into a marketing mix, in such a way that the social action of advertisers becomes a source of duties and obligations Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, [p. 95]). In contexts under hypercuratorial anticipation, abusiveness is not located only in an isolated statement, but may emerge from the communicational regime of the supply as a whole, that is, from the integrated way in which offer, persuasion and management of expectation are articulated to steer the consumer's decision. ↩
  3. Communicational unlawfulness, in the CDC, is not restricted to frontal, conspicuous advertising easily identifiable as such. It can also arise in hybrid, disguised arrangements or ones incorporated into apparently neutral media, in which the commercial message infiltrates narrative contexts that reduce the consumer's critical vigilance and intensify their vulnerability to inducement Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, [p. 123-125]). This occurs when marketing communication presents itself as entertainment, reporting or neutral recommendation, although it functions, in practice, as a vector of commercial steering, which is why the responsibility of the outlet lies precisely in not allowing confusion between advertising and editorial matter Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, [p. 123-125]). The legal problem, in these cases, lies not only in the content conveyed, but in the very scrambling of the status of the message. In contexts of hypercuratorial anticipation, abusiveness may result from the entire regime of inducement, and not only from a classic advertising piece, above all when commercial communication dissolves into hybrid formats that confuse the consumer about the nature of what they see, read or follow. ↩
  4. The legal analysis of contractual and communicational mechanisms that affect the consumer's decision cannot be compartmentalized without a serious loss of adherence to the reality of the bond. Under the CDC regime, the consumer contract is not reduced to the final text of the predisposed clauses, because the consumer's will is formed throughout a relational process in which advertising, information provided, commercial practices, accessory promises and inducement techniques materially participate in building expectation and adhesion. The interpreter of the consumer contract must observe not only the rule of Article 47 of the CDC, but all the rules of the Code that establish rights and duties of consumer and supplier, so that “the content of the contract to be interpreted is not only that ‘placed’ in clauses pre-drafted unilaterally by the supplier, but also the entire prior context that constitutes the offer”, such as advertising, brochures, information and commercial practices Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 1015). This contextual interpretation seeks the meaning of the contract also in its negotiating context, considering prior acts and information as legally relevant and forming the whole to be considered Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 1030). Consequently, in environments under hypercuratorial anticipation, Consumer Law must examine together the mechanisms of information, advertising and choice architecture, since it is precisely from this integrated operation that the capture of expectation, the management of urgency and the exploitation of vulnerability may emerge. ↩
