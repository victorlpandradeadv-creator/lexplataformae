6.2.5 On information, advertising and abusive practices in contexts under hypercuratorial anticipation

See this section in the PDF

p. 629

In platform contexts, information, advertising and abusive practices cannot be examined as autonomous phenomena, isolated in watertight compartments, because, in the real process of consumption, they work together to form expectation, urgency, trust and tolerance of frustration. The legal problem is no longer exhausted by asking whether a given clause existed, whether certain information was technically available or whether an advertisement contained a literal falsehood. What needs to be controlled is the integrated decision-making arrangement through which the platform organizes the consumer's joining of and staying in the p. 630 ecosystem, distributing visibility, scarcity, comparability and the promise of value in ways that serve its own interests. In IOCs, this becomes even more delicate because the consumed object does not settle into a thing delivered once and for all. The consumer's decision concerns a future governed experience, continuously modulated by architecture, curation and enforcement, so that the initial expectation becomes part of the very legal substance of enjoyment.

This finding calls for an important methodological shift. Information cannot be read only as declaratory content; advertising cannot be treated as promotional ornament; and abusive practice cannot be reduced to a marginal event after the contract. What exists, in many cases, is a single communicational and decision-making environment in which promise, selective visibility, managed scarcity, symbolic rewards, choice architecture and rituals of acceptance act together to produce adhesion. Within this frame, transparency is not a mere formal requirement of the final moment of contracting, but a prerequisite that must run through the consumer's entire approach to the object, from the capture of their attention to the settling of their expectation about what will be obtained, lost or tolerated as admissible frustration718. For this reason, the legality of the relationship cannot be assessed solely by the final text of the bond, but by the whole set of pre-contractual and contextual manifestations that shaped the consumer's will and perception of risk.

It is precisely within this framework that hypercuratorial anticipation acquires dogmatic relevance. The platform does not administer only the present experience of enjoyment. Before that, it administers the very horizon of expectation on which the consumer bases the decision. Temporary events, selected storefronts, curated rarity, artificial urgency, promises of distinction, the teasing of utility, suggested equivalences and cycles of emotional intensification make up a grammar of anticipation that acts on how the consumer learns to desire, to expect and to fear loss. The environment comes to govern not only access to the object, but the future interpretation of its value. This p. 631 profoundly alters how information, advertising and abusiveness are read, because the promise is no longer a side phenomenon. It becomes a structural component of the regime of governed enjoyment.

In these contexts, the platform produces something more sophisticated than ordinary commercial persuasion. It builds cycles of promise, intensification and partial delivery, in which the object is presented as a rare, prestigious, limited or virtually guaranteed opportunity, even though actual enjoyment depends on later mechanisms of randomness, mutability, internal rebalancing or unilaterally defined equivalences. The consumer is induced to join not only by a stated advantage, but by an architecture of expectation that teaches them to read that acquisition as a decisive moment, an imminent loss or a unique chance. The decision is thus formed within an environment that administers the meaning of value in advance. And it is precisely there that relevant omission, misleading advertising and abusiveness may arise together, with no need for an outright lie or a complete absence of information.

Relevant omission appears when the essential is deliberately pushed out of the decision-making focus. The supplier emphasizes urgency, benefit, rarity or prestige, but relegates to side conditions what really defines the substance of enjoyment, such as relevant limits on use, the object's ordinary mutability, equivalence parameters, possible losses, narrow windows of use, additional requirements for the promise to materialize or specific conditions for keeping the advertised value. Misleading advertising, in turn, manifests itself when the image of utility or equivalence built by the promotional environment finds no verifiable correspondence in the actual experience of enjoyment, either because the platform later downgrades the object, or because the initial promise already concealed, through ambiguity or an architecture of visibility, the real way in which utility would be delivered. Abusive advertising, finally, appears when the design of the environment systematically exploits the consumer's vulnerability, compressing their room for deliberation through artificial urgency, symbolic reward, the ritualization of acceptance and the fragmentation of the relevant conditions.

This reading avoids two symmetrical errors. The first would be to moralize every technique of commercial persuasion, as if all intense advertising, all interface curation or all architecture aimed at conversion were unlawful in itself. That is not the case. The market may promote, persuade, dramatize and organize interest. What the CDC does not allow is for this organization of interest to operate through functional opacity, the capture of expectation and the displacement of the essential out of the useful field of understanding. The second error would be to imagine that a problem exists only when an advertisement, taken in isolation, contains a literal falsehood or when a given p. 632 clause, taken in isolation, is abusive. In contexts of hypercuratorial anticipation, unlawfulness may lie precisely in the combination of elements that seem neutral when seen separately but that, combined, govern the consumer's decision in a structurally asymmetrical way.

At this point, advertising needs to be returned to its correct dogmatic place. It is neither external to the contract nor mere cultural ambiance of the market. Marketing communication takes an active part in manufacturing the disposition to contract, stoking needs, organizing desires and at the same time presenting the path to their satisfaction719. Instead of a clean, compartmentalized sequence of advertisement, information and contracting, what exists is a communicational continuum in which the platform produces expectation, guides the reading of the object, manages the pace of the decision and only then stabilizes acceptance in documentary forms that often fail to reflect, with the same emphasis, the promise that triggered the bond. This means that abusiveness cannot be located only in the final statement, because the pre-contractual environment itself already takes part in the material constitution of consent.

This conclusion is reinforced by the observation that, on platforms, marketing communication often dissolves into hybrid formats. Recommendation, entertainment, tutorial, community narrative, product demonstration, influencer content and advertisement blur together, lowering the consumer's critical vigilance and muddling the perception of the message's persuasive status. In these cases, the legal problem lies not only in the content communicated but in the very way it is inserted into the decision flow, making it less intelligible that this is a commercial push and maximizing its capacity to produce adhesion720. The abusive arrangement, then, does not depend on an p. 633 explicit banner or a classic advertisement. It may reside precisely in this dissolution of advertising into the environment, which makes it more effective as a technique for steering behavior and more opaque as a source of obligation and control.

This has direct consequences for the relationship between information, advertising and abusive practices. In IOCs, the supplier does not merely transmit messages about a pre-given object. It manages signs and metrics that constitute the very perceived value of enjoyment. Advertising does not surround the object from outside; it helps produce it as an expectation of performance, distinction, utility or permanence. Information does not serve only to describe this object; it determines how far the consumer can understand its real conditions of use and its ordinary margins of alteration. And an abusive practice does not appear only when there is brutal imposition or an outrageous clause; it can emerge from a decision ecosystem in which urgency, scarcity, performative transparency and elastic equivalences work together to induce adhesion in a context of materially defective understanding.

In this context, the duty to inform can no longer be conceived as an obligation of textual deposit. When the platform operates under hypercuratorial anticipation, informing comes to mean making reconstructible, at the exact moment of decision, what the anticipatory curation itself tries to obscure. The mere existence of text is not enough. The essential content must be presented in a comparable, hierarchized, conspicuous and timely way. If the promotional architecture of the environment creates intensity and scarcity to hasten the decision, the duty to inform must work as a counterweight to this acceleration, and not as a bureaucratic ritual fulfilled in background documents. Likewise, advertising cannot be assessed only by its microscopic statement, because its capacity to deceive or abuse also depends on how it is inserted in the flow, what it highlights, what it omits, what equivalences it suggests and how it manages the perception of time, risk and loss.

The most important methodological consequence of this item is the following: consumer-law analysis must consider the communicational and decisional arrangement as a whole, and not only isolated fragments of its formal expression. The jurist must ask how the expectation was produced, when the relevant conditions were revealed, with what prominence they appeared, what the actual sequence between promise and acceptance was, and whether the consumer had real means to p. 634 understand and compare what was being offered. This brings choice architecture back within the field of the CDC without technological mysticism and without creating a parallel law of platforms. It is simply a matter of recognizing that, when information, advertising and design operate together to govern the decision, legal control must also reach them in an integrated way721.

This shift prepares two operators. The first is open auditability, because, if abusiveness can reside in the communicational regime of supply, that regime must be institutionally reconstructible, with minimum preservation of screens, flows, messages, promotional pieces, interface changes and decision trails. The second is functional value equivalence, because the expectation produced by hypercuratorial anticipation cannot be dismissed as promotional smoke when it served precisely to induce the acquisition and shape the understanding of the object. If the platform promises a curated experience of value, rarity, performance or advantage, and later rebalances or downgrades it under opaque criteria, the frustration is not a mere annoyance. It may be a relevant inadequacy of the governed enjoyment itself.

In contexts under hypercuratorial anticipation, information, advertising and abusive practices belong to the same dogmatic problem. The platform does not merely offer an object; it organizes the semiotic field in which this object will be desired, understood and accepted. It is in this field that consumers learn what counts as an advantage, which losses they should fear, which costs they can ignore and which frustrations they should bear as normal. When this organization of meaning is conducted so as to displace what is essential, manipulate expectation and induce tolerance of loss under the appearance of freedom of choice, the CDC is not facing peripheral details of the digital experience. It is facing the very contemporary mode of exploiting vulnerability in IOCs.

Notes

  1. The consumer-law discipline of the decision environment does not permit information, advertising and the formation of will to be artificially separated as if each of these dimensions operated in an isolated compartment. In the real process of consumption, especially in contexts of hypercuratorial anticipation, these elements act together to shape the consumer's expectation, urgency, trust and practical disposition to adhere to and remain in the bond. The meaning of the transparency mandate can be traced back to the search for the “true and free will of the consumer”, functioning as a “precondition” for consumers to express their will freely and fulfill their legitimate expectations, that is, those that led them, “duly informed about the product or service, aware of their future contractual rights and duties”, to choose a given supplier as a contractual partner Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 818-819). This shows that transparency is not limited to the final instant of acceptance, but must characterize every pre-contractual manifestation of the supplier in the market. ↩
  2. In the consumer market, advertising and offer do not operate as watertight acts. Marketing communication takes part in manufacturing the very disposition to contract by stoking needs, organizing desires and at the same time presenting the path to their satisfaction, leading the consumer to the culminating moment of adhesion Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, [p. 91]). For this reason, the legal reading of the decision process cannot artificially split advertising, information and contracting into autonomous compartments, as if each of these layers acted in isolation. This inseparability is heightened because the marketing message usually combines, in a single arrangement, concrete offers, fanciful promises and ancillary advantages integrated into a marketing mix, so that the social conduct of advertisers becomes a source of duties and obligations Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, [p. 95]). In contexts under hypercuratorial anticipation, abusiveness is not located only in an isolated statement, but may emerge from the communicational regime of supply as a whole, that is, from the integrated way in which offer, persuasion and management of expectation are articulated to steer the consumer's decision. ↩
  3. Under the CDC, communicational unlawfulness is not restricted to frontal, conspicuous advertising that is easily identifiable as such. It can also arise in hybrid or disguised arrangements, or in ones embedded in apparently neutral media, in which the commercial message infiltrates narrative contexts that lower the consumer's critical vigilance and heighten their vulnerability to inducement Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, [p. 123-125]). This occurs when marketing communication presents itself as entertainment, reporting or neutral recommendation, although in practice it works as a vehicle of commercial steering, which is why the outlet's responsibility lies precisely in not allowing advertising and editorial matter to be confused Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, [p. 123-125]). In these cases, the legal problem lies not only in the content conveyed but in the very blurring of the message's status. In contexts of hypercuratorial anticipation, abusiveness may result from the entire regime of inducement, and not only from a classic advertisement, above all when commercial communication dissolves into hybrid formats that confuse the consumer about the nature of what they see, read or follow. ↩
  4. The legal analysis of contractual and communicational mechanisms that affect the consumer's decision cannot be compartmentalized without seriously losing touch with the reality of the bond. Under the CDC, the consumer contract is not reduced to the final text of the pre-drafted clauses, because the consumer's will is formed over a relational process in which advertising, the information provided, commercial practices, ancillary promises and inducement techniques take a material part in building expectation and adhesion. Whoever interprets a consumer contract must observe not only the rule of Article 47 of the CDC, but all the provisions of the Code that establish the rights and duties of consumer and supplier, so that “the content of the contract to be interpreted is not only that ‘placed’ in clauses pre-drafted unilaterally by the supplier, but also the entire prior context that constitutes the offer”, such as advertising, brochures, information and commercial practices Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 1015). This contextual interpretation also seeks the meaning of the contract in its negotiating context, treating prior acts and information as legally relevant and as part of the whole to be considered Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 1030). Consequently, in environments under hypercuratorial anticipation, Consumer Law must examine the mechanisms of information, advertising and choice architecture together, since it is precisely from this integrated operation that the capture of expectation, the management of urgency and the exploitation of vulnerability may emerge. ↩
