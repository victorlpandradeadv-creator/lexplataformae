6.5 From the social function of intellectual property to the semiotic governance of immaterial objects of consumption

p. 679

p. 680 Intellectual property matters here only insofar as it organizes power over enjoyment. In ecosystems of continuous digital consumption, it does not function merely as a technique for protecting creation or as an instrument of abstract economic exclusivity. It operates, in practice, as part of the legal infrastructure through which the platform defines what can be accessed, maintained, altered, withdrawn, audited and contested. The problem, therefore, is not merely knowing whether there is an exclusive right bearing on software, content, interface or related assets. It lies in understanding how this exclusivity comes to structure the consumption experience itself, conditioning continuity, intelligibility, functional equivalence and the user's real possibilities of reaction in the face of the mutability of the environment.

Intellectual property must be prevented from functioning as a theme parallel, competing or hierarchically superior to consumer protection. In continuous digital services that operate Immaterial Objects of Consumption, exclusivity does not hover above the relationship as an abstract shield of ownership. It bears on the relationship from within the supply regime, as an element of governance of the enjoyed object.

It is not enough to ask who technically controls the environment or who holds, in the abstract, the prerogative to modify the system. The legally relevant question is a different one: what limits apply when this control, founded on exclusivity, is exercised over continuous enjoyment, over expectations legitimately incorporated into the relationship and over the conditions of verifiability and contestation of the performance.

In this framework, intellectual property should be neither reduced nor demonized. Exclusivity remains legitimate and fulfills real functions of incentive, organization and protection. The point is only to refuse to read it as automatic authorization for opacity, for opportunistic mutability or for shielding against control. When exclusivity comes to operate, in the context of supply, as a technique of unilateral reconfiguration of utility, of blocking auditability, of compressing reversibility or of naturalizing opaque sanctioning regimes, it ceases to be mere protection of an asset and becomes part of the very dogmatic problem of the private governance of the IOC.

This also requires an important distinction. Not every intellectual property infrastructure offers the same type of exclusivity, nor authorizes the same claims of control. Especially in the case of software, the Brazilian discipline does not lead to an unrestricted functional sovereignty over the whole consumed experience. The legal protection of the program explains a relevant portion of the technical governance of the environment, but is not enough, by itself, to convert architecture, sanction, mutability, secrecy and the blocking of contestation into effects immune to consumer law. In IOC platforms, the exercise of exclusivity must p. 681 remain compatible with the social function, with the protection of trust, with the functional equivalence of enjoyment and with minimal requirements of verifiable transparency.

The central question, then, is neither to revise the general theory of intellectual property nor to place it back at the center of the thesis. It is to delimit its place within the IOC regime. It appears as a structural variable of the private governance of the object, and precisely for that reason it must be read in light of the same limits that apply to that governance when it affects the adequacy, intelligibility, permanence and controllability of enjoyment. Instead of functioning as an argument for immunity, intellectual property comes to be treated as a field of compatibilization. Its exercise remains protected, but it is not separated from the constitutional order, nor does it escape the control of abusiveness, nor does it dissolve the duty of restoration when exclusivity is used as a technique for degrading the consumed experience.

With this, exclusivity ceases to appear as the final word and comes to appear as an organized legal problem. What is under examination is not the exclusive right in itself, but the way it projects onto immaterial objects of consumption whose utility depends on a governed environment. Where exclusivity serves to preserve creation and organize the ecosystem, it remains intelligible on its own terms. Where it serves structural opacity, opportunistic reconfiguration, the blocking of auditability and the capture of enjoyment under the language of license and secrecy, it must be brought back within the limits imposed by the social function, by consumer protection and by the containment of private power. It is at this point that intellectual property ceases to be a screen and becomes, itself, part of the architecture that the law needs to control.
