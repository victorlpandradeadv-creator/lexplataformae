4.2.1 Choice architecture and informational domination

See this section in the PDF

p. 334

In light of the foregoing, the problem ceases to be only how signs, visibility and social validation sustain value in continuous immaterial consumption and becomes how the ecosystem is administered. It requires describing governance by architecture and by information, that is, the way in which the platform organizes the consumer's field of action without resorting to explicit commands, operating through design patterns, access flows, classification criteria and modulation instruments that make certain conducts more likely and others more costly.

p. 335 Instead of reducing these dynamics to individual preferences or to failures of contractual transparency, what matters is to describe how choices are configured and how the regime of intelligibility of the environment is controlled, because the public legibility of the ecosystem and the support of the position of enjoyment that economically constitutes the IOC depend on this control. “Information”, here, is not to be confused with the duty to inform of the Consumer Protection Code, understood as the obligation to disclose data on price, characteristics, risks, conditions and limitations of the supply.

What is at stake is informational power exercised through classification, ranking, curation and production of legibility of the environment, by which the platform defines what will be visible, comparable, recognizable and sanctionable and, with this, structures the practical conditions of enjoyment and permanence. Even if there are formal openings of information, the asymmetry may remain, because the relevant core is control over the operational criteria that organize opportunities and consequences. For this reason, informational domination designates government through data and through categories, and not mere insufficiency of documentary transparency.

Julie E. Cohen371 (2019) examines how patterns of entitlement, disentitlement372 and institutional change are reconfigured around data, platforms and intellectual property regimes, as well as how courts, regulation and transnational governance come to operate under a rationality that tends to protect the interests of informational capital and to hinder countermovements of social protection. The work offers a political economy framework of informational power that makes it possible to describe the platform as an instance that combines information, architecture and law to organize conduct, distribute opportunities and consolidate structural asymmetries, providing a conceptual basis for treating informational domination as a material component of vulnerability in IOC regimes.

p. 336 The operational description of governance by architecture requires treating protocols, standards and informational devices as practical means of ordering, because the platform defines the space in which activities become possible and legible. Along these lines, modularity is not a simple engineering choice, for it administers the tension between diversity of uses and ease of operation, expanding certain flexibilities and blocking others Cohen(Cohen, 2019, p. 42). When informational protocols and networked devices reshape spaces and practices, they encode dependencies and habits of behavior and stabilize paths as if they were natural Cohen(Cohen, 2012, [p. 19]). With this, architecture and information operate as infrastructure of everyday governance, creating tracks of conduct and limiting alternatives silently, without this being confused with formal law or with state authority, but still with normative effects dense enough to reorganize the possibilities of acting within the ecosystem.

Governance by architecture, for this scope, designates the way in which design and infrastructure decisions operate as the practical grammar of the environment, configuring the consumer's field of action before any explicit command. Defaults, standard paths and interface layers define ordinary enjoyment, while selective frictions make deviations costly and, at times, impracticable. Access flows, rhythms of use and visibility criteria organize what will be found, compared and recognized as value. In this regime, the platform does not merely present options. It also distributes probabilities and costs, decides where the user enters by default and which signals will suffice to produce prestige, belonging or sanction. With this, ordering stabilizes the experience and structures the administered market.

This description can be thickened by a vocabulary drawn from studies of interactivity, especially when one seeks to understand why the subjective experience of choice does not eliminate, but may even conceal, the objective density of architectural control. In examining interactive narratives and games, Lesnovski observes that this type of experience is organized by permanent tensions between choice and control, illusion and simulation, action and identity, culminating in the production of an “experience of controlled freedom”, in which the user's action always occurs in relation to a structure that precedes and conditions it Lesnovski(Lesnovski, 2014, p. 7). The usefulness of this formulation, for the present scope, lies in allowing the platform to be described not as a neutral space in which the consumer simply exercises pre-constituted preferences, but as a technical environment that distributes margins of action within a previously designed grammar. Freedom of choice, in this framework, does not disappear, but appears already shaped by paths, responses, hierarchies of visibility and p. 337 regimes of consequence that silently delimit what can be done, found, recognized and maintained.

The point becomes even more precise when the author notes that the one who interacts is foreseen in the structure itself and that the interactive experience is formed in the coupling between stimuli of the system and responses of the participant, without the latter ceasing to act, but also without acting in exteriority to the environment that organizes him Lesnovski(Lesnovski, 2014, p. 16; p. 18). In a similar direction, in addressing systems of reward and punishment, Lesnovski shows that they do not operate only as episodic reactions to isolated conducts, but as forms of control by the system over free and potentially deviant actions, keeping the experience in flow, avoiding aporias and leading the one who interacts back to the structurally foreseen path Lesnovski(Lesnovski, 2014, p. 79-80). Transposed to platform governance373, this means that choice architecture is not limited to the presentation of options, because it also involves the administration of the consequences that make certain trajectories more fluid, rewarded and legible, while others become more costly, frustrating or marginal. Informational domination, thus, falls not only on what the consumer knows, but on the very practical economy of his possibilities of acting.

This design becomes even clearer when the field of action is described not merely as a generic “digital environment”, but as a specific technical territory, in which the consumer's entry is mediated by a single point of passage. In the Riot Games ecosystem, p. 338 this point of passage is the client374, which centralizes authentication, social features, access to the match and, above all, the economic showcase. What appears as “interface” ceases to be a neutral layer of presentation and comes to operate as infrastructure of everyday governance, because it is there that the platform decides what will be placed in the foreground, which tracks will be offered as the standard path and which possibilities will be kept secondary, fragmented or simply improbable in ordinary use.

The structure of the store and of navigation does not function merely as convenience. It organizes a regime of visibility in which the platform distributes attention and creates practical opportunities through curation and highlighting, even when the user imagines he is “just choosing”375. This effect is particularly pronounced in personalized offer mechanisms, in which the platform converts activity records into individualized discounts, transforming the consumer's history into an economic operator of his own field of action Riot Games(Riot Games, 2023b). The point is not the discount as a one-off benefit, but the institutional asymmetry between what is measurable for the user and what is intelligible to him. The consumer sees metrics and superficial signals, such as win rates, match history and performance records, while the criteria for recommending and routing offers remain encapsulated in the internal regime of the service, which chooses what will be presented as an “opportunity” under parameters that do not lend themselves, as a rule, to external verification376.

p. 339 In this key, informational domination is realized less by an isolated act of “lack of transparency” and more by the administration of the variables that make the environment governable and responsive at scale. The client is, at the same time, the layer that stabilizes the experience and the means by which it is continually reconfigured. This reconfiguration does not appear as an exception, but as the normal mode of governing the service, for the relationship itself is structured to admit recurring changes through updates, adjustments and reorganizations. The possibility of redistributing visibility, recalibrating access flows and redefining functionalities by patch and update, including without prior notice, gives institutional density to the idea of governance by architecture, since the “space” in which the consumer acts is not simply given, but is produced and revised as a continuous provision Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e)377.

The modulation produced by architecture does not operate as a psychological suggestion directed at individual choices, because it is structured as an objective condition of possibility of acting in the ecosystem. Normalization advances when choice becomes constrained and dependent on previously defined trajectories, and the very criterion of what appears as normal is constructed and stabilized by technical and discursive arrangements, since code participates in this process at the same time as it is shaped by it Cohen(Cohen, 2012, [p. 144]). Standards and protocols do not serve only interoperability, for they organize practical hierarchies and define forms of conformity. For this reason, standardization can shift normative authority to the standard itself and, simultaneously, open opportunities for the exercise of economic and political power Cohen(Cohen, 2019, p. 218). In this scenario, defaults, frictions and tracks of use cease to be interface details. They come to operate as mechanisms of exclusion and steering, because they delimit alternatives, naturalize paths and make conduct predictable through the structural arrangement of the environment, even when there is no explicit prohibition.

The connection with the IOC regime becomes more precise when the object is described as a position of enjoyment and not as an isolated unit whose delivery is exhausted in the acquisition. This position exists economically only when it remains commensurable and recognizable within the governed environment, in which architecture distributes visibility, stabilizes patterns of reading and structures access paths. For this reason, the relevant materiality of the IOC is not limited to the promised content, for the object is configured by the infrastructure that defines practical conditions of enjoyment and preserves, or degrades, its public commensurability. Value, thus, is continually p. 340 constructed by the way the ecosystem makes the acquired position verifiable, rankable and sustainable over time.

Classification, ranking and recommendation do not function as informative accessories. They define who appears, who is found and which trajectories of recognition become practicable, so that visibility is converted into a condition of success and reputation becomes a governed asset. Cohen (2019, p. 43) records this shift by observing that competitive or reputational success in a platform environment depends on informational strategies aimed at visibility before other users. The algorithmic mediation of flows, in turn, is not limited to ordering content already given, for it anticipates preferences and distributes attention by selecting what will be exposed as relevant to each user Cohen(Cohen, 2012, [p. 158]). The result is that legibility, reputation and access cease to be neutral attributes of the market and come to operate as instruments of effective ordering that organize hierarchies and condition the positional value of enjoyment within the platform regime.

Informational domination, for this scope, designates the power derived from concentrated control over the variables that make experience measurable, classifiable and sanctionable in the ecosystem, such as data, metrics, ranking criteria, recommendation parameters and activity records. The core is not concealment of clauses or price, but administration of the categories and signals by which enjoyment will be recognized and converted into value. The consequence is an institutional asymmetry, since the consumer perceives the interface and its effects, while the platform controls the criteria of recognition and, with this, decides what will be visible, rewarded, demoted and practically contestable.

Informational domination consolidates when opacity ceases to be a contingency and comes to function as a structural condition of governance itself, because transparency tends to operate in only one direction. In societies marked by surveillance and classification, there is little public transparency about the algorithms and parameters used to categorize and order people, which concentrates decision-making power in whoever controls the criteria Cohen(Cohen, 2012, [p. 108]). In this configuration, the regulated subject may receive superficial signals of performance and conformity, but remains removed from the judgments and metrics that produce such results. This becomes more acute when systems display simplicity on the surface and complexity in their internal functioning, which makes it difficult to translate underlying judgments into forms suitable for public understanding Cohen(Cohen, 2019, p. 191). The practical consequence is the compression of contestability, since discussing the proportionality, coherence and grounds of p. 341 decisions becomes difficult when parameters of categorization, ordering and validation of the environment remain inaccessible or incomprehensible to whoever bears the effects.

Defaults organize the user's entry and stabilize tracks of enjoyment, because they determine where the experience “begins” and which choices emerge as natural, while selective frictions make deviations more expensive, multiply steps, impose waits or require specific competences to reach results off the script. Added to this is the administration of time as a structural operator of the field of action, since access windows, events, expirations, returns and update rhythms convert the availability of content into a practical condition of permanence, producing urgency and functional scarcity through calendar and recurrence. Finally, visibility functions as the economic language of the ecosystem, for rankings, metrics and signals of prestige make public comparison continuous and make the user's position continually legible and rankable. In this set, normativity need not present itself as a frontal prohibition to organize conduct, because it is inscribed in paths, costs, temporalities and signals that define what is easy, what is rare and what will be recognized as value.

Governance by architecture does not stabilize as a one-off act of command, because it operates by continuous modulation of the environment through informational flows, recurring adjustments and reorganizations that recalibrate, at every moment, conditions of visibility, relevance and access. Along these lines, Cohen mobilizes the notion of modulation as a form of control exercised by continuous flows of information that circulate to, from and about individuals, producing permanent calibration of the conditions of enjoyment Cohen(Cohen, 2012, [p. 171]). The consequence is a regime in which control is exercised through structural updating and constant reorganization of the environment, without eliminating individual agency or reducing the dynamic to determinism, but making the ecosystem continually responsive to the priorities of intermediation and predictability at scale.

In parallel, informational extraction comes to produce representations optimized for aggregate governance, in which flows of personal data are worked to form operational profiles aimed at modulating behavior systematically Cohen(Cohen, 2019, p. 67). These informational doubles do not function as neutral portraits of the user, for they serve as instruments of routing and indirect intervention, by making possible decisions of classification and exposure that bear on opportunities, rewards and trajectories of recognition. The result is that modulation is not restricted to the micro level of the interface, because it is articulated with governance at scale that reorders patterns of behavior and distributes risks through patterns of intermediation and continuous data processing.

p. 342 Moderation reinforces this normativity by transforming internal policies into technical effects, grading sanctions, demotions and functional restrictions that redefine the user's position in the environment and condition the continuity of his enjoyment. When these criteria remain opaque or only partially comprehensible, contestation loses traction, because the conflict ceases to bear on an explicit act and comes to fall on invisible parameters of classification and routing. The result is predictability and conformity by infrastructure, since conduct adjusts to what the ecosystem recognizes, rewards and keeps accessible, while alternatives remain formally possible, but practically disadvantageous.

In competitive ecosystems such as League of Legends, the simple moral reading of the player's performance and behavior runs, from the outset, into a prior structural fact: the environment is neither transparent nor fully intelligible to those who inhabit it. The very technical description of the game shows that it is an experience marked by partial observability, camera limitation, fog of war, high decisional density and strong dependence on context, so that the player's perception bears only on a contingent fraction of the real state of the match (Jalovaara378, 2024, p. 12). The problem is not only empirical, but methodological, because the very analytical modeling of the game state requires simplifications and choices of representation capable of making tractable a high-dimensional and multimodal state space, without this eliminating the underlying complexity of the competitive environment Jalovaara(Jalovaara, 2024, p. 23). In this scenario, ranked play can no longer be treated as a neutral field in which merit, fault and deviation would present themselves in a way immediately verifiable by the ordinary user.

This picture worsens when one observes that, even in matches governed by a matchmaking system aimed at giving roughly equivalent chances to both teams, pre-game prediction studies reach relevant levels of accuracy from variables such as draft379, performance history and the player's proficiency with the chosen champion, which shows that the match does not begin on a competitive blank slate, but on a p. 343 prior distribution of probabilities Jalovaara(Jalovaara, 2024, p. 17-18). It should also be noted that simple aggregate statistics, such as raw win rate, do not constitute a reliable basis for comparing strategic decisions, precisely because the environment is traversed by selection bias: certain actions are chosen when the player is already ahead and others as a last resort in desperate situations, which artificially inflates or depresses their apparent results Jalovaara(Jalovaara, 2024, p. 7; p. 24). In this way, if even the serious analytical attempt to estimate strategic value in League of Legends depends on rigorous contextualization so as not to produce unreliable inferences, then the simple moral attribution of the behavioral cost to the isolated player weakens when formulated from an environment whose internal logic remains only partially accessible to him.

This point gives the question of the private ban legal density. If the supplier structures the competitive environment, controls its communication channels, concentrates the means of collecting and reading the relevant variables and, at the same time, applies graduated sanctions that lower eligibility, lock progression and restrict communication privileges, then punishment cannot be read merely as a neutral response to a self-evident individual deviation. In official documentation, Riot presents Honor as a system that rewards sportsmanlike conduct with additional benefits and penalizes inappropriate behavior with reduced rewards and limited communication privileges, in addition to linking Honor levels to different availabilities of chat, social recognition and access to rewards. In parallel, the company states that penalties such as temporary suspension and chat restrictions drop the Honor level, lock progress in the system and make the account ineligible for rewards linked to the Hextech Crafting regime until there is consistent demonstration of behavioral “reform” Riot Games(Riot Games, 2017a; 2017b; 2025a).

The point, therefore, is not to sustain, without internal proof, a maximalist thesis of deliberate sabotage, but to recognize that the company governs a competitive environment that is structurally opaque, probabilistic and administered by internal criteria whose in-depth intelligibility is not shared with the consumer to the same extent that their effects are imposed on him. In this key, the central question ceases to be only whether the user broke a rule and comes to include the legitimacy of the very criteria by which the deviation is produced, read and punished.

When the platform governs the board, measures the match, organizes the probabilities of the environment, controls the means of visibility and concentrates the parameters of sanction, the textual externalization of reactivity cannot be taken as an autonomous datum, isolated from the architectural and informational context that conditions it. The relevant asymmetry, here, is not p. 344 merely contractual. It is epistemic, operational and sanctioning. The player bears the emotional, reputational and functional effects of the experience, but does not have the same access to the criteria, metrics and framings by which that same experience is classified, interpreted and converted into grounds for demotion, restriction or exclusion. It is on this plane that informational domination ceases to be mere lack of transparency and comes to appear as a problem of the legitimacy of private enforcement in continuous digital services.

On this plane, the problem lies not only in the existence of sanctions, but in the strategic selection of what will be most easily visible, measurable and punishable within the governed environment. The platform can keep relatively sheltered the deeper architecture that produces frustration, dependence, asymmetry of reading and competitive tension, while concentrating enforcement on manifestations that are more legible, cheap to detect and publicly presentable as unequivocal individual deviation. A selective economy of private discipline is thus formed: the structural arrangements that condition the experience are preserved as a neutral background, while what emerges as the most superficial, traceable and sanctionable symptom of the ecosystem is exposed as a personal problem. When the same agent designs the environment, measures its effects and chooses where to exercise punitive power most intensely, the legitimacy of enforcement ceases to be a presupposition and comes to require heightened justification.

There is, moreover, an especially relevant question regarding proportionality, which prevents naturalizing as reasonable the banning of the account when applied as an ordinary response to minor infractions committed through a functionality under the platform's full control, such as verbal aggression in chat. When the sanctioned conduct is carried out through a specific communicational functionality, detachable, graduable and fully controllable by the platform itself, the response primarily compatible with the structure of the problem tends to be the progressive restriction of that functionality, and not the complete suppression of the position of enjoyment built by the user in the ecosystem.

Riot itself describes Honor as a tiered regime of recognition and discipline, in which communication privileges, progression and access to benefits may be reduced, locked or conditioned by reason of the player's behavior Riot Games(Riot Games, 2025a). If the supplier therefore has automatic means to silence, limit, time out, block or condition the restoration of chat according to the seriousness and repetition of the infraction, the elimination of the entire account comes to exceed the neutralization of the concrete harm and takes on the character of an expansive private penalty, for it reaches not only the faculty p. 345 abusively exercised, but the entirety of the investments, histories, signs and positional advantages accumulated by the consumer.

More than that, total expulsion for infractions of this nature reveals a potentially dysfunctional sanctioning rationality, because the very design of the ecosystem allows the user to return almost immediately through a new account, provided he bears the cost of rebuilding his trajectory and, possibly, reinvesting economically in the environment. In this hypothesis, the sanction does not properly eliminate the agent from the game, but destroys the previous account and transfers to the consumer the burden of rebuilding, from scratch, the patrimonial, functional and symbolic elements of his position of enjoyment. The measure, then, is no longer justified merely as necessary containment of the harmful conduct, for it also operates as an extreme mechanism of discipline and private dispossession, in a context in which the platform could have neutralized in a much less burdensome way the exact vector through which the infraction manifested itself.

Technical enforcement shows how the normativity of the ecosystem is realized through architecture, by combining restrictive technologies, contractual regimes and legal prohibitions on interference to produce configurations that delimit, with granularity, the ranges of permitted conduct Cohen(Cohen, 2012, [p. 122]). With this, the effectiveness of the rule shifts to the level of implementation, because the possibility of acting comes to be filtered before the interpretive dispute and before the subsequent sanction. Thus, the norm ceases to depend only on ex post interpretation and application and comes to operate as a prior condition of possibility, converting governance into a design of permissions and blocks incorporated into the environment itself.

Along the same lines, compliance functions come to be measured and applied by the code itself, and automation adds opacity to the enforcement circuit, for the technical layer through which compliance is measured and imposed tends to conceal decisive criteria and operations Cohen(Cohen, 2019, p. 192). The result is that internal policies cease to depend on public controversy and come to operate as restrictions and filters incorporated into the environment, capable of producing automatic compliance. At the same time, the translation of nuanced requirements into technical binaries may introduce tensions between formal compliance and substantive adherence.

Thus, in the IOC regime, the consumer is not bound only by a contract, because he comes to operate in a governed frame in which architecture, information and technical enforcement define the practical conditions of access, permanence, visibility and recognition. This structure gives institutional density to vulnerability, for the asymmetry is not limited to ignorance of clauses or to difficulties in comparing prices, but derives from p. 346 concentrated control over the criteria that make enjoyment legible, rankable and continuable, as well as from the capacity to recalibrate such criteria over time.

It is not, therefore, a neutral informational advantage or a merely contingent technical superiority. What is observed is the functional exploitation of a structurally privileged position, by which the supplier designs the environment, controls the criteria of legibility of its own operations and reserves for itself the faculty of recalibrating, without equivalent transparency, the parameters that condition access, recognition, permanence and sanction. The asymmetry, here, does not act as a side datum of the digital market, but as an ordinary resource for governing the ecosystem, apt to produce administered vulnerability for the benefit of the intermediation model itself. When the platform reserves for itself the power to structure the field of action, interpret its effects and redefine the practical consequences of enjoyment, one is no longer faced with simple efficient organization of the service, but with the interested exercise of private power over existential conditions of continuous consumption.

For this reason, legal control cannot be content with the documentary reading of the supply as if it were exhaustive, because the effective shaping of the object is produced by continuous operations of the ecosystem that may degrade the acquired position, reorder reputations and impose functional losses without proportional transparency and without compatible contestability. The discussion must bear on the operational criteria that govern enjoyment and distribute risks of exclusion, demotion and loss of commensurability, for it is on this plane that private power becomes controllable without the platform being treated as the State.

The problem is institutional. Networked governance based on standards shifts authority to protocols, standards and algorithmic intermediations and produces practical sovereignty in everyday life. For this reason, the mismatch between this arrangement and the ideals of the Rule of Law points to a problem of institutional design that must confront arbitrary power and build accountability before networked publics, while at the same time recognizing the growth of the practical sovereignty of platforms Cohen(Cohen, 2019, p. 237).

This requirement can be formulated in operational terms, because institutional changes suited to the current scenario need to deal directly with dematerialization, datafication and platformization, as well as to develop modalities of oversight and liability compatible with algorithmic accountability, platform power and governance by networks and standards, which demands new constructs of legitimation and new institutional forms Cohen(Cohen, 2019, p. 270-271).

p. 347 Without proportional mechanisms of accountability, qualified transparency and effective contestability, infrastructural power tends to operate as a barely tempered functional authority. It is private power that, through technical and informational design, reorganizes exposure, access and conditions of recognition, producing effects that do not fit a purely contractual reading. This requires bringing the debate back to the legal tradition of responding to technical reconfigurations of power, in search of criteria of limitation and accountability compatible with the practical regulatory function exercised in the ecosystem.
