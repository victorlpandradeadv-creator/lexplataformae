Situation b) · 7.1.2 of the thesis

When the consumer only understands what the interface lets them understand

b) semiotic vulnerability and governance of meaning

Which facts show meaning governed by the supplier?

Expectation, perception of value, risk assessment or capacity to contest that depend on signs, interfaces and trails controlled by the supplier. The simplest case is a purchase advertised in the storefront as an acquisition and characterized in the contract as a revocable license.

How do you reconstruct the consumer's journey?

The reconstruction of the user's journey, the informational architecture, the communicational salience and the paths of understanding. Useful evidence includes dated screenshots, screen recordings and the purchase, acceptance and confirmation screens, with the messages about rarity, time remaining and discounts. The claim is no longer that the consumer did not understand but that the environment was designed in a certain way.

Which rules deal with vulnerability and information?

Article 4, item I, of the CDC1 recognizes the consumer's vulnerability, which takes its own form in digital environments. Article 6 of the CDC2 guarantees adequate information, protection against misleading advertising and facilitation of the consumer's defense, and Article 39, item IV, of the CDC3 prohibits taking advantage of the consumer's weakness.

Is semiotic vulnerability presumed?

Semiotic vulnerability is not a generic attribute of the digital, and it does not presume the consumer's incapacity. It requires a governance of meaning that acts structurally on understanding, comparability, predictability or contestation. Combined with other elements, it may ground the redistribution of the burden of proof. The expression digital vulnerability names the setting in which the harm occurs, but it does not isolate the mechanism. Semiotic vulnerability targets the unequal administration of meaning and, for that reason, gives proof a sounder framework and better prepares the remedy. It is not a psychological trait of the consumer but a structural mechanism of power.

When it applies
When expectation, perception of value, risk assessment or capacity to contest depend on signs, interfaces, flows and informational trails controlled by the supplier.

Duty of supply
Not to organize the experience through an asymmetry of meaning incompatible with good faith, transparency and the legibility of enjoyment.
The corresponding duty is not to organize the experience through a structural asymmetry of meaning incompatible with good faith, transparency and the legibility of enjoyment.
Minimum evidence
The user's journey, the informational architecture, the communicational salience, the paths of understanding and the records that make it possible to reconstruct how the expectation was formed.
The minimum evidence required involves reconstructing the user's journey, the informational architecture of the environment, the communicational salience, the paths of understanding and the records available to reconstruct how the expectation was formed.
Possible legal qualification
Abusive practice, informational insufficiency, deceptiveness through ambiguity or relevant omission. Combined with other elements, it supports the dynamic redistribution of the burden of proof.
The possible legal qualification may sustain an abusive practice, informational insufficiency, deceptiveness through ambiguity or relevant omission and, when combined with other elements, a basis for the dynamic redistribution of the burden of proof.
Institutional consequence
The conflict is brought back to the governance of the environment, without moralizing the consumer's conduct, and reinforced duties of information, advertising and proof apply.
The institutional consequence consists in bringing the conflict back to the governance of the environment, avoiding moralization of the consumer's conduct and allowing reinforced duties of information, advertising and proof to apply.

The law cited on this page

  1. art. 4, I, of the CDC ↩

    “Art. 4. The National Consumer Relations Policy aims at […] observing the following principles: I - recognition of the consumer's vulnerability in the consumer market; III - harmonization of the interests of participants in consumer relations […], always based on good faith and balance in relations between consumers and suppliers; VI - efficient restraint and repression of all abuses practiced in the consumer market […].”

    PT“Art. 4º. A Política Nacional das Relações de Consumo tem por objetivo […] atendidos os seguintes princípios: I - reconhecimento da vulnerabilidade do consumidor no mercado de consumo; III - harmonização dos interesses dos participantes das relações de consumo […], sempre com base na boa-fé e equilíbrio nas relações entre consumidores e fornecedores; VI - coibição e repressão eficientes de todos os abusos praticados no mercado de consumo […].”

    Wording transcribed in note 499, p. 511.

  2. Article 6 of the CDC ↩

    “Art. 6. The basic rights of the consumer are: I - protection of life, health and safety against the risks caused by practices in the supply of products and services considered dangerous or harmful; III - adequate and clear information about the different products and services […]; IV - protection against misleading and abusive advertising, coercive or unfair commercial methods, as well as against abusive practices and clauses […]; VI - the effective prevention and reparation of patrimonial and moral damages […]; VIII - the facilitation of the defense of their rights, including the reversal of the burden of proof […].”

    PT“Art. 6º. São direitos básicos do consumidor: I - a proteção da vida, saúde e segurança contra os riscos provocados por práticas no fornecimento de produtos e serviços considerados perigosos ou nocivos; III - a informação adequada e clara sobre os diferentes produtos e serviços […]; IV - a proteção contra a publicidade enganosa e abusiva, métodos comerciais coercitivos ou desleais, bem como contra práticas e cláusulas abusivas […]; VI - a efetiva prevenção e reparação de danos patrimoniais e morais […]; VIII - a facilitação da defesa de seus direitos, inclusive com a inversão do ônus da prova […].”

    Wording transcribed in note 500, p. 511.

  3. Article 39, item IV, of the CDC ↩

    “Art. 39. The supplier of products or services is prohibited from, among other abusive practices: IV - taking advantage of the weakness or ignorance of the consumer […]; V - demanding a manifestly excessive advantage from the consumer; XII - failing to stipulate a deadline for the performance of its obligation or leaving the setting of its starting date to its exclusive discretion.”

    PT“Art. 39. É vedado ao fornecedor de produtos ou serviços, dentre outras práticas abusivas: IV - prevalecer-se da fraqueza ou ignorância do consumidor […]; V - exigir do consumidor vantagem manifestamente excessiva; XII - deixar de estipular prazo para o cumprimento de sua obrigação ou deixar a fixação de seu termo inicial a seu exclusivo critério.”

    Wording transcribed in note 527, p. 520.
