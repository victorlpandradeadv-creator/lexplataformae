4.2.2 Private economic power and constitutional order

See this section in the PDF

This section in the layers of the site

  • Foundations · Sections of chapter 4

p. 347

The reorganization of immaterial consumption around governed ecosystems makes it insufficient to treat the conflict as a simple contractual divergence or as a fluctuation of individual preferences. The enjoyment that sustains the value of the IOC depends on an administered environment in which visibility, reputation, access, permanence and sanction are distributed according to internal criteria, by predominantly architectural and informational means. When the practical shaping of the object is produced by design, protocols, curation and technical enforcement, vulnerability ceases to be exhausted in occasional information deficits and takes on institutional density, since the asymmetry resides in the concentrated control of the variables that make the experience legible, commensurable and continuable over time, without proportional guarantees of intelligible publicity, verification and contestation. The question, then, is to situate this kind of private power within a tradition of legal reconstruction oriented by material changes in the conditions of social life, without depending on equating the platform with the figure of the State.

In their article, Warren and Brandeis380 ([201-?]) argue that recent inventions and expanding business practices intensified forms of intrusion that the legal order, if it remains tied to earlier schemes, cannot adequately capture. From there, the authors defend the recognition of a right to privacy as a legitimate right p. 348 to be let alone, distinguishing it from adjacent legal institutions that protect different interests, such as defamation, aimed at reputation before third parties, and literary/authorial property, aimed at the economic exploitation of publication. The text also sets out cautions and limitations, especially in situations of public interest and of exposure inherent to certain functions, and indicates legal remedies compatible with the structure of tort law, including damages and injunctive relief. The central gain lies in establishing a legitimate argumentative precedent. When the technical environment reorganizes vulnerability at scale, legal reconstruction is not moralism, but a necessary response to keep the effects of new forms of power and intrusion controllable in institutional terms.

The recourse to Warren and Brandeis does not operate by thematic identity, but by homology of situation. Technical change structurally alters the conditions of exposure, harm and vulnerability, and demands an adjustment of categories so that protection remains adherent to the real mode of production of the conflict. This does not imply equating platform with State nor constitutionalizing every private dispute, but recognizing that certain private powers, when exercised through architecture, information and enforcement at scale, generate asymmetries and risks that are not exhausted in contractual literalness. If reconstruction was necessary when techniques and business models reconfigured patterns of exposure and protection at the end of the nineteenth century, the same rationality justifies describing vulnerabilities produced by contemporary digital infrastructures in terms that allow legal control without resorting to simplifying fictions about market neutrality or about the sufficiency of formal will.

The structural orientation of this method becomes even clearer when the article shifts the problem from the sphere of individual vice to the social and economic machinery that makes intrusion reproducible at scale. The relevant critique is not organized as the reproach of an isolated agent, but as the diagnosis of an activity that becomes institutionalized as commerce and starts operating by its own logic. Warren and Brandeis describe the invasion of private life as an industry, pursued with method and effrontery, in which the mercantile dynamic reproduces itself by feedback, since supply itself manufactures demand381 Brandeis(Warren; Brandeis, [201-?], [p. 4]).

p. 349 By stating harm as the effect of a productive mechanism and of a circuit of manufactured demand, the text reinforces the idea that the adequate legal response is not moralization, but the reinterpretation of categories to make controllable a form of power whose operation is stabilized by technique, scale and business method. The consequence that matters here is that the focus falls on operating conditions and on the institutional organization of harm, opening room to recognize, in other environments of private governance, vulnerability produced by structure, and not by personal failure of the regulated subject.

Transposing this model to the IOC regime requires keeping the focus on the kind of power described. The core of the problem lies not in occasional breaches, but in a private infrastructure of practical governance capable of organizing, at scale, conditions of participation and enjoyment. In this environment, visibility and reputation function as economically relevant dimensions, internal rules are mutable, expectations are calibrated by curation and temporalization of the offer, and sanctions or functional restrictions may degrade the acquired position and reconfigure the economic meaning of enjoyment.

The relevant asymmetry stems from the concentrated control of the criteria that govern recognition, permanence and sanction, so that the consumer bears the effects without proportional access to the parameters that produce them. Once this kind of power is identified, it becomes justifiable to demand a legal language capable of following material changes in the conditions of enjoyment and vulnerability, without reducing the conflict to the literalness of the contract or resorting to fictions about the neutrality of intermediation.

The prudence of the design proposed by Warren and Brandeis appears clearly in the section on limitations, in which the right to privacy is framed as calibrated protection, incapable of functioning as a general instrument of informational blocking. Public interest functions as a limit, since protection does not extend to publications whose content is of public p. 350 or general interest, so as to preserve the circulation of information relevant to the community and to prevent privacy from becoming censorship Brandeis(Warren; Brandeis, [201-?], [p. 16]).

Their work also sets limits by institutional context, recognizing that certain informational flows are part of the very functioning of institutions and, therefore, cannot be paralyzed by the new protection, as occurs with publications made in judicial proceedings and in legislative bodies, as well as in equivalent communications Brandeis(Warren; Brandeis, [201-?], [p. 19]). The delimitation is completed by a criterion of proportionality associated with the means of propagation, admitting that, as a rule, there would be no redress for merely oral publication without special damage and that part of these occurrences must be tolerated to preserve freedom of expression, preventing the category from expanding to cover trivialities and preserving room for ordinary social practices without diffuse judicialization Brandeis(Warren; Brandeis, [201-?], [p. 19]).

The methodological consequence of this calibration is that the limitation of power does not depend on its public nature, but on the way it stabilizes and on the effects it produces on conditions of life and participation. When practical normativity is exercised through mutable internal rules, access architecture and opaque informational intermediation, the relevant deficit is not the absence of a public-law label, but the absence of institutional reciprocity proportional to the dominion exercised over recognition, permanence and sanction.

It is in this strict sense that the expression “without authorization” operates as a descriptive category, since it names a power of social ordering that does not submit, to an equivalent extent, to minimum requirements of intelligible publicity of criteria, accessible reasoning, decisional coherence and effective avenues of contestation compatible with the economic and identity relevance of what is at stake. From this point on, the task ceases to be discussing whether the relationship is private or public and becomes identifying which instruments of control make the rationality of the exercise of this power examinable, which places the tradition of legal reconstruction in the face of material changes once again as a parameter for building proportional limits and duties without resorting to statist fictions.

The institutional framework proposed by Warren and Brandeis also rests on a diagnosis of civilization and modern life, in which the need for protection emerges as a response to collective pressures and not as a casuistic reaction to isolated episodes. The text associates the expansion of legally protected interests with the fact that civilizational advance intensified intellectual and emotional life and heightened sensibility, making it more evident that a decisive part of human experience is not concentrated in physical goods Brandeis(Warren; Brandeis, [201-?], [p. 3]).

p. 351 Continuing, privacy is described as a functional condition for bearing modernity, since the intensity and complexity of life make some form of retreat from the world necessary and, under the refining effect of culture, the individual becomes more sensitive to publicity, making solitude and privacy more essential Brandeis(Warren; Brandeis, [201-?], [p. 4]). This allows protection to be treated as a mechanism of legal accommodation of new structural pressures, which reinforces the legitimacy of conceptual reconstructions when technical infrastructures and intermediation models reorganize, at scale, the conditions of exposure, enjoyment and vulnerability.

Once it is established that governance by architecture and by information constitutes a form of practical normativity capable of organizing access, visibility, permanence and sanction in environments of ecosystem-dependent enjoyment, the problem can no longer be stated as mere contractual friction and comes to demand a controllable language to describe limits and criteria of accountability compatible with the density of the dominion exercised. The contribution obtained situates the proposed shift within a tradition of legal reconstruction that responds to material changes in the conditions of social life, showing that, when techniques and business methods reorganize exposure and vulnerability at scale, the adequacy of categories is not voluntarism, but a condition of the institutional intelligibility of the conflict.

With this, it becomes possible to treat the asymmetry not as subjective discomfort, but as a structural deficit of proportional reciprocity, to be addressed by instruments that make the criteria, rationalities and impacts of the exercise of power inside the ecosystem examinable. This framework allows describing how governance produces adherence, self-adjustment and subjection through performance, comparison and the management of symbolic risk without individual moral attribution. With this, the position of enjoyment reveals itself also as a position of conformity and dependence, preparing the formulation of proportional duties and of parameters of transparency, stability and restoration adequate to the IOC regime.

Notes

  1. Entitled The Right to Privacy, the seminal article by Samuel D. Warren and Louis D. Brandeis ([201-?]), originally published by the Harvard Law Review in 1890, formulates a matrix of legal reconstruction in the face of technical and social mutations that alter conditions of exposure, vulnerability and harm. The text starts from the premise that the common law does not operate as a static system, but as a tradition capable of developing new protective contours when material changes reconfigure interests worthy of protection. Here, the interest of the argument is methodological and does not depend on institutional transplant between common law and civil law, since the point lies not in specific precedent techniques, but in the need to readjust legal categories when material transformation makes the available framework anachronistic. From this perspective, new technologies and new business models shift the boundary between the socially protected space and the sphere of subjection, and demand an adjustment of categories so that protection remains adherent to the real mode of production of the conflict. ↩
  2. Warren and Brandeis build The Right to Privacy as an institutional response to a material change in social life. The starting point is not a moral judgment about inconvenient individuals, but the perception that “Recent inventions and business methods call attention to the next step which must be taken for the protection of the person” Brandeis(Warren; Brandeis, [201-?], [p. 3]). Technical modernity structurally alters the conditions of exposure and capture of everyday life, and the text describes this passage with images of concrete intrusion, stating that “Instantaneous photographs and newspaper enterprise have invaded the sacred precincts of private and domestic life” Brandeis(Warren; Brandeis, [201-?], [p. 3]). The consequence is methodological, since inherited categories cease to capture the real mode of production of harm when technique and enterprise make intrusion reproducible at scale. In this key, the central critique shifts the focus from the “isolated agent” to the economic machinery that institutionalizes intrusion. The authors expressly state that “Gossip is no longer the resource of the idle and of the vicious, but has become a trade, which is pursued with industry as well as effrontery” and conclude, bluntly, that “In this, as in other branches of commerce, the supply creates the demand” Brandeis(Warren; Brandeis, [201-?]). Intrusion then comes to operate as a market practice, guided by circulation incentives and by a cumulative logic, in which repetition creates social tolerance and lowers standards, since “Each crop of unseemly gossip, thus harvested, becomes the seed of more” Brandeis(Warren; Brandeis, [201-?], [p. 3]). Harm, therefore, is not only the published episode, but the commercial circuit that manufactures appetite, normalizes exposure and expands vulnerability as a collective condition. It is from this diagnosis that the protective formulation emerges. The text proposes a protection that allows resisting intrusion without turning privacy into generalized censorship, articulating the right as “the right to be let alone” and, at the same time, setting limits and cautions, above all for public interest and for institutional contexts in which the circulation of information is functional Brandeis(Warren; Brandeis, [201-?], [p. 2]). The strength of the article lies in its design. Protection is presented as a necessary legal reconstruction when technique and business methods shift boundaries of protection and make the previous framework anachronistic, without depending on moralization and without assuming that only state powers can produce structural asymmetries. ↩
