Section 4 · pp. 261 to 264

4 Semiotics and governance of enjoyment in continuous immaterial consumption

View this section in the PDF

This section in the layers of the site

  • Foundations · Opening of chapter 4

Epigraph

MARINA, Oh No! (2010). What it announces · Read the epigraph in the PDF.

p. 261

The techno-media economic order of digital consumption does not operate only as market structure, infrastructure and continuous extraction of value. It projects itself, with equal force, onto the plane of signs and attention, where value and delivery cease to be perceived as stable attributes of a “good” and come to be constituted by administered visibility, reputation, metrics and belonging. In platform ecosystems, enjoyment is not reduced to using a functionality. It involves participating in a governed symbolic grammar, in which prestige, rarity, recognition and experience are part of the content delivered as perceived, and in which the promise is realized through reading codes and through equivalences built within the environment.

To make this regime knowable, semiotics is treated here as a material dimension of the legal problem, not as interpretive ornament. The consumption promise is stabilized by signs, interfaces and regimes of representation that define what “counts” as delivery and which equivalences are socially recognized as value. In this key, the contribution of Umberto Eco280 makes it possible to understand how codes and conventions make the performance legible and organize the consumer's expectation, whereas Guy Debord281 makes explicit the centrality of spectacle and of the circulation of images in shaping desire and adherence. Jean Baudrillard282 completes the axis by showing how, in attention economies, simulacrum and hyperreality shift the center of the commodity to its symbolic efficacy, causing consumption to present itself as access to experiences and positions, and not merely as acquisition of discrete utilities.

p. 262 This semiotic plane, however, does not function in a vacuum. It is governed. The platform appears as an instance of private administration that organizes enjoyment through architecture, rules and enforcement, defining access, permanence and visibility. This includes both explicit decisions, such as policies and sanctions, and decisions embedded in design, in curation and in the metrics that select what will be seen, rewarded and reproduced. In this sense, governance is not an “accessory” of consumption, but an operational condition of the performance itself. The contribution of Julie E. Cohen283 is mobilized to describe the platform as a form of organizing power that produces dependencies, structures conduct and naturalizes patterns of participation through technical and institutional means that are not exhausted in the contract.

The asymmetry that matters, on this plane, is not exhausted in a deficit of available information. It is formed as an inequality of codes and of language regimes that condition what can be perceived, compared and contested by the consumer within the environment. Luis Alberto Warat284 and Maurizio Gnerre285 are mobilized for this precise function, developing and fixing the perspective according to which vulnerability may arise from the difference between operational languages and languages of enjoyment, between vocabularies of promise and vocabularies of control, and between system metrics and expectations of experience. With this, semiotic vulnerability is maintained as a structural category, linked to intelligibility and agency, and not as an individual pathology or mere absence of “enlightenment”.

The legal problem thickens when this governance takes on total effects on the consumer's presence and reputation within the ecosystem. The prudential reference of Samuel p. 263 D. Warren II286 and Louis D. Brandeis287 is used as a methodological matrix to recalibrate categories in the face of technical mutations that reconfigure the field of incidence of rights and expectations, preventing the legal response from remaining trapped in material analogies that no longer reach the real experience of enjoyment. Next, Byung-Chul Han288 is mobilized to name psychopolitical dimensions of adherence and induced self-government, in which the bond is sustained by pressure of performance, comparison and continuous validation. Eros Roberto Grau289 contributes to recognizing that normativity can operate as an effective order imposed by an organization endowed with power, making it possible to qualify as practical governance what presents itself as a “condition of use” or “internal rule”, but produces structural effects on ways of life and social positions in the digital environment.

In this context, the asymmetry between supplier and consumer is not merely informational. It is structural, semiotic and institutional. It is structural because it stems from concentrated control of infrastructure, rules and channels of visibility. It is semiotic because it bears on the production of the very codes of value, reputation and delivery equivalence. It is institutional because it is stabilized by private procedures of adjudication, sanction and unilateral modulation, frequently opaque and with low contestability. The relevant vulnerability is not exhausted in “lack of information”. It involves asymmetry of intelligibility and of agency in an environment in which the consumer depends, to enjoy and to exist socially, on grammars, metrics and access regimes administered by the supplier.

The aim, here, is to make explicit how the capture of meaning and governed participation become constitutive components of continuous immaterial consumption, making legally visible losses and frictions that do not appear as classic nonperformance, but as degradation of enjoyment, downgrading of perceived value and corrosion of belonging through p. 264 technical and governance decisions. With this, semiotic vulnerability is stabilized as a structural asymmetry of intelligibility and agency in platform ecosystems, and the mechanisms are outlined by which enjoyment comes to depend on regimes of visibility, metrics and private sanction, to a degree sufficient to allow later legal qualification without depending on a demonstrative empirical narrative.

Notes

  1. A graduate in Philosophy from the Università di Torino, Umberto Eco was an Italian semiologist, philosopher, medievalist and cultural critic, recognized both for his academic contribution to semiotics and to the theory of interpretation and for his public projection as a writer. In 1975, he took up the chair of Semiotics at the Università di Bologna, where he also held academic leadership roles linked to communication and performing arts studies. ↩
  2. A graduate of the undergraduate program in Philosophy at the Université de Paris (Sorbonne), Guy Debord was a Marxist theorist, filmmaker and one of the main names of the Situationist International, an artistic and political movement active between the 1950s and the 1970s. Despite his non-academic trajectory in the strict sense, he is recognized for his role in the critique of consumer society, of symbolic alienation and of the use of the image as an instrument of social domination. His work proposes a radical analysis of the logic of the spectacle as a form of organization of life under late capitalism, standing out, for the purposes of study in this research, the book La Société du Spectacle (“A Sociedade do Espetáculo”, in the Brazilian edition), originally published in 1967. ↩
  3. Doctor in Sociology from the Université Paris X (Nanterre), Jean Baudrillard was a French philosopher, sociologist and cultural theorist of broad influence in the fields of communication, postmodern critique and philosophy of language. He is known for his critique of the culture of simulation and of the dissolution of reality into regimes of signs, especially in advanced consumer societies. ↩
  4. A graduate of Harvard-Radcliffe and with a J.D. from Harvard Law School, Julie E. Cohen is an American jurist and researcher who is a reference in law and technology, with prominent work in privacy, surveillance, intellectual property and platform governance. She is a professor at Georgetown University Law Center and conducts research on how informational infrastructures and legal arrangements reconfigure institutions and power relations in the contemporary economy. ↩
  5. Doctor in Law from the Universidad de Buenos Aires, Luis Alberto Warat was an Argentine jurist and legal philosopher based in Brazil, with decisive influence on Brazilian graduate legal education through a critical and avowedly interdisciplinary approach. His work puts traditional dogmatics under tension and invests in keys such as epistemology, language, semiology and legal pedagogy, in addition to engaging with psychoanalysis, art and political ecology. In the work “O direito e sua linguagem”, consulted for this research, Warat had the collaboration of Leonel Severo Rocha, Doctor from the École des Hautes Études en Sciences Sociales (EHESS), Paris, with a postdoctorate in Sociology of Law from the Università degli Studi di Lecce, a Brazilian jurist and researcher linked to legal sociology and general theory of law, with emphasis on systemic approaches and on the observation of law in contexts of high social complexity. ↩
  6. Doctor in Letters and Philosophy from the Università di Roma (La Sapienza) and with complementary training in anthropology at the University of California (Berkeley), Maurizio Gnerre is an Italian linguist-anthropologist recognized for research in ethnolinguistics and language contact in contexts of colonial and missionary expansion, as well as studies on linguistic ideologies and the semantics of unwritten indigenous languages. ↩
  7. A law graduate of Harvard Law School, Samuel D. Warren II was an American lawyer and businessman linked to the Boston elite and to the paper industry in Maine. He founded with Louis D. Brandeis the firm Warren & Brandeis (1879), predecessor of the current Nutter, McClennen & Fish. Also in partnership with Louis D. Brandeis, he published in 1890 the article The Right to Privacy, in which he systematizes civil protection against undue intrusions into private life, a protection that became an unavoidable reference in the history of the right to privacy. ↩
  8. A law graduate of Harvard Law School, Louis D. Brandeis was an American lawyer and, later, Associate Justice of the Supreme Court of the United States (1916-1939), recognized for his influence on progressive legal thought of the early twentieth century. ↩
  9. Doctor in Philosophy from the Albert-Ludwigs-Universität Freiburg, Byung-Chul Han is a South Korean philosopher based in Germany and a professor at the Universität der Künste Berlin. His work critically examines the culture of productivity, positivity and self-exploitation in neoliberal society. In reflecting on contemporary psychological exhaustion and the dissolution of the boundaries between life and work, Han proposes a critique of capitalist temporality and of invisible forms of domination. ↩
  10. Doctor in Law and livre-docente from the Faculty of Law of USP, Eros Roberto Grau is a Brazilian jurist and professor identified as one of the central names of Economic Law and of the constitutional reading of the economic order. He was full professor of the Department of Economic and Financial Law of USP and served as Justice of the Federal Supreme Court between 2004 and 2010. ↩
