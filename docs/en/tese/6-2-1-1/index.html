6.2.1.1 On the product-service dichotomy, long-term captive contracts and the continuous supply of IOCs

See this section in the PDF

p. 557

The product–service dichotomy reappears here not as a metaphysical enigma to be solved with a scholastic ruler, but as an instrumental variable for calibrating specific legal consequences in supplies that extend over time. With IOCs, enjoyment is not exhausted at the moment of initial availability, because it depends on continued maintenance, updating, modulation and administration by the supplier. As a result, the distinction between p. 558 immaterial good and performance activity remains legally useful, but insufficient as the sole axis for grasping the conflict. What is protected is not a frozen snapshot of the bond, but a consumption experience whose utility is realized and measured throughout performance. The legal structure remains in place: the CDC distinguishes product, including immaterial product, and service as an activity supplied in the market610, but this distinction only fulfills its function when read in light of the actual mode of enjoyment of the object consumed.

Therefore, the continuous supply of IOCs strains the classical model built around a completable delivery. The traditional logic works best when there is a relatively clear point of delivery and when inadequacy can be perceived by contrasting what was received with what should have been delivered. In lasting digital bonds, this point becomes blurred. The promised utility is not fulfilled all at once, because it remains dependent on the ecosystem that sustains it. Initial access may well occur without a hitch, but enjoyment may be downgraded later, through successive mutations, functional suppressions, silent reconfigurations or changes of environment that preserve the formal shell of the performance while corroding its concrete utility. Hence the importance of Article 26 of the CDC611, since it evidences the tension between the classical periods for ascertaining a quality defect and continuous performances in which inadequacy may emerge progressively, requiring a rereading of the point of cognizability instead of rigid fixation on the date of first access612.

The decisive technical point lies in placing quality defect and inadequacy back at the center of the analysis. In the CDC, the logic of quality of performance depends neither on dramatic collapse nor on p. 559 absolute uselessness. It operates on the basis of the concrete aptitude of the product or service to achieve the purpose legitimately expected by the consumer. For the IOC, this means that protection does not depend on proving the “disappearance” of the object; it is sufficient to show that enjoyment ceased to correspond, in a legally relevant way, to the functional standard that was part of the relationship. A product or service presents a quality defect of adequacy when it no longer corresponds to the legitimate expectation of use or enjoyment, or when the nonconformity compromises its serviceability613. In continuous bonds, therefore, relevant inadequacy is measured less by ostensive rupture and more by the maintenance, over time, of the utility legitimately incorporated into the bond614.

This is the point at which the parallel with long-term captive contracts becomes methodologically fertile. Not because the IOC should be forced into this category as if it solved everything, but because it helps to visualize something that continuous digital consumption lays bare: performance is not exhausted at the initial adhesion, and the bond comes to be structured by practical dependence, permanent asymmetry, sedimented trust and the supplier's renewed power of imposition over time. Lasting relationships, organized through mass contracting and an architecture opaque to the consumer, create captivity and dependence, p. 560 intensifying duties of information, cooperation, loyalty and protection of trust615. The consumer user of an IOC does not merely consume access, but enters a relationship marked by a legitimate expectation of continued access in an environment in which the future enjoyment of what they acquire remains subordinated to the private organization of the ecosystem.

It follows that contractual performance, in this type of bond, cannot be read as a simple sum of isolated performances. The obligation takes on a dynamic shape, projected in successive phases, and comes to incorporate legitimate expectations of stability, continuity and predictability. The contract ceases to be a one-off event and reveals itself as a program of performance developed over time, requiring collaboration, loyalty and respect for the concrete way in which enjoyment was organized and legitimately entrusted by the consumer616. The investment of trust arises not only from the initial promise, but also from repeated practice, from the continuity of utility and from the predictability of conduct that the supplier progressively sediments. Where this trust is built, unilateral changes or excessive instabilities affect not only the main performance, but the very contractual program concretely lived617.

Lasting bonds attract the ancillary duties of conduct with special intensity. Good faith, probity, qualified information, cooperation and protection of trust cease to be p. 561 moral adornments of performance and become part of the very structure of supply618. Long-term relationships carry expectations that do not fit the instantaneous model of exchange, because they rest on repeated coexistence, maintenance of service quality and a capacity to adapt without betraying the contracted utility619. With IOCs, this means that the protected core of the relationship is exhausted neither by the initial consent nor by the literal reading of the pre-drafted clauses; it includes the trust legitimately built around the material continuity of enjoyment.

This frame also reinforces the limit on unilateral mutability. The continued contract is not a space of the supplier's private sovereignty merely because performance remains open over time. Contractual freedom finds limits in the social function of the contract620, and, in continued consumer relationships, this limitation takes on a very concrete form: the power to alter, modulate or reconfigure performance cannot be exercised so as to frustrate the function that justified the consumer's original acquisition. In captive contracts, clauses that concentrate excessive discretionary power of revision in the supplier may place it in a position incompatible with good faith and with the stability of the promises that gave meaning to the adhesion621.

On the more classical plane of the CDC, this prohibition already appears with sufficient clarity. A clause authorizing unilateral modification of the content or quality of the performance after contracting is null, because it turns the consumer into a compulsory recipient p. 562 of a utility inferior to the one they legitimately acquired622. With IOCs, this rule gains surgical importance. The lowering need not come disguised as total cancellation; it may appear as a functional downgrade, compression of resources, loss of relational utility or systematic instability of the environment. Even so, it remains legally relevant because it breaks the balance of the relationship and diminishes the functional value of enjoyment without compatible restoration. Article 18 of the CDC623 therefore remains the central door for framing a relevant quality defect in a continuous environment, now measured by the yardstick of functional equivalence and not only by the classical image of the spoiled product on the shelf.

Nor does the offer disappear after adhesion. In lasting relationships, information, advertising and contractual performance remain connected, so that the promised content continues to function as a parameter of the performance's adequacy. All sufficiently precise information becomes part of the contract and binds the supplier624, and this holds with special force when the utility consumed is mediated by interface, visual presentation, promises of progression and the digital externalization of the experience. In the CDC, clear and adequate information does not govern only the pre-contractual moment; it affects the essence of the deal throughout the relationship, and a failure in it may constitute a quality defect of the performance625. In the digital environment, this is especially important because the “shop window” is not merely advertising: it is part of the very p. 563 substance of the IOC acquired. What was shown, described, promised and reiterated to the consumer continues to serve as a yardstick against opportunistic downgrades626.

The remedial consequence follows this logic. In lasting relationships, the relevant inadequacy of the performance need not be forced into the narrower filters of classical civil law, as if every revision depended on heroic unpredictability or contractual catastrophe. On the consumer-law plane, the breakdown of the objective basis of the deal, of the intrinsic balance of performances or of the essential purpose of the bond is enough to open room for revisional, resolutory or restorative protection627. In contracts of duration, this means that the relevant frustration of the utility legitimately incorporated into the relationship is already sufficient to trigger the protective system. The consumer may demand maintenance of the bond with correction of the performance, modification of the harmful clause, functional restoration of the utility or, as a last resort, rescission with the appropriate effects. Continuous performance does not reduce protection; it only changes the way of measuring the harm and choosing the remedy.

p. 564 This technical point is decisive because, in IOCs, performance projects itself into the future as a succession of periodic performances and counter-performances, but also as a legitimate expectation of maintenance of enjoyment over time. Where continuity is part of the contracted utility itself, quality defects, periods and remedies cannot be read as if everything had ended at the initial availability628. The relationship is not exhausted at the first activation, the first download or the first login. What is also protected is the material preservation of the enjoyment legitimately expected in the course of the bond. This changes the reading of inadequacy, of ascertainment and of legal response.

For this reason, the product–service dichotomy must be kept under strict methodological discipline. It matters insofar as it allows specific normative consequences to be adjusted, such as the technical regime of quality defects, the point of ascertainment, the applicable remedies and the calibration between functional restoration and compensation. It does not matter as a totalizing ontological key to the IOC. The utility consumed in a continuous digital environment is often hybrid: it involves an immaterial good and an activity of maintenance, access and modulation, without this authorizing the dispersal of protection into classificatory preciosity629. The central problem lies elsewhere: performance exists only as governed enjoyment, and it is this governance that must be made controllable by law.

Thus, product and service are not obstacles to the regime of the IOC; they are instruments for adjusting specific effects in a consumption whose utility depends on the ecosystem that maintains, alters and administers it. The classificatory debate matters only insofar as it helps to stabilize legitimate expectations, control relevant unilateral reconfigurations and structure remedies compatible with the continuity of performance. Everything else is conceptual filigree with little power to solve the real problem. And the real problem, here, is the private administration of enjoyment.

Notes

  1. The distinction between product and service, in the CDC, has real dogmatic utility, but it functions as a tool for organizing the protective regime, not as an ontological prison intended to narrow the Code's incidence. Article 3, paragraph 1, anchors the product on the plane of goods, encompassing a movable or immovable, material or immaterial object, while paragraph 2 brings the service back to the plane of activity supplied in the market for remuneration Filomeno(Filomeno, 2011, p. 51-53). In continuous and complex relationships, good and activity frequently appear functionally intertwined, without this authorizing the dissolution of consumer-law protection into classificatory preciosity. The legally relevant point remains the economically supplied utility and the way it is delivered, maintained or degraded in the course of the relationship. ↩
  2. “Art. 26. The right to complain about apparent or easily ascertainable quality defects lapses in: I - thirty days, in the case of supply of services and non-durable products; II - ninety days, in the case of supply of services and durable products. Paragraph 1. The lapse period begins to run from the effective delivery of the product or the end of the performance of the services. Paragraph 2. The following prevent the lapse: I - the complaint demonstrably made by the consumer to the supplier of products and services until the corresponding negative response, which must be transmitted unequivocally; [...] III - the institution of a civil inquiry, until its conclusion. Paragraph 3. In the case of a hidden quality defect, the lapse period begins at the moment the defect becomes evident.” Brasil(Brasil, [2022]). ↩
  3. The discipline of Article 26 of the CDC must be read in a key compatible with performances whose inadequacy is not revealed all at once. For apparent quality defects, the lapse period runs from the effective delivery of the product or the end of the performance of the service; for hidden quality defects, however, the starting point shifts to the moment the defect becomes evident Denari(Denari, 2011, p. 244). In continued relationships, the period cannot be used to turn the initial formal delivery into an artificial point for the early emptying of consumer-law protection. The count must engage with the real moment at which the inadequacy becomes cognizable, above all when it only emerges in prolonged use or in the supervening deterioration of serviceability Denari(Denari, 2011, p. 243-245). ↩
  4. Product, service, quality defect and inadequacy make up, in the CDC, a single systematic logic of control of the quality of performance, which makes it possible to frame lasting supply relationships without depending only on more sophisticated constructions about long-term captive contracts. The theory of quality explains that quality defects branch into two categories, the second being precisely that of “quality defects by inadequacy”, linked to the performance of products and services and to the fulfillment of their purpose in conformity with the legitimate expectation of the consumer Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 252]). This structure projects itself directly onto Articles 18, 20 and 26 of the CDC. While liability for product-caused accidents deals primarily with safety, liability for quality defects has as its main focus the “real adequacy to the proper purposes” of the product or service, that is, its concrete aptitude to achieve the legitimately expected utility Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 318]). The Manual itself summarizes the idea by observing that a product or service will present a quality defect of adequacy whenever it does not correspond to the legitimate expectation of the consumer as to its use or enjoyment, or when the nonconformity compromises its serviceability Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 121]). In this sense, the discipline of quality defects in the CDC is organized less by the abstract ontology of the object and more by the material idea of adequacy of performance. A product or service will be legally defective when it does not correspond to the legitimate expectation of the consumer as to its use or enjoyment, that is, when the nonconformity compromises its serviceability or usability Denari(Denari, 2011, p. 191). There is a quality defect when the performance proves inadequate for the purposes reasonably expected of it or when it does not meet the regulatory standards of serviceability Denari(Denari, 2011, p. 230). In continuous supplies, this means that the nominal survival of the bond is not enough; the protection of the CDC requires effective maintenance of the functional aptitude that legitimately became part of the relationship. ↩
  5. On the technical plane, the consumer-law protection of continued bonds is organized around the notion of quality defect as a failure of the duty of adequacy, and not only of defect in the sense of a risk to safety. A quality defect arises when a product or service does not serve the purpose legitimately expected of it, owing to the impairment of its quality or quantity Miragem(Miragem, 2020, [p. 487]). In continuous relationships of enjoyment, this shifts the focus from the extraordinary collapse of performance to the ordinary frustration of its utility. The quality defect of the product or service stems from the absence of properties or characteristics necessary to meet the purposes legitimately expected by the consumer, and amounts to the frustration of the presumable and reasonably expected utility of the product or service Miragem(Miragem, 2020, [p. 523]). In continuous supplies of IOCs, inadequacy must be measured by the maintenance, over time, of the utility legitimately expected by the consumer. ↩
  6. The category of long-term captive contracts offers an especially fertile dogmatic basis for understanding the continuous supply of IOCs as a complex, lasting and structurally asymmetric legal relationship. The bond is not exhausted in an instantaneous exchange, nor is it reduced to a snapshot of the initial moment of adhesion. It is a continued relationship, sustained by mass contracting methods, by a business organization opaque to the consumer and by a logic of permanence that turns enjoyment into practical dependence. This is precisely the movement captured in the description of "new contracts or contractual relationships" that, through adhesion contracts and general conditions, supply special services and create "complex long-term legal relationships", marked by the "position of 'captivity' or 'dependence' of the clients, consumers" Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 77-78). Keeping the bond alive may lead the consumer to readily accept any new imposition by the supplier, guaranteeing the latter a constantly renewed "power of imposition" throughout the long-term obligation Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 109-110). It is this grammar of captivity that justifies the intensification, in such relationships, of the duties of information, cooperation, loyalty and protection of trust. ↩
  7. Understanding lasting bonds requires abandoning the static reading of the contract as an event concentrated in a single moment of formation. The obligation must be read as a dynamic bond developed in successive phases, capable of radiating duties of protection and cooperation beyond the performance of the main obligation Martins-Costa(Martins-Costa, 2018, p. 232-233). The parties bind themselves around a true “program of performance”, to which they are bound by virtue of self-binding and whose implementation requires intersubjective collaboration Martins-Costa(Martins-Costa, 2018, p. 236). Objective good faith requires respect for the concrete way in which enjoyment was organized, maintained and legitimately entrusted by the consumer over time. Unilateral changes or excessive instability affect the very contractual program as actually lived and the trust that has settled within it. ↩
  8. In continued contractual relationships, performance is not exhausted at the initial delivery, because the bond also comes to be structured by expectations of stability, predictability and loyalty formed in the course of the relationship. When enjoyment is prolonged, the contractual program also comprises the concrete pattern of performance settled between the parties. The duties to act in good faith must be assessed in connection with the investment of trust legitimized by the predictability of conduct, so that the duties of loyalty lie at the point where the legitimate expectations of those who trust cannot be disloyally undermined Martins-Costa(Martins-Costa, 2018, p. 309). In the continuous supply of IOCs, repeated practice and trust settled over time become part of the legally relevant bond. ↩
  9. “Art. 422. The contracting parties are obliged to observe, both in the conclusion of the contract and in its performance, the principles of probity and good faith.” Brasil(Brasil, [2024]a). ↩
  10. Lasting contractual relationships attract, with special intensity, the incidence of ancillary duties of conduct, because the bond ceases to be understood as a one-off act of exchange and comes to be read as a continued process of legally relevant interaction. Within this framework, the obligation is no longer limited to the main performance, but projects itself over time as a complex of behaviors, expectations and reciprocal responsibilities. It is "a new view of the obligation, as a complex of acts, conducts, duties extending over time", objective good faith being a source of duties "to inform, to cooperate, duty of care, of secrecy, of advice, of loyalty etc." Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 110-111). The long-term contract "carries within it expectations other than those of contracts of immediate performance", since it is based "more on trust, on repeated coexistence, on the maintenance of economic potential and of the quality of services", which is why "the capacity for adaptation, for cooperation between contracting parties, for continuation of the contractual relationship is basic" Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 1222). This supports reading the continuous supply of IOCs as a performance whose protected core is not exhausted in the initial consent. ↩
  11. “Art. 421. Contractual freedom shall be exercised within the limits of the social function of the contract.” Brasil(Brasil, [2024]a). ↩
  12. In continued bonds, the control of supervening changes cannot be thought of only in formal terms of renewed consent or of the abstract possibility of renegotiation. The duration of the relationship intensifies dependence, trust and the practical centrality of the performance, requiring that the stability of the contractual program function as a parameter for containing the supplier's modifying power. Clauses imposed in relationships aimed precisely at future security and at the "maintenance of the contractually agreed promises and conditions" may represent "an excessive [...] discretionary power, placing the consumer at an excessive disadvantage contrary to good faith", allowing a factually "unilateral" variation of the content of the contract, of the performances and of the price Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 1225). In relational and captive contracts, revisions and readaptations cannot "authorize abuses of the dominant contractual position" nor frustrate legitimate expectations of the parties, and must operate according to cooperation, loyalty and good faith Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 101). ↩
  13. The prohibition on unilateral downgrading of the utility delivered to the consumer finds direct dogmatic formulation in Article 51, item XIII, of the CDC. A clause that purports to authorize the supplier to alter, by its exclusive will, the content or quality of the performance after contracting is null, since this breaks the balance of the relationship and turns the consumer into a compulsory recipient of a utility inferior to that which he legitimately acquired. Commenting on the provision, Nunes notes the nullity of the clause that authorizes “the supplier to unilaterally modify the content or quality of the contract, after its conclusion”, illustrating the abuse with a case in which the supplier reduces, without reducing the price, the intensity of the agreed performance Nunes(Nunes, 2018, [p. 508-509]). The quality defect constitutes “a minus of the consumer's right” and admitting a quantitatively or qualitatively inferior performance would amount to tolerating “the unlawful enrichment and unjust enrichment of the supplier” Nunes(Nunes, 2018, [p. 184]). ↩
  14. “Art. 18. Suppliers of durable or non-durable consumer products are jointly and severally liable for quality or quantity defects that render them unfit or inadequate for the consumption for which they are intended or that diminish their value […].” Brasil(Brasil, [2022]). ↩
  15. “Art. 30. All sufficiently precise information or advertising, conveyed by any form or means of communication with respect to products and services offered or presented, binds the supplier that conveys it or makes use of it and becomes part of the contract that may be concluded.” Brasil(Brasil, [2022]). ↩
  16. Offer, information and contractual performance remain legally connected throughout the consumer relationship, so that the promised or presented content continues to function as a parameter of adequacy of the performance during the execution of the bond. The Manual points out that the requirement of clear and adequate information is not limited to the pre-contractual moment, but also governs the conclusion of the contract, the contract itself and the post-contractual moment; for this reason, information “affects the essence of the deal”, becoming part of the contractual content and, if it fails, possibly constituting a quality defect in the product or service offered Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 126]). The binding effect is reinforced when the work explains that it acts in two ways: first, obliging the supplier even if it refuses to contract; second, entering into and prevailing in any contract that may be concluded, even when the contractual text itself tries to remove this binding character Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 404]). In addition, Article 48 of the CDC is read in the Manual as a command according to which declarations of will contained in private writings, receipts and pre-contracts bind the supplier, and may even give rise to specific performance, so that “everything that is said and offered” in the name of the supplier already produces legal effects and constitutes a contractual clause Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 597]). ↩
  17. In the CDC regime, the offer does not operate as a simple preparatory phase or neutral invitation to contract, but as a legally binding element of the consumer relationship itself. The consumer-law discipline breaks with the narrow privatist logic and recognizes that the offering message already produces an obligation for the supplier, which is bound to the content externalized to the market. “Every offer relating to products and services binds the offering supplier, obliging it to fulfill what it offers” Nunes(Nunes, 2018, [p. 310]). This binding effect also covers the presentation of the product or service, including visual display as a legally relevant fact: “the mere presentation of the product in the shop window, on the shelf, on the counter etc.” also constitutes an offer, the conveyed message being “the evidentiary element of its existence and binding effect” Nunes(Nunes, 2018, [p. 312]). This is especially useful for consumption environments mediated by interface, in which the visual externalization of utility is part of the offered content itself. ↩
  18. Quality defect, breach of the offer and alteration of the content of the performance may trigger, in the CDC, remedies of material restoration, and not only formal or pecuniary solutions. Declarations of will contained in private writings, receipts and pre-contracts bind the supplier and their breach may give rise to specific performance under Article 84, precisely to achieve the “practical result equivalent to that of performance” of the obligation Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 597]). Faced with a nonconforming offer, the consumer may demand forced performance of the obligation, accept another equivalent consumer good or rescind the contract with restitution of what he paid and losses and damages Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 408]). In case of a quality defect, the CDC allows the consumer to demand another product of the same kind in perfect conditions of use, alongside restitution or proportional price reduction Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 338]). The consumer-law system, therefore, is not content with the nominal survival of the performance when the promised utility was downgraded or diverted. In lasting relationships, the relevant inadequacy of the performance triggers consumer-law remedies of their own, without submission to the narrower filters of classical civil law. Article 6 of the CDC advances in relation to the Civil Code's regime of excessive onerousness precisely by not requiring unpredictability or irresistibility of the supervening fact, the breakdown of the objective basis of the deal, of the intrinsic balance between performances or the disappearance of the essential purpose of the contract being sufficient Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 133]). The consequence is decisive for continued contracts: the relevant frustration of the utility legitimately incorporated into the bond is already sufficient to open room for revisional or resolutory protection of the consumer. This logic appears even more concretely in Article 20 of the CDC. Faced with quality defects that diminish the value of the service, the consumer may demand contractual rescission without penalty or proportional price reduction, with no need to discuss the supplier's fault, since it is strict liability Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 354]). In contracts of duration, it is well known that one may request either the modification of the clause with maintenance of the bond or the rescission of the contract, with a declaration of nullity of the abusive clause or its modification if excessively onerous Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 136-137]). ↩
  19. In contracts of continued performance, periodic performances are not exhausted in their simple one-off realization, because they project into the future the satisfaction of the legitimate interests of the consumer. In these relationships, the structure of the bond becomes that of successive periodic performances and counter-performances, also encompassing the legitimate expectation of their maintenance over time Miragem(Miragem, 2020, [p. 393]). The need to preserve the legitimate expectations of continuity of the contract may imply constant readaptation of the contractual relationship, precisely to avoid its frustration Miragem(Miragem, 2020, [p. 393]). In continuous supplies of IOCs, performance is not exhausted at the first availability of access or functionality; what is also protected is the material continuity of the enjoyment legitimately expected in the course of the bond. Where performance projects itself into the future, quality defects, periods and remedies cannot be read as if everything had ended at the first click. ↩
  20. In a dogmatic key, the elementary distinction between product and service must be stabilized without losing sight of the protective functionality of the CDC. The service is identified, at its core, with the idea of activity, that is, with purposively oriented human action, whose realization is consumed in its very exercise. While the product falls under the category of good, the service falls under the category of doing: “service is, typically, activity”, this being “human action that has a purpose in view”, so that “every action is exhausted as soon as it is performed” Nunes(Nunes, 2018, [p. 102]). This differentiation, however, does not prevent the recognition of mixed situations, in which the activity performed makes use of goods that accompany or enable it. There are services “rendered with products that make up the service itself” and that, even so, “continue to be characterized as a service” Nunes(Nunes, 2018, [p. 113]). ↩
