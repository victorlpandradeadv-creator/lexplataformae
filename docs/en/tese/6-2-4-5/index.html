6.2.4.5 On misleading advertising by semiotic architecture

See this section in the PDF

p. 623

Advertising, in IOC ecosystems, cannot be treated as a peripheral layer of the offer. It participates directly in the production of the economic meaning of the object, organizes the p. 624 consumer's expectation before enjoyment and anticipates, to a large extent, the way in which utility, value, rarity, performance and equivalence will be perceived. It is precisely for this reason that consumer-law protection needs to treat it as a controllable semiotic mechanism. The problem lies not only in literally false sentences or in gross omissions. It lies in the strategic management of the visible and the invisible, of what gains emphasis and what is relegated to the documentary background, of what appears as an immediate advantage and what recedes as a cost, limitation or ordinary risk of the supply.

In platform markets, this question gains special density because the promised experience is not static. The advertised object will later be subjected to updating, curation, parameterization, enforcement and ordinary reconfigurations of the environment. This means that the expectation is not only formed before contracting; it can also be strategically overproduced before the consumer has real conditions to perceive mutability, limits of use, equivalence criteria and ordinary possibilities of functional downgrading. In IOCs, advertising does not announce only an object. It announces a future governed experience. And, for that very reason, it can produce error not only through express falsity, but through the semiotic construction of a trust incompatible with the reality of the regime of enjoyment.

The first point is, then, to recognize advertising as market semiotics. It does not operate only by transferring objective data, but through signs, framings, visual hierarchies and inductions of reading. The relevant vulnerability here is not a lack of technical information in the narrow sense, nor a diffuse psychic weakness of the consumer. What matters is the asymmetry of meaning produced by communication designed to guide adhesion under conditions of selective visibility. The marketing message defines what appears as an advantage, what is naturalized as tolerable, what is anticipated as rarity and what is concealed as an ordinary consequence of the contracting. We are not facing promotional ornament. We are facing a structure for the production of expectation.

This conclusion is perfectly compatible with the dogmatics of the CDC. When advertising contains sufficiently precise elements about qualities, conditions, advantages or characteristics of the object, it becomes part of the offer and radiates obligational effects over the consumer relationship713. The consumer-law system breaks, therefore, with the old privatist view according to p. 625 which advertising would be a simple invitation indifferent to the contracting. In more direct language: the promotional message enters the contract through the door of trust. If the supplier organizes the market through promises, signals and sufficiently guiding descriptions, it cannot later pretend that all of that was only aesthetic ambiance and that the real bond was hidden, dry and hostile, in later clauses. Advertising participates in the very shaping of the legally enforceable performance.

Here, therefore, objective good faith does not appear as a sympathetic abstraction, but as a concrete matrix of ancillary duties in marketing communication. Fair advertising is not merely advertising “without lies”. It is advertising structured in a way compatible with legitimate trust, identification of its persuasive character and consistency between what produces expectation and what will be effectively delivered. Good faith requires that the supplier promise only what it can fulfill and, if it provides information about characteristics of the product, the service or the conditions of contracting, that this information be correct, truthful and apt to guide the formation of will714. This matters a great deal in IOCs because the formation of expectation usually happens before the consumer has any real contact with the ordinary regime of mutability of the object. The promise circulates first. The frustration comes later.

From this follows the third point: advertising deceptiveness does not depend on a literal lie. The CDC does not require frontal, caricatural or gross falsity to recognize deception. Unlawfulness can arise when the message, even if it contains literally true elements, is apt to lead the consumer into error through ambiguity, relevant omission, strategic incompleteness or p. 626 simple persuasive organization of the visible715. The error need not be fabricated by the openly false sentence. It can be produced by the way the essential is hierarchized, by selective emphasis on advantages and by the displacement of relevant conditions to disclaimers, layers of low visibility, later links or discursive zones that do not usefully integrate the moment of decision.

This is the core of misleading advertising by semiotic architecture. The architecture of the advertisement becomes legally relevant when the supplier structures the consumer's understanding so as to reinforce the positive side of the experience and push the onerous, mutable or limited side out of focus. In simpler terms, communication does not need to lie in order to deceive. It suffices that it manage the interpretive framing in a sufficiently asymmetrical way. Advertising can tell the truth in pieces and still lie as a whole. It can keep the real cost out of the visual center. It can suggest a functional equivalence that the ordinary regime of the IOC later downgrades without verifiable correspondence. It can speak of rarity, urgency, exclusivity or status without adequately integrating, on the same plane of emphasis, the precariousness, reversibility or mutability of what is being acquired.

This mode of operating intensifies when the platform organizes pre-deliveries of expectation. Teasers, shop windows, trailers, event campaigns, passes, rarity panels, countdown timers, suggested equivalences, narratives of distinction and communication through influencers are not merely advance publicity. They are mechanisms of curation of expectation prior to effective enjoyment. In IOCs, this is of enormous relevance because the governance of the decision begins before the purchase and before acceptance. The platform constructs in advance what the consumer expects to find, value, fear losing and tolerate as admissible frustration. The marketing environment, thus, already functions as an architecture of the future tolerable disappointment. If the expectation is inflated by a semiotics of value and later absorbed p. 627 by an ordinary regime of downgrading, elastic equivalence or unilateral mutability, advertising was not merely a pre-contractual stage. It was a mechanism for capturing trust.

In this context, the case of hybrid formats is especially important. In digital ecosystems, advertising, content, entertainment, recommendation and sale are frequently confused. Influencers, creators, “community” posts, demonstrative experiences, in-game shop windows and events mediated by the platform's own environment make less sharp the distinction between descriptive communication, cultural recommendation and marketing persuasion. This hybridization weakens the intelligibility of the advertising character of the message and compromises communicational fairness. The consumer need not be facing a banner explicitly labeled as an advertisement to be persuasively guided toward a contracting. When identification of the marketing character is weakened, the semiotic asymmetry deepens, because advertising ceases to be perceived as advertising and comes to operate disguised as experience, recommendation or simple ecosystem content.

It is precisely for this reason that Article 38 of the CDC proves so important here. The burden of proving the truthfulness and correctness of the advertising information or communication lies with whoever sponsors it716. This is not an elegant evidentiary detail. It is a normative recognition that advertising is a space of power and, therefore, of responsibility. In IOCs, this provision prevents the consumer from having to prove alone that the expectation produced by the communication was incompatible with the effective regime of enjoyment. If the platform announces a certain equivalence, a certain benefit, a certain utility, a certain standard of stability or a certain implicit value of rarity, it is for the platform to demonstrate documentarily the correctness of this narrative in light of what is ordinarily delivered and administered in the ecosystem. The system does not presume the innocence of the marketing promise. It requires that it be sustained by whoever produced it.

This logic is very strongly articulated with the protection of trust. Legally protected trust is born not only from the final contractual instrument, but also from the communicational behavior of the supplier in the market717. The more precise, eloquent p. 628 and guiding the advertising, the greater its aptitude to generate legitimate expectation and, therefore, the greater also its binding force. This is decisive because it prevents the old maneuver of capturing trust in the pre-contractual phase and then trying to dehydrate it in the final text of the contract or in mutable internal rules. Advertising does not stay outside, since it participates in the legally relevant core of the consumer relationship. If it organizes the expectation, it also organizes the parameter by which the frustration will be measured.

The discipline of children's advertising functions, here, as a normative limit case that internally reinforces this reading without diverting the axis of the discussion. The point is not to equate adults with children, nor to open a new thematic front. The point is to show that the CDC itself already recognizes that abusiveness can result from taking advantage of a structural deficiency of judgment exploited by persuasive communication. When the system considers abusive the advertising that takes advantage of the child's deficiency of judgment and experience, it recognizes, in an explicit key, that the manipulation of meaning and the exploitation of limitations of decoding are legally relevant. The most important normative datum is not childhood in itself, but the underlying logic. When communication takes advantage of a structural asymmetry of understanding, the control of abusiveness intensifies. This reinforces, from within the system itself, the legitimacy of treating semiotic vulnerability as a legal fact and not as a sociological metaphor.

The argumentative utility of this limit case is quite elegant. It shows that the CDC already operates with gradations of vulnerability linked to real conditions of understanding and resistance, and not only to the episodic lack of data. Advertising can be abusive even without lying, simply because it exploits a structural limitation of judgment. Transposing this reasoning in a controlled way to IOCs, without infantilizing the adult consumer, it is maintained that there are also situations in which architecture, curation and private governance of meaning produce real asymmetries of decoding, comparison and proof. When these asymmetries are systematically exploited by advertising and by the communicational environment of the platform, space opens for reinforced control of deceptiveness and abusiveness.

The immediate dogmatic consequence is that advertising in IOCs must be treated as a controllable practice when the essential is kept out of focus and the negative is displaced to p. 629 disclaimers or useless cross-references; when marketing communication produces, through anticipatory curation of expectation, an image of value, rarity, performance or functional equivalence that the ordinary regime of mutability later frustrates without verifiable correspondence; and when the marketing character of the message is weakened by hybrid formats that confuse content, recommendation, entertainment and sale. In these cases, deceptiveness is not reduced to a literal lie. It appears as a defect of the very architecture of the promise.

It is necessary, then, to connect semiotic vulnerability, good faith, trust, deceptiveness and abusiveness without psychologizing consumption or resorting to moralism against advertising in itself. The problem is not desiring, fantasizing or attributing symbolic value to the object. The problem is when the marketing production of expectation is organized so as to guide the consumer's understanding toward an image of the object that the supply regime itself does not sustain, or sustains only under hidden reservations, opaque equivalences and silent mutability. Advertising thus ceases to be merely an announcement and becomes an objective element in the production of a legally controllable trust.

With this, the transition is then prepared to the broader decision environment in which advertising, information, interface and choice architecture operate in an integrated way. Advertising will not be the only gear of semiotic vulnerability, but it is one of its most powerful cores. It pre-shapes expectation, organizes the imaginary of value, distributes visibility and conditions what the consumer understands as risk, opportunity and loss. In IOCs, where enjoyment is governed and mutable, this makes it one of the main points of control of the CDC. The supplier answers not only for what it promises in isolated sentences. It answers for the regime of meaning it produces when it advertises.

Notes

  1. Under the regime of the Consumer Protection Code, advertising does not occupy a merely peripheral or ornamental position. When the advertising message contains sufficiently precise elements about the object, its qualities, conditions or advantages, it becomes part of the offer and comes to radiate obligational effects over the consumer relationship, binding the supplier to the content conveyed Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, p. 140-142). The consequence is direct: advertising does not remain outside the contract as a simple prior persuasive stage, but participates in the shaping of the legally enforceable content of the performance. This logic is reinforced by the overcoming of the old privatist understanding that treated advertising as a simple harmless invitation to contract. In the consumer-law system, the marketing message ceases to be legally indifferent and comes to function as a source of obligation whenever, by its degree of precision and aptitude to form expectation, it makes up the content of the offer addressed to the consumer Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, p. 144). If advertising is part of the offer and produces obligational binding, then the way in which it organizes the consumer's understanding, hierarchizes elements, distributes visibility and produces expectation also becomes legally relevant. The advertising message does not stay outside the relationship doing marketing; it participates in the very obligational structure of the supply. ↩
  2. Marketing communication, under the CDC regime, is subject to material control of fairness, truthfulness and adequacy because it is capable of shaping the consumer's legitimate expectation even before the bond is formalized. Marques states that, in matters of advertising, objective good faith requires it to be “a fair activity”, reflected also from the position of whoever receives the message, such that it promises only what it can fulfill and, if it brings information about characteristics of the product, the service or the contractual conditions, that these be correct and truthful Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 832; p. 886). The author adds that the main characteristic of misleading advertising, according to the CDC, is being liable to lead the consumer into error, including by “omissions”, the rule having to be interpreted broadly, since the error consists in a “false notion of reality” potentially formed in the consumer's mind by the advertising action Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 909). Applied to the problem of semiotic architecture, this guideline authorizes control of promotional messages that, without lying frontally, organize the interpretive field of the offer so as to produce an illusory understanding of utility, extent, stability or conditions of enjoyment. ↩
  3. Advertising deceptiveness, in the CDC, does not depend on literal falsity or on a frontally demonstrable lie. Unlawfulness can arise whenever the message, even if partially true, is apt to lead the consumer into error, including through omission, ambiguity, relevant incompleteness or the very form of presentation of the content Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, p. 167). The decisive point is not the microscopic truthfulness of each isolated sentence, but the deceptive potential of the meaningful whole of the communication as effectively perceived by its recipient. This perception becomes especially important when the essential datum is displaced to zones of low visibility, diluted in accessory information or weakened by graphic and drafting strategies that reduce its practical apprehension. Deceptiveness can emerge, for example, when the real price does not appear clearly, when charges are relegated to a visually irrelevant position or when the decisive information is technically included, but functionally hidden in the context of the piece Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, p. 168). Misleading advertising by semiotic architecture does not require gross falsity. It can operate through the strategic management of the visible and the invisible, through the functional ambiguity of language and through the persuasive organization of the advertisement so as to guide the consumer's understanding away from what really matters. ↩
  4. “Art. 38. The burden of proving the truthfulness and correctness of the advertising information or communication lies with whoever sponsors it.” Brasil(Brasil, [2022]).. ↩
  5. Advertising, in consumer law, participates directly in the formation of contractual trust. This occurs because, in markets marked by lean, opaque or gap-ridden adhesion contracts, the marketing message frequently anticipates, completes and densifies the understanding that the consumer forms about the utility, the qualities and the mode of enjoyment of the object offered, becoming a relevant element for the construction of their legitimate expectation Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, p. 142). Legally protected trust is not born, therefore, only from the final contractual instrument, but also from the communicational behavior of the supplier in the market. This binding can be understood in light of the protection of the expectation provoked by the advertiser's own conduct. The more eloquent, precise and guiding the message, the greater its aptitude to generate trust worthy of protection and, therefore, the greater also its binding force Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, p. 42). Advertising not only describes the object, but pre-shapes the expectation of enjoyment that will later serve as the parameter for assessing performance, inadequacy and deceptiveness. When marketing communication structures the consumer's expectation, it enters the legally relevant core of the consumer relationship and can be a source both of obligation and of legally censurable frustration. ↩
