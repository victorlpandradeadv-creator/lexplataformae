7.2.4 On competition under the Lex Plataformae

See this section in the PDF

This section in the layers of the site

  • Platforms · Extension of the typology

p. 769

The competition-law dimension of the typology of the Immaterial Object of Consumption does not stem from an external thematic graft, but from the very logic of the governed supply regime. When the platform administers visibility, access, comparability and intelligibility within the ecosystem, it organizes more than the end user's enjoyment. It also organizes, in a materially relevant way, the conditions under which economic agents compete for attention, preference, trust and conversion within the environment. Competition, in this context, does not appear as a problem separate from consumption, but as an institutional continuation of the same governance of enjoyment described in the preceding items. The platform that governs the user's experience governs, at the same time, the perceptible field in which competition becomes possible, legible and economically effective.

This parallel finds solid grounding in the reading of Daniel Firmato816 de Almeida Glória, for whom competition protection and consumer protection are not autonomous or rival ends, but instruments aimed at shaping a transparent, healthy, harmonious and fair market, in which a plurality of suppliers, freedom of choice, respect for the consumer and the quality of products and services are kept in balanced tension with economic freedom Glória(Glória, 2003, p. 131).

This perspective makes it clear that competition matters here not as a discipline foreign to the IOC, but because the preservation of real choice, meaningful plurality and market legibility is part of the very protection of enjoyment under the CDC. If competition, read in this way, is not an end in itself, but an instrument for protecting an uncaptured environment of choice, then the architectural governance of the platform, when it distorts the field of choice, already acquires competitive relevance from within the consumer experience itself.

This conclusion is reinforced by the centrality that Glória attributes to freedom of choice and to information as the common core between competition and consumer protection. The author states that the consumer's freedom of choice constitutes the primordial point of all p. 770 competition law and that such freedom brings with it information as a guiding principle, because whoever does not know is not truly free to choose Glória(Glória, 2003, p. 113).

In platform ecosystems, this formulation gains new density. The platform does not merely offer alternatives. It produces, through interface, curation, ranking, recommendation, highlighting, reputation and the semantics of the environment, the conditions under which these alternatives can be perceived as real, comparable or preferable. The competition problem, therefore, is no longer reduced to the formal presence of multiple agents in the market. It comes to involve the way in which the perceptible market itself is administered by whoever controls the infrastructure of appearance.

It is precisely for this reason that the competition reading must reject the so-called myth of competition, that is, the idea that the mere apparent multiplicity of options would be enough to guarantee factual freedom of choice. Glória observes that the existence of options does not, by itself, mean real freedom for the consumer, especially when products, services and contractual clauses present themselves with a homogeneity capable of obscuring genuinely distinct alternatives Glória(Glória, 2003, p. 115-116). On platforms, this warning becomes even more serious.

Homogenization does not occur only on the plane of the product or the contract. It may be produced by the governance of the interface itself, when the environment artificially brings certain offers closer, renders others invisible, shifts what is sponsored into the zone of the apparently organic, manipulates comparability and administers the user's perception of what is worth more, appears more or seems more reliable. The illusion of plurality may thus coexist with severe compression of the real conditions of choice.

The consequence is that the governance of enjoyment also comes to have a competitive function. When the platform ranks, recommends, highlights, integrates logistically, certifies reputations, sets parameters of appearance and gathers intelligence about the behavior of users and suppliers, it ceases to be merely the environment in which competition takes place. It becomes an agent in its material shaping. In markets of this kind, internal preferencing, self-preferencing, informational barriers, capture of competitive intelligence and opaque discrimination do not necessarily depend on overt acts of exclusion. They may operate through structural micro-advantages, produced by the continuous administration of the visibility, comparability and intelligibility of the ecosystem.

The platform does not need to formally expel a competitor to restrict its competitiveness. It is enough to govern the perceptible field asymmetrically, making certain p. 771 offers more legible, more reliable, faster, more highlighted or more “natural” in the user's eyes.

At this point, Glória's contribution allows a particularly felicitous link between consumer and competition. The author maintains that, in a market deformed by oligopolies, cartels and dominant positions, the consumer's vulnerability intensifies because their freedom is compromised and, at times, they have no choice but to contract with the supplier that dominates the economic sector in question Glória(Glória, 2003, p. 106). On platforms, this logic reappears in reconfigured form. The dominant position does not always manifest itself as a classic monopoly over a physical good or as an explicit cartel among traditional companies. It may manifest itself as dominance over the private infrastructure that organizes the encounter between user and market. The consumer still sees “several options”, but the practical conditions under which these options appear, are compared and are selected have already been shaped by the agent that governs the environment. Freedom of choice does not formally disappear. It is administered.

This shift is central to the Lex Plataformae. The platform does not control only a distribution channel. It controls the practical semantics of competition within the environment. It defines, or at least strongly conditions, which competitors will be visible, on what terms they will be compared, under what reputation signals they will appear, what kind of friction they will suffer, what strategic data will be extracted from their activity and how the user will perceive the very space of choice. To that extent, competition ceases to be merely a relation between suppliers and comes to depend on the private architecture of the ecosystem. The governance of enjoyment and the governance of competition overlap. The same mechanism that affects the consumer's experience also affects the concrete freedom to compete of the economic agents that depend on the platform to appear, sell, circulate or reach an audience.

The task here is not to reconstruct the entire theory of infringements of the economic order. The task is to establish reading parameters for situations in which the platform, by governing enjoyment, also comes to govern the perceptible conditions of competition. The question becomes less the exhaustive definition of the competition infringement and more the requirement of institutional controllability over mechanisms of visibility, comparison, ranking, prioritization and strategic use of ecosystem data. Read in this way, the IOC regime does not replace competition law. It provides an entry grammar for understanding when the architecture of the consumer's experience has already become, simultaneously, the architecture of competitive advantage.

p. 772 It is at this point that the parameters presented earlier acquire a specific competitive function. Open auditability applies as a requirement of minimum intelligibility over relevant criteria of ranking, highlighting, recommendation, preferencing, economic moderation of the environment and any use of strategic data obtained from the intermediation itself. Without this, the platform governs competition from self-legitimated obscurity, turning opacity into structural advantage. Functional value equivalence, in turn, makes it possible to control rule changes that, without formally excluding certain agents, significantly downgrade their practical position in the ecosystem, emptying the utility of remaining in competition under the pretext of updating, optimization or a change of internal policy. Semiotic vulnerability helps explain why competitive harm also runs through the private administration of the user's perception. And abusive teleology comes into play when the design of the environment no longer merely organizes circulation and comes to programmatically exploit the gatekeeper position to extract competitive advantage from the very power to govern the perceptible market.

From this, the link between competition protection and consumer protection no longer seems a rhetorical rapprochement. One protection came to be seen as a corollary of the other, but both go hand in hand as legal guideposts of the market, precisely to correct deformations produced by economic power and by the corrosion of free competition Glória(Glória, 2003, p. 2; p. 129). This fits the reality of platforms precisely. When the platform controls the environment of circulation and competition, consumer protection cannot be limited to the final object acquired or to the final content displayed. It must reach the way in which the market itself is privately and architecturally produced. Otherwise, consumer protection would remain blind to the mechanism that manufactures, behind the appearance of plurality, the material conditions of reduced choice and compressed competition.

The perspective also makes it possible to refine the institutional reach of consumer choice. Competition is only fully justified when it prevents price setting, the supply of goods and economic power from being at the mercy of structural abuses, preserving a market in which the consumer is genuinely the central character, and not a mere residual recipient of the effects of economic domination Glória(Glória, 2003, p. 78; p. 118). In platform ecosystems, this centrality requires understanding that the consumer no longer stands only before competing economic agents. They stand before a private organizer of the competitive arena itself. It is this structural mediation that diminishes the usefulness of purely formal traditional categories. The consumer suffers not only when a dominant agent overtly excludes rivals. They also suffer when the architecture of the environment p. 773 silently reduces comparability, obscures alternatives, normalizes induced preferences and turns choice into a path selected in advance.

The possible institutional consequence is not to assert that every intermediation problem is automatically resolved by the CDC, nor that all interface governance constitutes, by itself, a competition infringement. Whenever the platform, in organizing enjoyment, also comes to organize, in an opaque and asymmetric way, the perceptible conditions of competition within the ecosystem, there must be a requirement of controllability commensurate with the dual dimension of the problem. This means, at the very least, minimum auditability over relevant ranking criteria, intelligibility about highlighting and preferencing practices, control of changes that distort the functional equivalence of remaining in competition, and a non-naive reading of the use of data obtained through the very position of intermediation. To this extent, the IOC regime does not absorb antitrust, but prevents architecturally governed competition from remaining invisible to consumer law doctrine.

The result is a thesis modest in its formal competition-law ambition, but strong in material reach. The platform that governs visibility, access and intelligibility also governs the arena in which consumers choose and competitors compete. In this arena, freedom of choice may be preserved, compressed or theatricalized. Plurality may be real or merely staged. Comparison may be enlightening or administered. Competition, then, can no longer be thought of only on the plane of the abstract structure of the market, but also on the plane of experience concretely mediated by architecture. It is at this point that the Lex Plataformae reveals its most sensitive dimension. By governing enjoyment, it comes to govern competition without needing to name it as such. The work of Law, here, is precisely to make this governance institutionally visible and controllable.
