7.2.3 On IOCs of productivity under the gig economy

See this section in the PDF

This section in the layers of the site

  • Platforms · Platform family

p. 765

The incidence of the regime of the Immaterial Object of Consumption on work platforms under the gig economy requires starting from a structural fact. In this family, the central utility is not reduced to the final payment per ride, delivery or completed task. What is supplied, in functional terms, is a private infrastructure of economic possibility. The platform organizes access to demand, distribution of calls, minimum predictability of remuneration, permanence in the productive flow, reputational criteria, prioritization standards, acceptance rules and sanction mechanisms. The object enjoyed, here, is not a static good nor a simple service externally provided to the worker. It is a governed productive environment, without which everyday economic activity does not become viable in the terms in which the platform itself promised and structured it.

This point is important because it prevents the analysis from being captured by excessively formal narratives about contractual autonomy. The instruments used by iFood, 99Food, Uber and 99 insist, with variations of language, on characterizing partners as independent, on presenting the platform as an environment of technological intermediation and on ruling out a hierarchical bond or mandatory exclusivity. iFood's terms for couriers speak of independent activity, of freedom as to when, how and where to make deliveries and of the company's role as a connection between customers, establishments and couriers IFOOD(iFood, [2023?]). 99's terms for partner drivers and motorcyclists follow the same line, presenting 99 as the intermediary of a marketplace, describing the partner as an autonomous and independent professional and providing for the platform's remuneration for the intermediation, as well as acceptance and completion metrics and temporary ride restrictions 99(99, [202-?]). Uber's General Terms of Use, in turn, also cover the making of deliveries by an independent partner at the location indicated in the app, while the Uber Community Guidelines make explicit that their rules apply to users, partner drivers and couriers, which reveals unified governance of permanence and behavior in the ecosystem UBER(Uber, 2026).

p. 766 None of this, however, suffices to resolve the dogmatic problem. The legally relevant question is not only whether the platform describes itself as an intermediary or whether the partner is formally free to switch the app on and off. The question is another. It is a matter of recognizing that the functional utility indispensable to the exercise of economic activity is offered, modulated and reconfigured by a private architecture that the subject does not control. The formal freedom to join or leave the environment coexists, here, with intense dependence on the private conditions of access to demand, calculation of earnings, formation of reputation, reading of performance and maintenance of eligibility in the productive flow. Enjoyment, therefore, is productive enjoyment of a governed environment. The platform worker consumes the very infrastructure that makes them economically operative.

It is precisely there that the subtype differs from a generic labor-law reading. The central point lies not only in asserting algorithmic subordination in the abstract, nor in automatically reclassifying the entire relationship under traditional categories of the employment contract. The focus, for the purposes of this typology, is on the supply regime. The platform supplies an ecosystem whose utility consists in making possible the exercise of economic activity under private conditions of visibility, distribution, prioritization and permanence. Income does not arise outside the environment and merely pass through it. It depends materially on the architecture of the environment. Whenever this architecture concentrates access criteria, unilaterally modulates the functional value of permanence and makes opaque the elements needed to understand losses, drops in demand, downgrades or restrictions, there will be a typical field of incidence of the IOC.

In this family, the central vulnerability takes a particularly acute form. It is semiotic vulnerability associated with productive dependence. The partner depends on the platform not only to obtain calls, rides or deliveries. They also depend on the internal signs by which the environment informs their position, projects reliability, organizes expectation of earnings, justifies variations in remuneration and communicates the risk of restriction or exclusion. Metrics, incentive messages, quality alerts, promotions, acceptance standards, pricing dynamics and performance notifications operate as a private language for governing conduct. This is not simple neutral operational information. It is a semiotic regime that organizes the subject's economic experience and redistributes onto them the costs of interpreting an environment whose material functioning they cannot audit autonomously.

This structure appears clearly in the materials examined. iFood states that it does not supervise the courier's activities “through the platform or by any other means”, but at the same time provides for ratings, analysis of complaints, minimum criteria related to the p. 767 obligations assumed before customers and establishments and cases of deactivation and inactivation on the platform IFOOD(iFood, [2023?]). 99, even more explicitly, structures the governance of the partner through an intermediation fee, objective criteria for use of the app, acceptance and completion metrics, the possibility of temporary ride restriction and account cancellation in cases defined at its sole discretion, including for registration issues and inappropriate conduct 99(99, [202-?]). The overall design is eloquent. The platform asserts independence at the contractual end and intensifies governance at the operational end.

That is why the dogmatic problem cannot be reduced to the simplistic dichotomy between autonomy and subordination. What is at stake is the legal control of a private productive infrastructure that materially conditions the continuity of the partner's economic life. Opacity about the distribution of opportunities, prioritization criteria, variable remuneration parameters, the effects of ratings and the causality of restrictions falls, in this context, not only on abstract contractual comfort, but on the very material conditions of life of subjects who depend on this environment to work. The governance of the app, here, does not regulate merely a user experience. It regulates access to income, minimum predictability of livelihood and the practical possibility of remaining economically active.

Hence the centrality of open auditability in this subtype. Relevant rule changes, changes in calculation, reconfigurations of priority, redefinitions of metrics or sanctions with material impact cannot be shielded by a generic discourse of efficiency, safety or algorithmic optimization. They must be made minimally verifiable. This does not mean fully opening the system, nor turning the Judiciary into the technical administrator of the platform. It means requiring sufficient institutional intelligibility about what relevantly affects the distribution of opportunities, the formation of earnings, the incidence of restrictions and the functional continuity of the partner's permanence in the ecosystem. Without this minimum, the platform simultaneously concentrates the power to organize economic activity and the power to make opaque the facts needed to contest it.

In environments of captive productive enjoyment, mutability is not limited to updating the interface or improving operation. It can substantially degrade the functional utility of the partner's permanence. Changes in call distribution criteria, pricing parameters, eligibility rules, incentive models or reputational weights may keep the name of the program, the appearance of the partnership and even the formal existence of the account, but relevantly empty the economic utility that justified joining and sustained the continuity of productive enjoyment. The legal test cannot be only whether the account remains p. 768 active. It must be whether the partner's functional position in the environment remains substantially equivalent to the one that structured their legitimate expectation of earnings and permanence.

The standard of evidence compatible with this type of conflict requires a more sophisticated reconstruction of the productive experience. It is not enough to show that there was a reduction in earnings or a one-off block. It is necessary to reconstruct the relation between governance mechanism and functional impact. This may involve call history, variations in remuneration, in-app communications, performance alerts, criteria disclosed or omitted, restriction records, rating patterns and comparative data on the change in the environment. There are, here, recurrent blind spots that need to be avoided. The first is to treat fluctuation in earnings as a pure natural risk of the activity, when it may stem from an opaque unilateral reconfiguration of the distribution infrastructure. The second is to treat the final sanction as an isolated event, without reconstructing the regime of conduct, incentive and threat that preceded it. The third is to accept the lack of transparency about metrics and criteria as an inevitable consequence of technology, when precisely this opacity is part of the central legal problem.

The possible dogmatic consequence, in this subtype, tends to gravitate around an abusive practice by structural opacity, qualified informational insufficiency, a defect of the productive supply regime and the unlawfulness of functionally degrading changes when not accompanied by compatible restoration. In borderline cases, especially when the combination of mutability, metrics, sanctions and restrictions reveals a stable program of normalizing losses and capturing conduct, there may be room for a reading in the key of abusive teleology. The most adequate institutional response will tend to favor preservation and disclosure of minimum records, duties of verifiable reasoning, review of restriction criteria, intelligibility about relevant parameters of distribution of opportunities and functional restoration of the regime whenever the platform has degraded, by design, the productive utility it offered as infrastructure.

This incidence does not dissolve the subtype into undifferentiated labor law, but neither does it allow itself to be neutralized by contractual self-descriptions of formal autonomy. The IOC regime makes it possible to capture precisely the zone in which the platform supplies a governed productive environment and thereby comes to organize access, permanence, visibility, intelligibility and contestation in economically relevant terms. It is at this point that iFood and 99Food, on the one hand, and Uber and 99, on the other, cease to be merely delivery or mobility apps and reveal themselves as private infrastructures of economic possibility. When this infrastructure is offered under opacity, unilateral mutability and concentrated sanctioning governance, the problem is no longer p. 769 merely contractual nor merely technological. It is a problem of institutional controllability of supply in a context of productive dependence.
