6.2.2 On the doctrine of digital assets and the regime of the Immaterial Object of Consumption

p. 565

Bruno Torquato Zampier Lacerda's doctrine of digital assets630 performs an important function because it resolves, with considerable depth, a problem that cannot be sidestepped by contractual rhetoric or by nostalgia for tangibility: techno-digital assets are legal goods and do not vanish from the world of Law merely because they are incorporeal. Digitally organized information can acquire a utility of its own, become part of the subject's sphere of interests and, for that reason, be treated as a legally relevant incorporeal good, with or without immediate economic content Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 51; p. 57; p. 72)631. This starting point is correct and indispensable. Without it, the debate on continuous digital consumption would be held hostage to the crude fiction that everything that cannot be held in the hand is legally light, dissolvable and disposable at the supplier's mere will.

Another real gain of this framework is the recognition that, once admitted as goods, these assets become susceptible to appropriation, circulation, enjoyment and protection. They enter the legal world not as conceptual curiosities, but as objects capable of sustaining organized legal positions, including use, enjoyment, disposal and transfer. Incorporeality neither prevents the exercise of the faculties typical of ownership nor turns enjoyment into a gracious tolerance granted by technology Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 73; p. 82; p. 151)632. This matters a great deal here p. 566 because it rules out, from the outset, the semantic fraud of the “revocable license” as a formula capable of making everything already economically incorporated into the user's sphere evaporate. If there is an identifiable virtual object, associated with the account and enjoyed within the ecosystem, there is legal substance to be taken seriously, even if the platform tries to cloak it in a vocabulary of precariousness.

Riot's own contractual clauses help to show this. The company expressly defines “Virtual Content” as a set that includes in-game currency, champions, skins, emotes, accessories and other items associated with the account633, and admits that this content may be obtained not only by conventional purchase, but also by earning, reward, gift or other internal dynamics of the ecosystem634. This is useful for two reasons. First, because it shows that the contractual design itself recognizes an identifiable core of enjoyment associated with the account, which dismantles the puerile thesis that the object does not legally exist. Second, because it shows that the formation of this core does not end in a one-off market exchange, but takes place within a continuing dynamic of progression, circulation, reward and participation. There, the consumed object is born less as an isolated “digital thing” and more as a position of enjoyment produced and maintained within a governed environment.

The concept of the digital asset also renders another useful service by providing an internal typology of the phenomenon. Not every techno-digital asset has the same structure, function or regime, and the distinction between patrimonial, existential and mixed assets helps keep everything from being treated as an amorphous mass. This classification makes it possible to allocate the effects of ownership, use, transfer and protection more accurately, and it shows that certain digital assets gravitate closer to the patrimonial sphere, others closer to personality, and many combine both p. 567 dimensions Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 56; p. XIV; p. 146)635. Such refinement is useful and should not be discarded. The problem is that it still organizes the phenomenon mainly around the asset, whereas the core of the IOC demands a reading oriented by governed enjoyment.

It is here that the thesis turns. The gain of the civil-law doctrine lies in resolving the problem of the legal status of the techno-digital asset and in blocking the nominalist dissolution of what has already entered the user's sphere. But when the conflict shifts to platform consumption involving continuing, mutable enjoyment dependent on private governance, the civil-law axis of ownership does not by itself capture the heart of the problem. The IOC regime starts from the same recognition of legal status, but shifts the focus: instead of asking predominantly who “has” the asset and how it circulates or is transferred, it asks how enjoyment is maintained, modulated, degraded, made intelligible and restorable over time. It is a change of axis, not a denial of the previous step.

It is worth being explicit about the nature of this change of axis, because it is often read, without due attention, as a simple move from one library shelf to another. The project of the doctrine of digital assets is dogmatically delimited and responds to a recognizable problem. It operates within civil law and confronts, first and foremost, the silence of the Brazilian legal order about an entire class of assets that exists, circulates and produces economic and affective effects, but had no express regime of existence, ownership, transfer and extinction. Lacerda fills this silence with functional taxonomy and classificatory rigor, and that is why the work has become a canonical reference for digital inheritance, for the succession of digital assets and for the civil-law protection of the subject's digital estate. The service rendered is real and cannot be treated as a mere disposable preparatory stage. p. 568 When this framework is called upon to answer what it set out to answer, it answers well.

The present thesis, however, operates in a different domain and responds to a different problem. It confronts not the silence of the Civil Code on the status of the digital asset, but the opacity of the Consumer Protection Code on the regime of the immaterial product under continuing and governed enjoyment. This shift of domain is not a detail of disciplinary organization. It carries a structuring dogmatic consequence, because civil law regulates relationships between formally equal subjects, while consumer law regulates structurally asymmetric relationships under mandatory public policy. Where the civil-law doctrine asks to whom the good belongs, how it circulates and how it is extinguished, the IOC regime asks how the promised utility is maintained, modulated, degraded and restorable over time. The questions are not opposed. They inhabit distinct layers of the phenomenon and call for correspondingly distinct techniques of attribution.

The difference between these layers can be described more sharply through a temporal distinction. The doctrine of digital assets works with a predominantly static ontology of the asset. The good exists, is classifiable, can have an owner, can be transferred, can be extinguished. There is, so to speak, a photograph of the asset, and the relevant legal faculties are organized around that photograph. The IOC regime, in turn, works with a processual ontology of enjoyment. The immaterial object of consumption cannot be captured in a static photograph because its identity lies not in a synchronic cross-section, but in a diachronic course of maintenance, updating, curation and administration. It is, in a legally relevant sense, what is continuously made of it within the environment that produces it. That is why the passage from the civil-law grammar to the consumer-law grammar is not merely a change of applicable code. It is the recognition that the time of performance is part of the very existence of the consumed object.

This temporal difference explains, in part, why the axis of the conflict also shifts. In the typical scenarios addressed by the doctrine of digital assets, the controversy revolves around ownership, circulation and extinction. The questions are who the owner is, whether the good can be inherited, whether the platform can block it after death, whether contractual clauses can bar disposal inter vivos or causa mortis. These are relevant conflicts, and the civil-law framework is well equipped to address them. In the typical scenarios addressed by the IOC regime, however, the controversy revolves around enjoyment, modulation and functional integrity. The questions are whether the object is still what it promised to be, whether the unilateral alteration preserved sufficient functional equivalence, whether the degradation produced by an ordinary update exceeded a legally tolerable margin, whether the opacity of the environment allows the p. 569 consumer to reconstruct institutionally what was altered. These are questions that the civil-law framework can formulate but has no natural tool to answer, because its instruments were forged for another type of litigation.

It is also worth situating the difference between the relevant subjects in the two constructions. For the doctrine of digital assets, the legally central subject is the owner of the digital asset, often the user as holder of a digital estate whose preservation, transfer or allocation is in dispute. The vulnerability that matters in this framework is, as a rule, the existential vulnerability linked to the owner's personality or the patrimonial vulnerability linked to their holdings. For the IOC regime, the legally central subject is the consumer under a platform, living, active and in a continuous relationship with a supplier that concentrates architecture, curation, enforcement and the production of meaning. The vulnerability that matters here is contemporary factual vulnerability, of which semiotic vulnerability is a current specification, and it is not to be confused with any of the figures the civil-law framework has traditionally worked with. The two vulnerabilities coexist in digital environments, but call for different protection techniques and activate distinct normative sources.

It should also be noted, for the sake of methodological honesty, that these differences do not make the doctrine of digital assets irrelevant to the present work. On the contrary, they reveal how precisely that doctrine must be invoked. Lacerda blocks, with a force that could hardly be achieved by any other route, the semantic fraud that would dissolve the digital asset into a mere permission of use revocable at any time. This block is irreplaceable, because without it the platform would win the controversy by private dictionary before it was even framed as a legal controversy. The doctrine of digital assets builds, in other words, the floor from which the debate on regime becomes possible. If the asset is legally light, dissolvable by mere contractual nomenclature and disposable at the supplier's unilateral will, any discussion of continuing enjoyment, functional equivalence and auditability becomes idle talk about something whose legal existence has not even been stabilized. Lacerda stabilizes that existence. This is the point of departure of the present thesis.

The methodological relationship between the two constructions can then be summarized in terms that reject both artificial competition and simulated continuity. The doctrine of digital assets rigorously answers the question of whether the techno-digital asset is a good. The IOC regime answers, with its own instruments, the question of whether the immaterial object of consumption remains enjoyable, in legally equivalent terms, within platform ecosystems. One builds the legal status of the asset; the other builds the legal regime of its continuous governance. One resolves the entry of the digital object into the world of law; the other p. 570 resolves the persistence of the promised utility within a consumer relationship whose performance does not end with the initial act. That is why the thesis, instead of simulating that it has surpassed the prior civil-law step, embraces it and repositions it in its proper function. Lacerda provides the premise of the asset's legal existence. The IOC provides the regime for supplying the enjoyment that this asset, now legally recognized, sustains over time.

This synthesis finally makes it possible to gauge clearly the dogmatic place that the doctrine of digital assets occupies in the edifice of this thesis. It is not a parallel track, nor a competing track, nor a prior track in a chronological sense that had to be surpassed. It is a contiguous and operationally complementary track, which addresses a layer of the phenomenon different from the one on which the present work concentrates and which, by virtue of that difference, makes the advance proposed here possible. Acknowledging this contiguity, instead of disguising it as competition, is an elementary requirement of dogmatic honesty.

Denying a contribution that structures the very possibility of the debate would, moreover, be rhetorically self-defeating, because it would weaken the floor from which the argument of governed enjoyment becomes intelligible. Recognizing the contribution in its proper measure, and marking precisely where the turn is necessary, is what allows the IOC regime to present itself as what it in fact is: an internal development of the consumer-law system based on the immaterial product already contained in art. 3, paragraph 1, of the Consumer Protection Code, built on a civil-law floor that seriously recognizes the legal existence of the digital asset and that, at the same time, leaves open the problem whose answer is the object of this thesis.

One further observation must be made before the comparison proceeds, because it concerns the way in which the doctrine of digital assets may be applied by third parties in concrete cases involving immaterial objects of consumption. The tripartite typology that distinguishes patrimonial, existential and mixed digital assets was formulated to answer problems of ownership, circulation and succession with precision, and it performs that role with recognized competence.

There is, however, a non-negligible operational risk when this same typology is invoked, without mediation, to resolve consumer disputes in platform environments. The risk is that the interpreter, faced with an object such as a skin, a champion, a cosmetic item, the ecosystem's internal currency or equivalent content associated with the account, immediately asks whether the asset is patrimonial, existential or mixed, and organizes the whole dispute around that classification as if it were enough to yield the applicable legal regime.

p. 571 The question, even if technically well formulated on the civil-law plane, dispenses with the consumer-law microsystem before it is even invoked, because the taxonomy at play was forged to answer what the asset is considered in itself, and not what the governed enjoyment made of it within a continuing consumer relationship is. The practical effect of this elision is problematic. The case receives a civil-law framing that is coherent in its own logic, but loses the platform's governance layer, which is precisely where the dispute usually lies in cases of continuous digital consumption.

It is therefore worth recording the methodological position this thesis adopts. The patrimonial, existential and mixed taxonomy, considered in itself, is neither discarded nor demonized. It remains a useful instrument when the conflict actually turns on ownership, succession, post mortem allocation or inter vivos disposal of the digital asset, cases in which the civil-law floor offers a technically adequate answer. The point, however, is that applying this taxonomy to platform consumer disputes demands a prior methodological step, and that step is verifying that the object in question is not merely a digital asset considered generically, but an Immaterial Object of Consumption under governed enjoyment.

When this qualification holds, the relevant initial question is no longer about the patrimonial or existential nature of the asset but about the regime of the enjoyment that this asset sustains within the environment that produces it. This inversion of the order of questions does not annul the civil-law typology, but restores it to its proper function, preventing the classification of the asset from improperly absorbing the entire legal framing of the case and from neutralizing the public-policy protections that the consumer-law microsystem brings into play when the digital object appears as an object of consumption in an environment continuously governed by a supplier.

This shift matters because, on continuous consumption platforms, governance does not act from outside on an already complete digital good. It is part of the very legally relevant utility of the object. Maintenance, updating, curation, enforcement, parameterization and intelligibility of the environment are not incidental vicissitudes of a fully given thing; they are constitutive dimensions of enjoyment. That is why the IOC is formulated as a concretization of the CDC's immaterial product, and not as a simple static “digital thing” anchored in the Civil Code's book on goods636. The civil-law grammar serves to validate the p. 572 legal existence of the asset; it is not enough to exhaust the consumer-law syntax of a form of consumption whose utility stabilizes only through continuous private administration.

This is the first relevant limit of the civil-law framework. It captures more sharply events of suppression, blocking, account termination, unavailability and transfer. It does an excellent job of showing that the digital object is not mist. The problem is that, in ecosystems of governed enjoyment, a decisive part of the conflict takes the form not of an abrupt withdrawal of the object, but of progressive functional erosion through ordinary modulation of the environment. The user apparently still has access to the object; yet its utility may be lowered by updates, balancing, reclassifications, interface changes, alterations of equivalence and continuous reorganizations of the regime of use. The nominal persistence of the asset, or even of the account, is not enough to rule out a defect. The conflict is no longer only about existence or availability, but about the substantial preservation of the function enjoyed.

The IOC category gains explanatory advantage by allowing continuing maintenance, modulation and administration to be treated as part of the consumer performance itself, and not as accidents external to an already formed good. This makes room for two standards that the purely civil-law framework sees less sharply: functional value equivalence, as a criterion for assessing a defect when utility is lowered without formal suppression, and open auditability, as a minimum requirement of controllability over relevant alterations in the supply regime. In other words, the IOC does not contest with the doctrine of digital assets the problem of “being a good”; it confronts the problem of “continuing to be enjoyable in a legally equivalent way”.

The second limit lies in the very idea of vulnerability. The civil-law framework tends to work well with technical disadvantage, classic informational asymmetry, ownership, access and availability. But in governed environments the consumer's vulnerability goes further. It is also semiotic. The perception of loss, of degradation and of how much a downgrade matters depends on signs, interfaces, metrics, rarities, narratives of prestige, showcases and framings controlled by the supplier itself.

The problem is not merely knowing less about the internal workings of the system, but depending on the platform's mediation to form expectations, measure value, perceive functional erosion and name what was actually lost. Where the platform controls not only the object but also the reference points by which the loss can be perceived and contested, vulnerability does not fit entirely within the classic schemes of informational deficit.

p. 573 That is also why the critique of the rhetoric of the “revocable license” remains valuable, but not sufficient. The doctrine of digital assets is right to block the corporate attempt to requalify, by simple contractual nominalism, objects already integrated into the user's sphere as permissions dissolvable by unilateral will. When there is actual economic outlay or a relevant incorporation of utility into the subject's sphere, the language of the license is not enough to erase the legal density of the asset, nor to neutralize the application of public-policy norms Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 124; p. 193)637. This block is fundamental. Without it, the platform would always win by private dictionary. But even after this trick is barred, the central problem of the IOC remains: how to control, within an enjoyment that continues to exist, the ordinary mutations that corrode its functional and symbolic integrity?

The IOC regime recognizes that abusiveness does not necessarily reside entirely in a clause or in an event of overt blocking. In certain cases, it is built into the very design of the supply, when the architecture organizes inducement, extraction and the normalization of losses as an operating principle. The doctrine of digital assets is very helpful in identifying contractual nullities, arbitrary blocks and suppressions of assets. The IOC incorporates this layer, but adds another: reading architecture as conduct and abusive teleology as a design defect of the regime of governed enjoyment. This makes it possible to address not only abrupt withdrawal, but also managed deterioration.

That is why the function of this item is to provide a basis and a contrast, not a competing track. The doctrine of digital assets supports the crucial premise that digital assets are legal goods and that contractual precariousness is not the user's inevitable fate. At the same time, it reveals its own limits when the problem is no longer only ownership, transfer and availability, but the continuous governance of enjoyment. The IOC regime takes on the civil-law gain, rejects the straw man of the legal non-existence of the digital and repositions the debate on the ground p. 574 that matters. It seeks the protection of using, the control of functional integrity and, in certain cases, the protection of being, whenever enjoyment incorporates the symbolic identity and biographical continuity of the consumer user.

This consequence is decisive for the present item, because it dispels from the outset the fantasy that the digital asset would be legally light just because it cannot be held in the hand. If the owner can delete it, assign it, encumber it or transfer it, then we are already dealing with a good dense enough to support legal positions typical of ownership. The enjoyment of the digital asset is therefore not a mere gracious tolerance granted by technology; it can be legally organized and protected, including through the assignment of use to third parties as a corollary of the very exercise of the corresponding subjective right Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 82). This point is especially important because it strengthens the argument without resorting to terminological magic. The civil-law doctrine of digital assets forcefully resolves the problem of the asset's capacity to form part of an estate, to be enjoyed, to circulate in transactions and to receive legal protection. The insufficiency would lie not in this framework's inability to recognize the asset, but in the fact that, even while recognizing appropriation and circulation, it does not exhaust the conflicts of continuous enjoyment, unilateral mutability and functional governance in a platform environment Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 151). ↩

The usefulness of the doctrine of digital assets also lies in offering an internal criterion for organizing the phenomenon, which keeps all techno-digital reality from being treated as an amorphous mass. Not every digital asset has the same structure, function or regime. There are assets with clearly patrimonial content, assets of an existential character, and cases in which both dimensions appear in combination. This distinction matters because it allows the legal effects of ownership, use, transfer and protection to be allocated more precisely Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 56). This classificatory refinement is one of the most important gains of the civil-law framework, because it keeps the debate from getting stuck in vague generalities. Knowing whether the asset is patrimonial, existential or mixed is not academic taxonomic fussiness; it is what allows circulation, succession, the exercise of private autonomy and the protection of goods linked to the owner's personal sphere to be properly calibrated. The very adoption of this criterion in proposals for legislative reform shows that it is not a mere ornamental doctrinal invention, but a genuinely useful tool of legal work Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. XIV). This gain must be honestly acknowledged because it strengthens, rather than weakens, the critical step of the thesis. The doctrine of digital assets classifies the asset well, distinguishes its species intelligently and provides a reliable compass for the rules on ownership and its circulation. The limit appears elsewhere: even with a good typology of the asset, the framework still does not fully resolve the problems that arise when the conflict no longer revolves only around ownership and comes to bear on continuous enjoyment, governed mutability and the functional integrity of the experience consumed on a platform Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 146). ↩

  8. The contractual language of the precarious or revocable license is not enough, by itself, to dissolve the legal density of a digital asset already incorporated into the user's sphere. When there is actual economic outlay to acquire digital content or utilities, the mere corporate requalification of the relationship as a “mere license” cannot operate as a semantic trick capable of disfiguring the existence of a digital estate. In other words, the fact that the platform calls it a license does not oblige Law to pretend that it never had patrimonial substance Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 124). The doctrine of digital assets makes it possible to state seriously that music libraries, video libraries, virtual libraries and other similar collections may form part of the user's estate, including for purposes of succession, despite corporate rhetoric to the contrary. The legal density of the good's incorporation into the subject's sphere does not disappear by private decree Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 124). The same logic extends to clauses that seek to neutralize access to, use of or disposal of these assets in terms incompatible with public-policy norms. Provisions that absolutely prohibit access to the asset or its legal allocation may be null when they collide with the general parameters of the system, including those prohibiting the advance waiver of rights in adhesion contracts Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 193). The value of this approach here is central: the doctrine of digital assets effectively blocks the nominalist dissolution of the asset. What the thesis will need to show later is that this block, although indispensable, still does not exhaust the problem when the enjoyment of the object is continuously governed, reconfigured and strained by the platform. ↩
