6.2.4.1.1 On the critical review of recent literature

p. 590

Here, the critical review of recent literature on “digital vulnerability” serves a strictly instrumental function. It is not intended to open an autonomous state of the art nor to compete for bibliographic prominence with the central argument. The aim is to show that the competing vocabulary, although relevant and academically serious, does not pin down with sufficient sharpness the legally relevant mechanism of the asymmetry at issue, which is why it works better as a scenario label than as a decision criterion. In dogmatics, this difference matters. Concepts are valuable not only for their capacity to describe a plausible environment, but for their aptitude to organize the passage from fact to normative consequence, carving out the p. 591 legally relevant aspect of the conflict and making it tractable in terms of attribution, evidence and remedy Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 108; p. 247])666.

This is the first criterion for reading the selected corpus. The question is not whether recent literature correctly perceived that consumer vulnerability is aggravated in digitally mediated environments. To a large extent, it did. The question is whether the category mobilized converts this diagnosis into a legal form stable enough to guide normative application. In dogmatic discourse, concepts need to function as operational theoretical abbreviations, linking factual hypothesis and legal effect. When they remain too broad, they manage to condense a legitimate concern, but lose the precision needed to organize the factual basis, the required evidence and the corresponding consequence Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 92; p. 144; p. 246; p. 310])667.

The review was therefore conducted within a controlled scope. It considered recent Brazilian academic works that employ “digital vulnerability” or very close expressions, in the context of consumption, platforms, continuous digital services or technologically mediated ecosystems, provided that the category was used as an explanatory element of the argument and not merely as a passing mention. The scope does not aim at exhaustiveness. It aims at minimum verifiability. Only texts in which the expression triggers some normative consequence, however elementary, such as a duty of information, liability, p. 592 abusiveness, redistribution of the burden of proof or expanded protective relief, enter the corpus. The requirement is consistent with the very idea that legal research cannot stop at a descriptive understanding of the phenomenon, but needs to convert this diagnosis into normation, or at least into a legal form apt to guide conduct and decision Reale(Reale, 2001, p. 304; p. 173)668.

The reading matrix applied to the corpus was deliberately simple. Each work was tested for whether “digital vulnerability” named only the scenario in which the problem arises or actually identified the legally relevant mechanism of the asymmetry. From there, four points were observed: whether there was a definition or only an invocation; what concrete asymmetry was described; whether the text indicated some minimally reconstructible evidentiary standard; and what normative structure or legal consequence was triggered. The criterion is important because it prevents impressionistic criticism. The objection is not that the term is new, attractive or too diffuse for the interpreter's taste. The objection is that the label often fails to deliver enough normative form to convert the phenomenon into an operable category, that is, into a stable correspondence between apprehended social reality and a structure apt to guide the application of the CDC Reale(Reale, 2001, p. 174; p. 175; p. 187)669.

The first result of the survey is that recent literature correctly perceived the intensification of the consumer's fragility in platform environments. This appears sharply in more robust formulations, which link so-called digital vulnerability to the p. 593 monetization of data, the centrality of attention, behavioral advertising, dark patterns and decision-making opacity distributed along the consumer journey Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 230; p. 15; p. 143-144)670. It also appears in works that describe the consumer's vulnerabilization on the basis of lengthy terms of use, data transfer, the appearance of gratuitousness, opaque language and decision-making acceleration on digital platforms Pinto(Furtado; Pinto, 2024, p. 13; p. 12; p. 21)671. The problem, therefore, is not an invention of the thesis. The field already sees that something important has changed.

p. 594 The second result, however, is more decisive. Even when the literature is sophisticated, the category “digital vulnerability” usually operates as an aggregator of very diverse phenomena. Data protection, technical opacity, behavioral manipulation, profiling, deficient consent, dark patterns, hyperconnectivity, the monetization of attention and informational asymmetry all appear at once under the same label. The formulation is strong as an environmental synthesis, but less precise as a mechanistic delimitation. The concept ends up gathering an ecosystem of risks instead of stabilizing the specific gear of the legally relevant asymmetry. This explains why it works better to describe the problem than to guide its fine-grained juridification.

p. 595 This deficit also appears in closely related competing formulations. The notion of “cybervulnerability” records, with keen sensitivity, that cyberspace aggravates already known vulnerabilities and widens the power gap between consumer and supplier, including through factors such as insufficient literacy, informational asymmetry, data collection and manipulation of the will Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 86; p. 93; p. 97; p. 120; p. 83-84)672. There is real merit in this. But the category itself remains closely tied to the technological scenario as its organizing principle, which makes it less sharp for isolating the specific legal mechanism of vulnerabilization it seeks to explain. The author even seeks juridification by way of the CDC and the Constitution, which shows a serious effort at normativity, but this still coexists with a strong dependence on “cyberspace” as the structural axis of the formulation Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 93; p. 113; p. 119)673.

p. 596 Something similar happens with the “situational digital hypervulnerability” worked out in the field of electronic games. The literature identifies, with relevant precision, real mechanisms of aggravation of vulnerability on platforms, such as continuous data collection, dynamic architecture, adaptation of design to user behavior, captivity, situational monopoly, irregular rewards and immersion strategies Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 8; p. 23; p. 15-16; p. 11; p. 21)674. There is even some normative density, with protective consequences drawn for cases involving children, family and p. 597 market practices in digital games Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 22-23)675. Even so, the category remains very dependent on the public affected and on the specific context of application. It explains a set of situations well. It explains less well the general mechanism that needs to be stabilized in order to recognize the contemporary form of structural risk that manifests itself in the key of the factual vulnerability already recognized by the CDC.

The analysis of the vocabulary of “electronic” or “technological vulnerability” reinforces the same diagnosis. Such expressions are useful for naming real barriers created by the compulsory digitalization of services, channels and procedures, especially in access to justice and in the technical exclusion of certain groups Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 3; p. 2)676. They also reveal the proliferation of internal subdivisions, such as technical, informational, algorithmic, behavioral and related vulnerabilities, which attests to the field's sensitivity to new asymmetries Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 4)677. But this fertility comes with classificatory instability. The p. 598 term helps to describe technological obstacles and institutional accessibility responses. For reconstructing, in a consumer-law key, the violated duty, the factual basis, the required evidence and the appropriate remedy, however, its usefulness remains limited.

There is, further, an important contrast with the field of access to justice. The expression “digital vulnerability” circulates there to designate phenomena of technical exclusion, lack of access, skills deficits and barriers to insertion in digitalized environments, especially for more socially exposed groups Simões(Simões, 2024, p. 79; p. 83; p. 86)678. In another formulation, it appears as a general layer of vulneration associated with broad sociotechnical transformation, privacy, informational self-determination, citizenship and the overlaps between income, gender and race Simões(Simões, 2024, p. 69; p. 79-80)679. This confirms that the vocabulary is serious, current and relevant. But it also confirms its polysemy. In many uses, “digital vulnerability” names a structural condition of exclusion or exposure in a p. 599 technological environment, and not a more delimited legal mechanism of relational asymmetry within the CDC.

The contrast with the work of Ricardo Morishita Wada helps to make this point clearer. The legally relevant problem lies not in the relationship taking place in a sophisticated technological universe, but in whether there is functional opacity capable of compromising the consumer's understanding, choice and practical self-determination. Informational failure does not stem only from the absence of data; it may also arise when the information exists, but the way it is presented neutralizes its practical usefulness, reduces transparency, hinders comparison or compromises understanding Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 168; p. 193-194; p. 213-214)680. This yardstick is decisive because it shows what the competing vocabulary lacks in many cases. It is not enough to point out that vulneration occurs in the “digital”. One must show how the environment concretely produces opacity, noise, decision asymmetry and evidentiary asymmetry.

The table confirms a simple point. The literature reviewed is relevant and, in several cases, normatively ambitious. But the label “digital vulnerability” or its close equivalents works, most of the time, better as a synthesis of a scenario than as a means of isolating the legally central mechanism. At times it yields a concrete protective consequence, such as the defense of a stronger reversal of the burden of proof or of broader contractual control. Even so, that consequence is usually drawn wholesale, from the overall perception of a hostile environment, and not from a more precise stabilization of the specific mechanism of asymmetry.

It is precisely from this that the refining criterion adopted here is drawn. To operate dogmatically within the CDC, any category of vulnerability must satisfy at least four cumulative requirements: identify a mechanism, and not merely a context; convert that mechanism into a duty, an unlawful act or a criterion of control; offer a minimally reconstructible evidentiary standard; and point to a typical consequence compatible with the structure of the protective system. When these elements are absent, the category may remain useful as a diagnosis of the environment, but it remains weak as a concept for decision.

The adoption of “semiotic vulnerability” responds to this problem. It does not replace “digital vulnerability” out of mere terminological whim. It replaces a predominantly environmental category with one aimed at describing the mechanism. The relevant asymmetry comes to be located in the production and governance of meaning, that is, in the way the platform manages expectation, perception of value, perception of risk, comparability, intelligibility of the offer and conditions for contestation. This yields greater operability. p. 601 Information ceases to be thought of as volume and comes to be thought of as usefulness for decision and verifiability. Advertising ceases to be a mere promotional backdrop and becomes subject to control for ambiguity and relevant omission. Evidence no longer gravitates only around the consumer's narrative and comes to require the preservation of trails, verifiable transparency and a rational redistribution of the burden of proof. And abusiveness may, when necessary, be reconstructed at the level of the very architecture of supply. It is this operational gain, and not a dispute over labels, that justifies the terminological substitution adopted here.

  4. Assessing a legal category requires checking whether it establishes a sufficiently stable correspondence between the social reality it apprehends and a normative form capable of guiding application. In Miguel Reale, the maturity of the Science of Law stems precisely from the perception that certain human behaviors, although situated in a changing historical context, reveal regularities tied to factual presuppositions and to axiological demands, which makes their legal modeling possible. As the author points out, social life, “despite its continuous change, presents stable and regular relations”, allowing “an anticipated representation of what will occur” Reale(Reale, 2001, p. 174). Hence a stable legal form can be neither the product of loose abstraction nor of mere terminological adherence to current vocabulary. Reale maintains that legal models are “normative structures” carved “in the concreteness of human experience” Reale(Reale, 2001, p. 175), and adds that the fact stands “at the beginning and at the end of the normative process”, first as a species provided for in the norm and then as a legally qualified event, by virtue of the correspondence between what occurred and the typical form stabilized beforehand Reale(Reale, 2001, p. 187). The critical examination of the literature, therefore, cannot settle for expressions that merely portray a social scenario in diffuse terms; the concept must apprehend relevant regularities and translate them into a consistent legal form, capable of guiding the application of the CDC. ↩
  5. The most sophisticated recent literature on consumption in the digital environment has already grasped, in considerable depth, that the consumer's contemporary vulnerability can no longer be thought of solely through the classic schemes. This becomes evident when one takes as a starting point the simultaneous condition of the consumer who is a personal data subject as the target of marketing practices, the raw material for their development and the product traded among the agents of the system, together with growing informational asymmetry and the intensification of abusive practices based on prediction products embedded in the consumer journey Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 230). This robustness grows when the picture is linked to technology as a structuring factor of relationships, to big data and big analytics, to the monetization of data, to the growth of information flows and to the centrality of attention as a capturable economic resource Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 15). The critical value of this construction, for the present review, lies in showing that the competing vocabulary is neither superficial nor accidental. On the contrary, it is strong, current and academically serious. But it is precisely for that reason that its limit matters more: even in its most elaborate version, the category tends to function as a synthesis of a broad ecosystem of risks and practices, better suited to condensing the problematic environment than to isolating, with maximum dogmatic precision, the central mechanism that produces the legally relevant asymmetry. The notion of digital vulnerability acquires special explanatory force when linked to the idea of a structure of stimuli and controls that imperceptibly pervades the consumer journey. In this picture, digital advertising, behavioral advertising, dark patterns and decision-making opacity appear not as isolated phenomena, but as elements of a broader architecture of influence over the consumer's choice process, in a setting in which the sufficiency of personal data protection is examined and the existence of a new and universal digital vulnerability is asserted, giving rise to an equally universal state of hyposufficiency Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 6). The formulation is especially relevant because it shows the category's capacity to bring together central problems of the contemporary digital market. This aggregating vocation becomes even more explicit when dark patterns and digital advertising are placed in the broader context represented by the consumer journey itself, going beyond what is immediately visible both to the user and to the legal community Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 143-144). The point is valuable for the critical review because it reveals that the competing category does not merely name a specific risk, but organizes a comprehensive explanatory superstructure. And it is precisely there that its limit appears for the purposes of this thesis: the category works very well as an environmental synthesis of an ecosystem of risks, but less well as an analytical instrument designed to carve out, in a mechanistic and attributable way, the specific mechanism of asymmetry that matters to the dogmatic reconstruction proposed here. The dogmatic operability of the competing category shows clearly when a concrete procedural consequence is drawn from it: the universalization of the consumer's hyposufficiency in the digital environment and, by derivation, of the reversal of the burden of proof. The idea is that, in the face of ever more sophisticated and imperceptible deceptive practices, what once functioned as an individual mark of procedural weakness becomes a general trait of digital consumer relationships, so that there could be no just legal protection as long as the reversal of the burden of proof did not operate as the rule in this field Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 98). This is important because it shows that the category does not stand still on the sociological plane; it seeks to produce a verifiable legal effect. This consequence is reaffirmed when it is argued that the protection currently offered by the CDC and the LGPD is insufficient in view of the new bases of relationships in the digital environment, which is why digital vulnerability and the corresponding hyposufficiency should be affirmed universally, with reversal of the burden of proof ope legis, and not merely ope judicis Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 234). The gain of this formulation is clear: it offers a concrete protective answer, above all on the evidentiary plane. The limit, however, appears in the same movement. The central legal consequence drawn from the category tends to be organized wholesale, through one large general effect, which confirms its strength as a comprehensive diagnosis, but also its more global and aggregating character. Instead of selectively carving out the specific mechanism that produces the asymmetry, the category serves as the foundation for a protective regime expanded across the board. ↩
  6. The recent circulation of the expression “digital vulnerability of the consumer” reveals that the literature already clearly perceives the worsening of the user's fragility in platform-mediated consumer relationships, especially when the business model is organized around lengthy terms of use, data sharing and the appearance of gratuitousness. In this context, vulnerability is described as a factual situation of heightened weakness in the face of transactional complexity and the dysfunctions inherent in cyberspace, capable of reducing both the understanding of risks and the consumer's practical capacity to react Pinto(Furtado; Pinto, 2024, p. 13). The value of the formulation, here, lies in showing that the problem has already been clearly perceived in the contemporary Brazilian consumer-law field. At the same time, it is precisely this descriptive virtue that exposes the dogmatic limit of the label. The same category comes to gather, under a single name, the digital medium, the form of contracting, data processing, behavioral economics, decision inducement and the crisis of consent, functioning as an aggregator of multiple risks and asymmetries Pinto(Furtado; Pinto, 2024, p. 12; p. 21). Consequently, the expression proves strong for naming the problematic scenario of platforms, but less precise for isolating, with maximum sharpness, the legally relevant mechanism of vulnerabilization. It is precisely in this mismatch between a good perception of the phenomenon and a less refined operative key that the critical review of the corpus finds its usefulness. The worsening of vulnerability in the digital environment is presented not merely as a generic abstraction about the internet, but as the result of concrete factors that reorganize the asymmetry between consumer and supplier. Among them are the technical opacity of the tools, the complexity of the languages and codes used, the user's lack of control over the chain of processing of their data and ignorance of the functionalities actually at work in the service Pinto(Furtado; Pinto, 2024, p. 11-12). The relevance of this description lies in showing that the problem is not exhausted by the mere modernity of the medium, but involves concrete forms of inaccessibility, invisibility and loss of control. The same difficulty deepens when the speed, apparent ease and fluidity of the virtual environment lower the user's guard and disrupt their perception of risk, encouraging ill-considered acceptance and little reading of the terms of use Pinto(Furtado; Pinto, 2024, p. 16). The point is important because it already reveals real mechanisms of vulnerabilization: accelerated decision-making, opaque language, technical invisibility and the weakening of informational control. Even so, all these elements remain gathered under a category heavily dependent on the digital scenario itself, which makes it a good contextual description of the problem, but a less sharp tool for stabilizing, on its own, the most dogmatically precise form of the asymmetry in question. The critical usefulness of the expression “digital vulnerability of the consumer” does not disappear because it falls short of maximum refinement as an explanatory category. On the contrary, its recent circulation shows a real effort to draw concrete protective consequences in the consumer-law field, especially when it is argued that unrestricted authorization for data processing, imposed as consideration in zero-price models, is abusive, even if formally provided for in the terms of use Pinto(Furtado; Pinto, 2024, p. 23). The category, therefore, does not remain entirely on the plane of sociological setting: it already seeks to ground contractual control, a protective rereading of consent and the application of the CDC to the structure of the business model. This normative aspiration also appears when it is accepted that the binding force of the vulnerable user's will may be relativized in view of the concrete fragility of the situation, with axiological parameters tied to the protection of the person and to the containment of abuse in standard-form clauses prevailing Pinto(Furtado; Pinto, 2024, p. 22). Added to this is the parallel with the idea of contracts tied to existential or functionally central needs, which reinforces the demand for a protective reading of the clauses and of the very design of the relationship Pinto(Furtado; Pinto, 2024, p. 14-15). The critical balance, then, is not that the expression is useless, but that it is partially useful. It already manages to produce relevant legal consequences, but continues to work better as a broad aggregating label, stronger at gathering multiple practical problems under one name than at isolating, with maximum methodological precision, the dogmatically specific mechanism of asymmetry that the present thesis seeks to capture. ↩
  7. Leonardo Machado Acosta's dissertation is useful, at this point, for showing the contrast within the very recent literature that seeks to name the worsening of consumer vulnerability in digital environments. His contribution is relevant because it shows that the field has already clearly perceived that the classic framework of consumer vulnerability comes under strain in cyberspace, requiring additional formulations to capture the increased asymmetry between consumer and supplier. Along these lines, the author defines “cybervulnerability” as “the hypervulnerability of the consumer caused by the greater fragility of this subject in consumer relationships established in cyberspace in view of the existence of more uncertainties, risks and occurrences of damage in this environment, which increases the power gap between the consumer and the supplier” Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 86). The formulation has value because it records, without caricature, the contemporary perception that the digital environment intensifies known fragilities and imposes more robust protective demands. At the same time, Acosta's own conceptual construction helps to bring out the critical point that justifies the present review. Acosta maintains that cybervulnerability stems from the “aggravation of the consumer's vulnerability(ies) caused by the insertion and action of this agent within cyberspace” Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 86), stressing that it is a form of hypervulnerability resulting from the sum of vulnerabilities beyond the usual, arising from the characteristics of this environment Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 93). The problem, therefore, was correctly perceived. What remains open is whether the chosen category delivers, with sufficient dogmatic precision, the legally operable mechanism that explains this worsening. The worsening of vulnerability in the digital environment need not be described only through broad labels of technological setting; it can be broken down into concrete factors of asymmetry produced by the platform's own design. This is what makes his work useful for identifying concretely which factors worsen vulnerability, keeping the debate from remaining at a generic level. The author shows that this worsening stems not from a vague abstraction about “cyberspace”, but from empirically perceptible elements that reorganize the asymmetry between consumer and supplier. Prominent among them is aggravated technical vulnerability associated with two central circumstances: one structural, tied to the very architecture of the virtual environment, and the other situational, related to the cognitive and behavioral effects produced by the use of this medium, both of which further weaken the consumer in relationships established digitally Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 97). He also sets out other concrete factors that deepen this picture, such as the informational power gap, the lack of digital literacy, the indiscriminate collection and processing of personal data and the manipulation of individuals' will Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 120). In an especially illuminating passage, the author describes how the digital market tends to make the provision of services conditional on the collection of data, which are then stored and processed by algorithms designed to shape the will, whether through behavioral advertising or through broader forms of decision inducement in favor of companies or of other centers of power that control such information bases Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 83-84). The merit of the formulation lies in demonstrating that recent literature has already accurately perceived several of the material factors that worsen vulnerability in the digital environment. The critical point, however, remains whether the category chosen to name them succeeds in translating, with sufficient dogmatic density, the specific mechanism of vulnerabilization that emerges from them. ↩
  8. Reading Leonardo Machado Acosta makes it possible to assess, with some precision, how far the category of “cybervulnerability” advances toward the juridification of the problem and how far it still remains closely tied to the technological scenario it seeks to describe. On the one hand, the proposal is not presented as a mere sociological label for the digital environment. The author insists on the need to establish a concept and parameters grounded in the principles of the Consumer Protection Code and the Federal Constitution, precisely to allow effective protection and to guarantee material equality to consumers in cyberspace Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 93). There is, therefore, a genuine effort to juridify the category. On the other hand, the very structure of the formulation reveals that this juridification still coexists with a strong dependence on the technological scenario as its organizing axis. This shows when the author acknowledges the precariousness, indeed near absence, of normative protection specifically aimed at the “cybervulnerable”, which hinders the direct subsumption of protective rules in the Brazilian legal order and makes the search for provisional interpretive solutions inevitable Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 113). The same tension reappears when he calls, for the future, for specific regulation that recognizes and combats the vulnerabilities aggravated in cyberspace, while, for the present, resorting to interpretation favorable to the consumer based on the CDC and the Constitution Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 119). The critical balance, then, is not that the category is useless, but that it is a formulation that correctly perceives the need for heightened protection, while still adhering markedly to the technological scenario as its organizing principle, without isolating with ideal sharpness the specific mechanism of vulnerabilization it seeks to explain. ↩
  9. The perception that the consumer's classic vulnerability is insufficient to explain asymmetries produced in digital ecosystems already appears clearly in recent literature, including in the field of electronic games. The problem, therefore, is not an artificial creation of the present thesis, but has already been identified by competing formulations that seek to name the worsening of the consumer's fragility in platform environments. It is in this vein that Azevedo and Torres develop the notion of “situational digital vulnerability”, noting that it may take, among other forms, a form “by captivity”, tied to relationships that extend over time, and a “neuropsychological” one, caused mainly by the application of behavioral economics and gamification Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 8). The article becomes especially relevant because it brings this diagnosis to the world of games, showing that, in the very empirical object given priority here, the need for supplementary categories to explain the intensification of vulnerability is already perceived. Discussing Fortnite, the authors observe that situational digital vulnerability by captivity manifests itself “mainly through the consumer's submission to the platform, which holds the items acquired for use in the development of the game”, to which are added the multiplayer character and the formation of teams as factors that keep players in the environment Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 23). The merit of the reference lies precisely in showing that the problem was well perceived. The critical point, however, remains whether categories of this kind, heavily dependent on the environment and on the public affected, manage to isolate with sufficient precision the dogmatic mechanism of vulnerabilization. The worsening of vulnerability in the digital environment need not be described only through broad labels of technological setting; it can be broken down into concrete factors of asymmetry produced by the very design of the platform. This is what makes the article by Azevedo and Torres useful for the critical review of the corpus. The authors describe contemporary digital architectures as structures that are “customizable, dynamic and governed by the analysis and interpretation of data”, capable of continuously collecting data, learning from user interactions and changing the environment “at any moment” to secure better results for the supplier by exploiting the consumer's vulnerabilities Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 15-16). Here one faces no longer a merely generic technological scenario, but an operational arrangement that monitors, adjusts and influences conduct. This mechanical dimension appears even more clearly when the authors explain that prolonged use of the service increases the capacity to identify and target vulnerabilities, because continuous data collection allows the design to be adjusted to influence behavior, while captivity makes it hard to leave the platform and builds a kind of “situational monopoly” Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 11). Added to this, in the case of electronic games, are irregular rewards, sounds, colors and greater graphic immersion as factors of behavioral conditioning Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 21). The article thus has the merit of pointing to real mechanisms that worsen vulnerability, even though the chosen category remains firmly anchored in the broad frame of the “situational digital”. ↩
  10. The dogmatic operability of competing categories can also be tested by their capacity to generate concrete legal consequences, and not only by their descriptive aptitude. In this dimension, the formulation of Azevedo and Torres does not remain entirely on the sociological plane, since the authors seek to link it to specific protective responses within the consumer-law and constitutional system. This appears when they argue for the “hypervulnerability of the family unit” through equivalence with the child on the basis of Article 29 of the CDC, treating the family as a collectivity exposed to commercial practices which, although they reach minors first, spread their effects to the whole family unit and weaken the exercise of parental authority Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 22). The same movement can be seen when they associate the platform's practices with concrete duties to contain economic power and to protect children and adolescents fully, calling on the State, society and the family to act against the harm to healthy development produced by such dynamics Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 22- 23). The article, therefore, shows some normative density and a capacity to generate real protective consequences. Even so, the way these consequences are triggered reveals that the category remains highly dependent on the public affected and on the specific context of application, which reinforces its usefulness as a qualified object of critical review, but also exposes the limits of its dogmatic stability as a general explanatory category. ↩
  11. The practical usefulness of categories such as “electronic”, “digital” or “technological vulnerability” appears more clearly when they are used to describe real barriers produced by the compulsory digitalization of services, channels and procedures. At this point, the vocabulary makes it possible to identify concrete exclusions, above all when technology becomes an obstacle to access to justice, to communication or to the exercise of legally relevant positions Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 3). There is, therefore, some minimal normative yield: the duty of institutional adaptation, inclusion and accessibility. This connection is reinforced when the problem is linked to the so-called “platform effect”, that is, the concentration of economic growth in a few scalable digital organizations Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 2). The text no longer speaks only of individual technical ignorance, but of structural asymmetries produced by platformized markets. Even so, it is precisely in this test of operability that the limit of the category appears most clearly. The core of the formulation remains tied to technological obstacles, the digital environment and access to justice, and not to the rigorous identification of a dogmatically specific mechanism that produces asymmetry within the consumer relationship. The vocabulary works well for describing a context of exclusion and for justifying institutional accessibility responses. But for reconstructing more precisely the duty breached, the factual basis, the required evidence and the appropriate remedy in digitally mediated consumer disputes, it remains too atmospheric. ↩
  12. The critical review of recent literature reveals that the field is organized not only around the expression “digital vulnerability”, but also moves among neighboring formulations such as “electronic vulnerability” and “technological vulnerability”. This proliferation of terms is in itself a symptom of conceptual instability: the problem is clearly perceived, but there is still no sufficiently stable delimitation either of the category's name or of its scope. In one of these distinctions, it is argued that technological vulnerability would cover those unskilled in or excluded from the digital world, while digital vulnerability would reach subjects already present, in some way, in this setting Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 4). The critical consequence is clear: the recent competing vocabulary still has shifting and poorly consolidated boundaries. The dispersion grows when several internal dimensions of this phenomenon are proposed, such as technological, technical, informational, algorithmic and neural or psychological-behavioral vulnerabilities Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 4). The field undoubtedly shows sensitivity to the problem, but it also reveals a certain classificatory inflation. The picture becomes even more revealing when specific labels appear, such as the so-called “vulnerability by manipulation”, associated with gamification and conditioning on digital platforms, including in gig economy contexts Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 4). Here the material is valuable precisely because it shows the current state of the literature: fertile, intuitive, sensitive to new asymmetries, but semantically dispersed and oscillating among environment, technique, behavior, algorithm and exclusion from access. It is precisely this scenario that reinforces the need for the conceptual refinement undertaken by the present thesis. ↩
  13. The circulation of the expression “digital vulnerability” in fields other than consumption reinforces the perception that this is relevant, current and serious terminology, but semantically broad and dogmatically unstable. In the debate on access to justice, it appears in connection with a multifaceted phenomenon ranging from the lack of physical access to the internet and to adequate devices to the absence of minimal digital skills for using technologies, bearing especially hard on the elderly, people with little schooling and socially disadvantaged groups Simões(Simões, 2024, p. 79). The term is used to name a structural condition of exclusion and of difficulty in taking part in technologically mediated environments, and not a more clearly delimited legal mechanism of relational asymmetry. The same reading is reinforced when the problem is described in terms of barriers to access to justice: even with the gradual expansion of internet access, exclusions persist that are linked to poor connections and insufficient technical skills, factors that become concrete obstacles to the use of institutionally digitalized channels Simões(Simões, 2024, p. 83). In this frame, so-called digital vulnerability appears as a new obstacle to access, adding to the classic barriers already identified Simões(Simões, 2024, p. 86). The vocabulary describes a structural scenario of exclusion well, but does so at a broad, environmental and cross-cutting level, proving less precise for guiding attribution and evidence within the CDC. ↩
  14. The polysemy of the expression “digital vulnerability” appears even more clearly when it is presented as a new general layer of vulnerability tied to the growth of technological means and to the need for continuous adaptation to a fully digitalized social life Simões(Simões, 2024, p. 69). In this framing, the term already emerges less as a delimited dogmatic tool than as the name of a general condition produced by a sweeping sociotechnical transformation. This semantic openness widens further when digital vulnerability is described as a state of predisposition to risk in cyberspaces, fostering inequities, power asymmetries, diminished citizenship and violations of privacy and informational self-determination, and is even likened to the idea of socio-spatial vulnerability Simões(Simões, 2024, p. 79-80). Added to this is the recognition that the problem takes on various strands and often overlaps with other situations of vulnerability, intersecting with income, gender and race Simões(Simões, 2024, p. 80). The point is very useful here because it shows both the strength and the limit of the competing vocabulary: it sensitively captures a complex environment of exclusions, but tends to function as a broad category of context, less able to isolate the central legal mechanism of asymmetry that the thesis seeks to delimit. ↩
  15. The critique of the literature on “digital vulnerability” cannot rest on the trivial fact that many consumer relationships today unfold in technically complex contexts; that alone does not yet produce a dogmatic category. What matters, for consumer law, is whether the formulation identifies a mechanism of functional opacity capable of compromising the consumer's understanding, choice and practical self-determination to the supplier's benefit. It is precisely in this respect that the passage invoked by Wada (2016) offers a sounder criterion. Citing Benjamin, the text shows that, in mass-scale and technically complex operations, the legally relevant fact lies not in the technology itself, but in the supplier's exploitation of the informational asymmetry of the “lay subject”. The problem arises when, faced with consumers unversed in “complex technical, commercial, financial or tax transactions and operations”, the supplier takes advantage of the “weakness or ignorance of the consumer” to impose the opportunistic logic of “if it sticks, it sticks” Wada(Benjamin apud Wada, 2016, p. 193-194). This makes it possible to assess whether the literature on “digital vulnerability” actually isolates an operable informational failure, marked by noise, opacity and the exploitation of ignorance, or merely names, loosely, the technological scenario in which the relationship takes place. The usefulness of Ricardo Morishita Wada's thesis, at this point, lies in allowing the consumer's informational vulnerabilization to be described through concrete mechanisms rather than vague abstractions. Although the work does not establish “noise” or “functional opacity” as autonomous nominal categories, it accurately describes equivalent phenomena by showing that information can be formally provided and, at the same time, materially degraded in its function of clarification, comparison and decision. This can be seen, for example, when the structure of the offer reduces transparency and makes comparison impossible. Discussing bundles of banking products and services, Wada observes that aggregating multiple items “reduces price transparency”, hindering the comparison of offers, and that it was “very difficult for the consumer to compare the benefit packages offered by banks” Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 168). The information exists, but the way it is presented neutralizes its practical usefulness. The same logic appears when the author treats the inadequacy of the channel and the informational deficit as concrete instruments for exploiting vulnerability, noting that the “precariousness of verbal information may compromise the consumer's understanding” and is incompatible with the regime of transparency, information and balance required by the CDC Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 213-214). Seen this way, noise, functional opacity and informational inadequacy cease to be convenient metaphors and come to designate concrete ways in which defective communication compromises understanding, comparability and practical freedom of choice. ↩
