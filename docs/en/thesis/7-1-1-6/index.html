7.1.1.6 On the parameters of functional value equivalence as the core of enjoyment in a mutable environment

See this section in the PDF

p. 729

The legal legitimacy of mutability in platform ecosystems cannot be assessed on the basis of the mere nominal continuity of the object, nor by the simple finding that the digital environment, by its nature, admits ordinary updates, adjustments and reconfigurations. Mutability, by itself, is not unlawful. The problem begins when it ceases to operate as a legitimate form of maintaining, improving or adapting the supply and comes to degrade, empty or divert, to a relevant degree, the function that justified the acquisition and structured the legitimate expectation of enjoyment. It is at this point that functional value equivalence emerges as a central parameter of control. Its usefulness lies in providing a criterion capable of distinguishing change compatible with the continuity of the relationship from alteration that converts the plasticity of the environment into a technology of normalized loss.

Functional value equivalence cannot be reduced to maintaining technical performance in the strict sense, nor to the quantitative preservation of isolated attributes. Its content is more demanding. What matters is the substantial preservation of the role the object plays in the acquired experience, considering its concrete utility in the ecosystem, its relational place in the environment of use and, when legally relevant, its identity or symbolic density. In immaterial objects of consumption, functional value does not necessarily coincide with operational efficiency measurable by linear indicators. It is often also constituted by the position the object occupies in the consumer's trajectory of enjoyment, by the type of interaction it allows, by the way it connects with other parts of the ecosystem and by the practical meaning it takes on in the acquired experience.

Legal control of mutability must not be held prisoner by an excessively formal or technicist logic. Relevant alterations may keep the name, general appearance or nominal existence of the object and still substantially compromise the p. 730 enjoyment legitimately incorporated into the relationship. The permanence of the item, the functionality, the account, the access or the feature does not, by itself, neutralize the emptying of the experience if the concrete role of the object is degraded, diverted or made substantially different from the one that justified the acquisition. Functional equivalence works precisely against this kind of defense. It prevents the supplier from relying on apparent continuity to conceal a relevant loss of practical content.

From this perspective, legally legitimate mutability is that which substantially preserves the function of the object in the consumer's experience. This does not mean demanding perfect identity between the earlier and later versions, nor imposing a freeze on the service. Continuous digital environments presuppose evolution, adaptation and, in many cases, internal rebalancing. What the parameter rejects is not change in itself, but its use as an instrument to shift onto the consumer the cost of reconfigurations that destroy or significantly lower the acquired utility without compatible restoration. Equivalence, therefore, does not demand formal equality of attributes. It demands preservation of the functional role of the object in the ecosystem of enjoyment, so that the alteration does not become a technique for extracting or normalizing losses.

This formulation is especially suited to controlling ordinary reconfigurations presented as unavoidable technical routine. Nerfs, removals, reworks, migrations, rule changes, reordering of access, reconfiguration of interfaces and suppression of features are usually narrated as the natural evolution of the environment. In many cases, they may indeed be so. The parameter of functional equivalence is precisely what makes it possible to separate legitimate mutation from degrading mutation. The legally relevant question then ceases to be whether the change occurred within the supplier's general power to update. It becomes whether, after the alteration, the acquired experience remains substantially functionally equivalent to the one that legitimated the entry of the object into the user's sphere of consumption.

This shift has an important dogmatic consequence. It means that the core of the conflict is resolved not by abstract reference to the business autonomy to manage the ecosystem, but by concretely assessing whether enjoyment has been preserved. The analysis must address the impact of the alteration on the role played by the object, on the relational performance of its utility, on its meaningful place in the environment and, when the case requires, on its identity or symbolic dimension. In some ecosystems, the value of the object lies not only in its instrumental efficacy, but also in its form of recognition, visibility, belonging or projection within the experience of use. In these cases, functional equivalence p. 731 may also require preservation of the relational and symbolic density of enjoyment, provided this dimension forms, in an objectively recognizable way, the core of the acquisition.

The parameter thus prevents two opposite distortions. On one side, it sets aside the claim to freeze the environment in a fixed state, as if any mutation automatically constituted non-performance. On the other, it refuses the thesis that every alteration would be legitimated by the simple operational continuity of the system or by the mere nominal survival of the object. Between these extremes, functional equivalence offers an intermediate and institutionally more useful yardstick. It makes it possible to recognize that mutability is compatible with continuous supply, but only as long as it does not destroy the core of enjoyment that gave economic and legal meaning to the acquisition.

This yardstick also structures the legal response to the conflict. When equivalence is broken without adequate restoration, the alteration ceases to be read as ordinary evolution and comes to be treated as a relevant inadequacy of the supply regime. Room then opens for protection aimed not only at residual economic compensation, but at restoring enjoyment in functionally equivalent terms. That is precisely where the centrality of this parameter lies. It serves not only to identify unlawfulness. It also serves to guide remedies. By shifting the focus to the functional core of the acquired experience, it makes it possible to prioritize restoration, equivalent substitution, technical reversal when feasible or other forms of restoration that preserve the practical meaning of the relationship, instead of automatically reducing the injury to later monetization.

Functional value equivalence, therefore, constitutes the material core of the control of mutability in immaterial objects of consumption. Its function is to ensure that the plasticity of digital environments is not turned into a license to empty out, from within, what was economically incorporated into the consumer's experience. Where the alteration substantially preserves the function of the object, there will be legitimate mutability. Where the change relevantly degrades the functional, relational or symbolically constitutive role of enjoyment without compatible restoration, there will be legally controllable inadequacy. It is at this point that the IOC regime proves capable of treating mutation not as a brute fact of technology, but as conduct subject to limits, evidence and normative consequence.
