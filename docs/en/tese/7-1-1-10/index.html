7.1.1.10 On the parameters for remedies suited to the primacy of functional restoration and regime correction

See this section in the PDF

p. 739

The legal response to conflicts involving the Immaterial Object of Consumption cannot be organized from the traditional automatism of converting the injury into pecuniary compensation. This solution, although often necessary, frequently proves insufficient when what is at stake is not only the immediate economic loss, but the degradation, interruption or abusive reconfiguration of a continuous enjoyment, governed and functionally situated within an ecosystem. In relationships of this nature, the mere later payment of a sum of money may leave the harmful mechanism untouched, preserve the architecture that produced the problem and normalize the repetition of the loss as an ordinary cost of the supply model. For this reason, the discipline of remedies must start from another premise. The priority protection, whenever technically and legally feasible, must seek the functional restoration of enjoyment and the correction of the regime that rendered it inadequate.

This primacy does not derive from an abstract preference for specific relief nor from a ritual attachment to the idea of restoration in natura. It derives from the very structure of the relationship. If the consumed utility presents itself as an immaterial experience maintained by continuous governance, the relevant injury tends to bear on access, continuity, integrity, equivalence, intelligibility, history, relational position or the consumer's meaningful insertion in the environment. In these cases, the automatic monetization of the conflict frequently fails to give back what was p. 740 effectively compromised. Money may compensate approximately for certain patrimonial losses, but it does not always restore the practical utility, the functional position or the relational and symbolic dimension of enjoyment. Functional restoration assumes centrality because it corresponds more adequately to the nature of the legal interest affected.

This becomes even more evident in immaterial objects of consumption endowed with high identity, historical or reputational density. In such cases, the value of enjoyment is not limited to the acquisition cost of an item, an account or a feature. It may be linked to a trajectory of use, accumulated history, belonging to a community, recognition internal to the ecosystem, relational insertion and meaningful continuity of the experience. When these elements are affected, protection that simply arbitrates a pecuniary sum runs the risk of treating as equivalent what, in practice, is not. Functional and symbolic restoration then becomes not only a more protective alternative, but a form legally more faithful to the content of the injury.

This parameter thus performs the function of closing the regime. Once the object is recognized, the vulnerability identified, the duties of information and advertising controlled, auditability required, functional equivalence measured, any abusive teleology ascertained and evidence organized in an environment of structural opacity, the decisive question arises as to what to do with the conflict. The answer cannot be neutral with regard to the architecture of the injury. When the problem stems from continuous, repeatable and structural governance, protection must also be conceived in a functional and structural key. This requires going beyond later compensation and considering remedies capable of restoring the experience, interrupting the reiteration of the harmful mechanism and correcting the mode of operation that made the injury ordinary.

Functional restoration, in this context, comprises a set of possible measures, calibrated according to the structure of the compromised enjoyment. It may involve maintenance or restoration of access, reversal or review of sanctions, preservation of history, reactivation of features, substitution by a functionally equivalent position, reversibility of versions when technically possible, procedural corrections of governance, duties of verifiable reasoning, intelligible mechanisms of contestation and other means apt to re-establish, substantially, the utility legitimately incorporated into the relationship. The criterion is not the literal reproduction of the prior state at any cost, but the functionally adequate restitution of the injured experience, at a level compatible with what the consumer had legitimately incorporated into his sphere of enjoyment.

This point is important because it prevents two undue reductions. The first is to treat all specific relief as impracticable merely because the environment is technically complex p. 741 or continuously mutable. Complexity is not equivalent to impossibility. The second is to assume that any restoration would be excessively intrusive upon business freedom. Here too the reasoning needs to be more sober. What is demanded is neither the freezing of the ecosystem nor the submission of every technical decision of the supplier to the individual will of the consumer. It is demanded only that, in the face of a relevant injury to governed enjoyment, the legal response not be automatically content with residual monetization when there are reasonable means of restoring access, functionality, position of use or minimal conditions of intelligibility and contestation. In other words, the architecture of the environment cannot serve as an automatic excuse for the inferiorization of remedies.

The primacy of functional restoration assumes even greater importance when the injury has an architectural character, repeatable and incorporated into the ordinary regime of supply. In these cases, merely compensatory individual protection tends to operate as a manageable cost of the business model, without altering the structure that produces the harm. The problem then ceases to be only the loss suffered by a specific consumer and comes to reside in the permanence of a harmful arrangement capable of indefinitely reproducing the same inadequacy. The parameter, for this reason, opens space for regime correction. This means admitting, when the case reveals a structural pattern of abusiveness, remedies aimed at the reconfiguration of flows, the imposition of procedural duties, the review of sanction mechanisms, the requirement of verifiable reasoning, the creation of intelligible channels of contestation and other measures apt to reach the continuous source of the problem.

This opening is particularly relevant because it reconnects individual protection to the collective and institutional dimension of the IOC regime. In many platform conflicts, the harmful experience of the single consumer is only a localized manifestation of a broader form of inadequate governance. Treating the case exclusively as an isolated event means losing sight of the repeatability of the harm and of the environment's capacity to produce the same injury in series. Regime correction, on the contrary, makes it possible to treat the harm as architectural and not only as an episode. With this, it becomes possible to justify injunctive relief, structural measures, collective measures and even mechanisms of compulsory compliance compatible with open auditability and institutional control of enjoyment. The legal response then comes to act not only on the consequences of the model, but on its mode of operation.

Pecuniary compensation remains, evidently, a legitimate and in many cases indispensable remedy. The parameter formulated here does not eliminate it. It merely places it back in a systematically more precise position. It assumes a residual or complementary nature whenever functional restoration is unfeasible, insufficient or incapable of fully covering the p. 742 losses suffered. In these cases, monetary compensation continues to play an important role, whether to make good material losses or to recognize non-patrimonial damages when present. What is refused is its transformation into an automatic and preferential response in any case, as if the injury to the IOC could always be adequately translated into a figure without consideration for the structure of the compromised enjoyment.

The parameter, therefore, affirms a remedial orientation coherent with the whole preceding path. If the relationship is one of governed enjoyment, the remedy must aim at enjoyment. If the injury is structural, protection must reach the structure. If the loss is functional, the priority response must be functionally restorative. Only thus does the regime of the Immaterial Object of Consumption avoid being reduced to a sophisticated grammar of diagnosis without real force of transformation. The primacy of functional restoration and regime correction prevents consumer protection from limiting itself to pricing harms while preserving intact the machine that produces them. Transforming structural abuses into mere operating expenses is, in practice, to neutralize the Law by the accounting route.
