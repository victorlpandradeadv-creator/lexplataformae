7.2.5 On collective redress under open auditability

See this section in the PDF

Concepts in this section

  • Open auditability

This section in the layers of the site

  • Platforms · Extension of the typology

p. 773

The clearest institutional consequence of the applied typology is the passage from the individually perceived conflict to the injury structurally produced by the supply regime. In platform-governed ecosystems, opacity does not operate merely as an episodic difficulty of proof, nor does mutability appear merely as a scattered contractual inconvenience. When the absence of verifiable trails, the adoption of non-auditable change regimes and the opaque administration of sanctions, priorities and reconfigurations become ordinary elements of the environment, the problem ceases to be adequately understood as a sum of isolated harms. What emerges is a form of injury that projects itself onto the very p. 774 controllability of enjoyment. It is at this point that collective redress ceases to be a mere procedural convenience and imposes itself as a coherent dogmatic consequence of the IOC regime.

The basis of collective redress, here, lies not only in the circumstance that many consumers may be affected by similar facts. This finding, although relevant, would still remain too dependent on a quantitative logic. The central point lies elsewhere. In platform environments, the harmful source may reside in the very design of the supply regime, so that the unity of the problem does not stem primarily from the empirical repetition of episodes, but from the permanence of a single, repeatable and functionally expansive structure producing loss, opacity and asymmetry. The injury, then, is not merely plural. It is architectural. It arises from the way the environment was organized to govern enjoyment, administer the intelligibility of the bond and selectively distribute the conditions of evidence, reaction and restoration.

That is why one may speak, in this context, of architecture harm. The expression does not refer to a simple technical defect of the system nor to a metaphorical category intended to embellish the argument. It refers to an injury produced by the very design of the supply regime, and therefore endowed with sufficient functional unity to justify collective institutional treatment. When the platform structures the environment so as to make relevant changes unverifiable, to obscure the relation between promise and delivery, to hinder reconstruction of sanctions, to volatilize essential records or to govern the experience by criteria whose intelligibility remains selectively concentrated, the injury is not limited to the consumer who first manages to perceive it or litigate it. It affects the very collective possibility of controlling enjoyment. The harm, thus, is not only to the individual result of consumption. It is to the institutional verifiability of supply.

This formulation is important because it prevents two recurrent errors. The first is reducing collective redress to a mechanism for aggregating individual claims, as if its function were only to make the handling of multiple similar event-harms more efficient. The second is inflating the structural discourse without demonstrating to what extent the unity of the problem stems from the very governance of the ecosystem. The parameter adopted here avoids both. Collective redress is justified when the harmful source remains unitary, repeatable and incorporated into the ordinary functioning of the platform. In this case, the problem lies not only in the quantitative repetition of the harm, but in the qualitative stability of the arrangement that produces it. Repeated opacity, persistent absence of history and non-auditable mutability together form a regime defect.

p. 775 This reading follows directly from the centrality of open auditability in the IOC regime. If the controllability of enjoyment depends on minimum institutional intelligibility about changes, sanctions, presentation criteria, distribution mechanisms and other relevant aspects of the governed experience, then the systematic absence of these conditions already constitutes an injury that goes beyond the sphere of the single case. It is not merely a matter of saying that a consumer was left without sufficient evidence. It is a matter of recognizing that the environment was structured to make precarious, at scale, the possibility of reconstructing the bond. The problem, in this scenario, lies in the architecture of supply, and not merely in the bad luck of a specific user. The insufficiency of verifiable trails ceases to be an accidental procedural obstacle and becomes a mode of operation of the ecosystem.

The institutional consequence of this is clear. When the platform organizes supply under recurrent patterns of structural opacity, protection cannot settle for strictly retrospective and individualized remedies. Correction must reach the regime. Collective redress then proves particularly suited to impose injunctive, procedural and analytical measures capable of reintroducing institutional verifiability into the environment. This may involve duties to preserve minimum records, a requirement of verifiable reasoning for materially relevant sanctions, maintenance of an accessible history of relevant changes, explanation of minimum operational criteria of distribution, priority or ranking, and other measures aimed not only at repairing past harms, but at reorganizing the future conditions of controllability of enjoyment.

This corrective vocation is especially important because, in governed ecosystems, the repetition of the injury does not depend on an identical reiteration of external facts. It stems from the permanence of the same private decision structure. An opaque sanctions policy, a non-auditable regime of functional change, an interface logic that dilutes the essential under informational excess, a materially decisive ranking system without usable history or a trust architecture that absorbs and neutralizes contestation before it becomes litigation are all examples of problems whose unity lies in the design, not in the episode. The legal system loses responsiveness when it tries to treat each manifestation of this pattern as an isolated fact. Collective redress, on the contrary, makes it possible to bring the manifestations back to their unitary source.

This does not mean abolishing the role of individual protection. It remains indispensable, above all for the reparation of concretely experienced losses and for the situated reconstruction of certain specific impacts. What is asserted is that, when the litigation reveals a structural pattern of opacity, absence of trails, degrading mutability or non-auditable sanction, the p. 776 adequate institutional response cannot be exhausted on the individual plane. Merely atomized treatment of the conflict tends to produce two bad results. On the one hand, it monetizes episodes without altering the machine that reproduces them. On the other, it transforms the systemic difficulty of proof into the private failure of dispersed consumers, each obliged to face alone an informational regime designed precisely to hinder reconstruction. Collective redress corrects both deviations, because it shifts the focus from the event-harm to the governance that makes it normal.

The notion of architecture harm also helps explain why the indivisibility of the injury does not depend only on a material community of losses, but on the functional unity of the aggressing source. When the platform operates under the same opaque regime for all users placed within a given dynamic of enjoyment, the collective injury may reside precisely in the common subjection to an environment whose verifiability has been structurally compromised. The impact is not homogeneous in all its effects, but it is common in its matrix. All users are placed in an ecosystem that asymmetrically distributes meaning, evidence and capacity for contestation. The collective injury, then, is not limited to the final content of each one's loss. It bears on the very level of institutional controllability of the supply regime.

It is at this point that open auditability turns from a dogmatic standard into a condition of effectiveness of collective redress. Without it, the action of parties with collective standing, administrative protection bodies and regulatory and supervisory structures tends to run into the same opacity that already hinders individual reaction. With it, it becomes possible to transform the requirement of verifiability into the main object of protection, shifting the debate from isolated episodes to the shaping of the environment. Collective redress then comes to operate not only as a response to consummated harms, but as an institutional technology for reintroducing intelligibility, preserving evidence and containing private regimes that remain functionally impenetrable.

If the typical models analyzed demonstrate that the platform governs enjoyment through recurrent mechanisms of visibility, opacity, mutability, sanction and administration of trust, then the institutional response cannot remain confined to the horizon of the single lawsuit. The typology reveals precisely that certain harms are programmatic. Not because all cases are the same, but because the source that produces them remains structurally the same. In these contexts, collective redress does not represent an expansionist deviation from the CDC. It represents the appropriate way of treating injuries whose dogmatic unity is already given by the architecture of supply.

p. 777 The result is a strong institutional conclusion, but a methodologically restrained one. The repetition of structural opacity, the persistent absence of verifiable trails and the adoption of non-auditable regimes of change and sanction naturally lead to structural injuries typical of collective redress because they make the very controllability of enjoyment a legally affected interest. What is protected, then, is not only the patrimony or the individual experience of dispersed consumers, but the institutional integrity of the conditions under which enjoyment can be understood, proven, contested and restored. When the private supply regime compromises these conditions on an architectural scale, the legal response must be equally capable of reaching the architecture. Otherwise, the system merely tallies harms while opacity keeps producing them in silence.
