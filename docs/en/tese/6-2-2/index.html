6.2.2 On the doctrine of digital assets and the regime of the Immaterial Object of Consumption

p. 565

The doctrine of digital assets of Bruno Torquato Zampier Lacerda630 performs an important function because it resolves, with good density, a problem that cannot be circumvented by contractual rhetoric or by nostalgia for tangibility: techno-digital assets are legal goods and do not disappear from the world of Law just because they are incorporeal. Digitally organized information can take on its own utility, become part of the subject's sphere of interests and, for that reason, be treated as a legally relevant incorporeal good, with or without immediate economic content Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 51; p. 57; p. 72)631. This starting point is correct and indispensable. Without it, the debate on continuous digital consumption would be held hostage to the crude fiction according to which everything that cannot be held in the hand would be legally light, dissolvable and disposable by the supplier's mere will.

It is also a real gain of this framework to recognize that, once admitted as goods, these assets become susceptible to appropriation, circulation, enjoyment and protection. They enter the legal world not as conceptual curiosities, but as objects capable of sustaining organized legal positions, including use, enjoyment, disposal and transmission. Incorporeality neither prevents the exercise of faculties typical of ownership, nor turns enjoyment into a gracious tolerance of technique Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 73; p. 82; p. 151)632. This matters a great deal here p. 566 because it sets aside, from the outset, the semantic fraud of the “revocable license” as a formula capable of evaporating everything that has already been economically incorporated into the user's sphere. If there is an identifiable virtual object, associated with the account and enjoyed in the ecosystem, there is legal substance to be taken seriously, even if the platform tries to cover it with a vocabulary of precariousness.

Riot's own contractual clauses help to show this. The company expressly defines “Virtual Content” as a set that includes in-game currency, champions, skins, emotes, accessories and other items associated with the account633, and admits that this content may be obtained not only by classic purchase, but also by earning, reward, gift or other internal dynamics of the ecosystem634. This is useful for two reasons. First, because it shows that the contractual design itself recognizes an identifiable core of enjoyment associated with the account, which dismantles the puerile thesis of the object's legal non-existence. Second, because it shows that the formation of this core is not exhausted in a one-off market exchange, but takes place within a continued dynamic of progression, circulation, reward and participation. The consumed object is already born, there, less as an isolated “digital thing” and more as a position of enjoyment produced and maintained within a governed environment.

The concept of the digital asset renders, further, another useful service by providing an internal typology of the phenomenon. Not every techno-digital asset has the same structure, the same function or the same regime, and the distinction between patrimonial, existential and mixed assets helps to avoid everything being treated as an amorphous mass. This classification makes it possible to better distribute effects of ownership, use, transmission and protection, besides showing that certain digital assets gravitate closer to the patrimonial sphere, others closer to personality, and many combine both p. 567 dimensions Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 56; p. XIV; p. 146)635. Such refinement is useful and does not deserve to be discarded. The problem is that it still organizes the phenomenon mainly on the basis of the asset, whereas the core of the IOC demands a reading oriented by governed enjoyment.

It is here that the thesis makes its inflection. The gain of the civil-law doctrine lies in resolving the problem of the legal status of the techno-digital asset and in blocking the nominalist dissolution of what has already entered the user's sphere. But when the conflict shifts to platform consumption with continued, mutable enjoyment dependent on private governance, the civil-law axis of ownership does not by itself capture the center of the problem. The IOC regime starts from the same recognition of legal status, only it shifts the focus: instead of asking predominantly who “has” the asset, how it circulates or is transmitted, it asks how enjoyment is maintained, modulated, degraded, made intelligible and restorable over time. It is a change of axis, not a denial of the previous step.

It is worth being explicit about the nature of this change of axis, because it is frequently interpreted, without due attention, as a simple shift of library shelf. The project of the doctrine of digital assets is dogmatically delimited and responds to a recognizable problem. It operates within civil law and confronts, in the foreground, the silence of the Brazilian legal order about an entire class of assets that exists, circulates, produces economic and affective effects, but that found no express regime of existence, ownership, transmission and extinction. Lacerda fills this silence with functional taxonomy and classificatory rigor, and that is why the work has become a canonical reference for digital inheritance, for the succession of digital assets and for the civil-law protection of the subject's digital estate. The service rendered is real and cannot be treated as a mere disposable preparatory stage. p. 568 When this framework is summoned to answer what it set out to answer, it answers well.

The present thesis, however, operates in a different domain and answers a different problem. It does not confront the silence of the Civil Code on the status of the digital asset, but the opacity of the Consumer Protection Code on the regime of the immaterial product under continued and governed enjoyment. This shift of domain is not a detail of disciplinary organization. It entails a structuring dogmatic consequence, because civil law regulates relationships between formally equal subjects and consumer law regulates structurally asymmetric relationships under mandatory public order. Where the civil-law doctrine asks whose the good is, how it circulates and how it is extinguished, the IOC regime asks how the promised utility is maintained, modulated, degraded and restorable over time. The questions are not opposed. They inhabit distinct layers of the phenomenon and call for correspondingly distinct techniques of attribution.

The difference between these layers can be described more sharply by means of a temporal distinction. The doctrine of digital assets works with a predominantly static ontology of the asset. The good exists, is classifiable, can have an owner, can be transmitted, can be extinguished. There is, so to speak, a photograph of the asset, and the pertinent legal faculties are organized on that photograph. The IOC regime, in turn, works with a processual ontology of enjoyment. The immaterial object of consumption does not admit a static photograph because its identity lies not in a synchronic cut, but in a diachronic course of maintenance, updating, curation and administration. It is, in a legally relevant sense, what is continuously made of it within the environment that produces it. That is why the passage from the civil-law grammar to the consumer-law grammar is not merely a change of applicable code. It is the recognition that the time of performance is part of the very existence of the consumed object.

This temporal difference explains, in part, why the axis of the conflict also shifts. In the typical scenarios faced by the doctrine of digital assets, the controversy gravitates around ownership, circulation and extinction. One asks who the owner is, whether the good can be inherited, whether the platform can block it after death, whether contractual clauses can bar disposal inter vivos or causa mortis. These are relevant conflicts and the civil-law framework faces them with good equipment. In the typical scenarios faced by the IOC regime, however, the controversy gravitates around enjoyment, modulation and functional integrity. One asks whether the object continues to be what it promised to be, whether the unilateral alteration preserved sufficient functional equivalence, whether the degradation produced by an ordinary update exceeded a legally tolerable margin, whether the opacity of the environment allows the p. 569 consumer to institutionally reconstruct what was altered. These are questions that the civil-law framework can formulate, but has no natural tool to answer, because its instruments were forged for another type of litigation.

It is also worth situating the difference between the relevant subjects in the two constructions. For the doctrine of digital assets, the legally central subject is the owner of the digital asset, frequently the user as holder of a digital estate whose preservation, transmission or destination is under discussion. The vulnerability that matters in this framework is, as a rule, the existential vulnerability linked to the owner's personality or the patrimonial vulnerability linked to their holdings. For the IOC regime, the legally central subject is the consumer under a platform, alive, active and in a continuous relationship with a supplier that concentrates architecture, curation, enforcement and the production of meaning. The vulnerability that matters here is contemporary factual vulnerability, of which semiotic vulnerability is a current specification, and it is not to be confused with any of the figures traditionally worked by the civil-law framework. The two vulnerabilities coexist in digital environments, but call for different protection techniques and activate distinct normative sources.

It is further worth noting, in homage to methodological honesty, that these differences do not make the doctrine of digital assets irrelevant to the present work. On the contrary, they make visible the precision with which that doctrine must be summoned. Lacerda blocks, with a force that could hardly be reached by any other route, the semantic fraud that would seek to dissolve the digital asset into a mere permission of use revocable at any time. This block is irreplaceable, because without it the platform would win the controversy by private dictionary before it was even formulated as a legal controversy. The doctrine of digital assets builds, in other words, the floor from which the debate on regime becomes possible. If the asset is legally light, dissolvable by mere contractual nomenclature and disposable by the supplier's unilateral will, any discussion of continued enjoyment, functional equivalence and auditability becomes idle talk about something whose legal existence has not even been stabilized. Lacerda stabilizes that existence. From this point the present thesis departs.

The methodological relationship between the two constructions can then be summarized in terms that refuse both artificial competition and simulated continuity. The doctrine of digital assets answers, with rigor, the question about the techno-digital asset's being a good. The IOC regime answers, with its own instruments, the question about the immaterial object of consumption's continuing to be enjoyable, in legally equivalent terms, within platform ecosystems. One builds the legal status of the asset; the other builds the legal regime of its continuous governance. One resolves the entry of the digital object into the world of law; the other p. 570 resolves the permanence of the promised utility within a consumer relationship whose performance is not exhausted in the initial act. That is why the thesis assumes, instead of simulating overcoming, the prior civil-law step and repositions it in its correct function. Lacerda provides the premise of the asset's legal existence. The IOC provides the regime of supply of the enjoyment that this asset, now legally recognized, sustains over time.

This synthesis allows, finally, a clear sizing of the dogmatic place that the doctrine of digital assets occupies in the edifice of this thesis. It is not a parallel track, nor a competing track, nor a prior track in a chronological sense that needed to be surpassed. It is a contiguous and operationally complementary track, which faces a layer of the phenomenon distinct from the one on which the present work concentrates and which, by that distinction, makes the advance proposed here possible. Assuming this contiguity, instead of feigning it as competition, is an elementary requirement of dogmatic honesty.

Denying a contribution that structures the very possibility of the debate would, moreover, be rhetorical self-defeat, because it would weaken the floor from which the argument of governed enjoyment becomes intelligible. Recognizing the contribution in its fair measure, and demarcating precisely where the inflection is necessary, is what allows the IOC regime to present itself as what it in fact is: an internal development of the consumer-law system based on the immaterial product already contained in art. 3, paragraph 1, of the Consumer Protection Code, built on a civil-law floor that seriously recognizes the legal existence of the digital asset and that, at the same time, leaves open the problem whose answer constitutes the object of this thesis.

An additional observation must be made before the comparison advances, because it concerns the way in which the doctrine of digital assets may be applied by third parties in concrete cases involving immaterial objects of consumption. The tripartite typology that distinguishes patrimonial, existential and mixed digital assets was formulated to answer precisely problems of ownership, circulation and succession destination, and performs that role with recognized competence.

There is, however, a non-negligible operational risk when this same typology is summoned, without mediation, to resolve consumer controversies in platform environments. The risk is that the interpreter, faced with an object such as a skin, a champion, a cosmetic item, the ecosystem's internal currency or equivalent content associated with the account, immediately asks whether the asset is patrimonial, existential or mixed, and organizes the whole controversy around that classification as if it sufficed to extract the applicable legal regime.

p. 571 The question, even if technically well formulated on the civil-law plane, dispenses with the consumer-law microsystem before it is even summoned, because the taxonomy at play there was forged to answer what the asset is in itself, and not what the governed enjoyment made of it within a continued consumer relationship is. The practical effect of this elision is problematic. The case receives a civil-law framing coherent in its own logic, but loses the platform's governance layer, which is precisely where the controversy, in cases of continuous digital consumption, usually lies.

It is therefore worth recording the methodological position adopted by this thesis. The patrimonial, existential and mixed taxonomy, considered in itself, is neither discarded nor demonized. It remains a useful instrument when the conflict is effectively organized around ownership, succession transmission, post mortem destination or inter vivos disposal of the digital asset, cases in which the civil-law floor offers a technically adequate answer. The point, however, is that applying this taxonomy to platform consumer controversies demands a prior methodological step, and that step is verifying that the object in question is not merely a digital asset generically considered, but an Immaterial Object of Consumption under governed enjoyment.

When this qualification is affirmed, the pertinent initial question ceases to be about the patrimonial or existential nature of the asset and becomes one about the regime of the enjoyment that this asset sustains within the environment that produces it. This inversion of the order of questions does not annul the civil-law typology, but puts it back in its proper function, preventing the classification of the asset from ending up improperly absorbing the entirety of the case's legal framing and from neutralizing the application of the public-order protections that the consumer-law microsystem summons when the digital object appears as an object of consumption in an environment continuously governed by a supplier.

This shift matters because, on continuous consumption platforms, governance does not act externally on an already complete digital good. It is part of the object's very legally relevant utility. Maintenance, updating, curation, enforcement, parameterization and intelligibility of the environment are not lateral vicissitudes of a fully given thing; they are constitutive dimensions of enjoyment. That is why the IOC is formulated as a concretization of the CDC's immaterial product, and not as a simple static “digital thing” anchored in the Civil Code's book of goods636. The civil-law grammar serves to validate the p. 572 legal existence of the asset; it is not enough to exhaust the consumer-law syntax of a consumption whose utility only stabilizes through continuous private administration.

This is the first relevant limit of the civil-law framework. It grasps more sharply events of suppression, blocking, account termination, unavailability and transmission. It does very well the work of showing that the digital object is not mist. The problem is that, in ecosystems of governed enjoyment, a decisive portion of the conflict does not take the form of an abrupt withdrawal of the object, but of progressive functional erosion through ordinary modulation of the environment. The user continues, in appearance, to have access to the object; only its utility may be lowered by updates, balancing, reclassifications, interface changes, alterations of equivalence and continuous reorganizations of the regime of use. The nominal permanence of the asset, or even of the account, is not enough to rule out a defect. The conflict ceases to be only about existence or availability and becomes one about the substantial preservation of the function enjoyed.

The IOC category gains explanatory advantage by allowing continued maintenance, modulation and administration to be treated as part of the consumer performance itself, and not as accidents external to an already formed good. This opens space for two standards that the purely civil-law framework sees less sharply: functional value equivalence, as a criterion for gauging a defect when utility is lowered without formal suppression, and open auditability, as a minimum requirement of controllability over relevant alterations in the supply regime. In other words, the IOC does not dispute with the doctrine of digital assets the problem of “being a good”; it confronts the problem of “continuing to be enjoyable in a legally equivalent way”.

The second limit lies in the very idea of vulnerability. The civil-law framework tends to work well with technical hyposufficiency, classic informational asymmetry, ownership, access and availability. But, in governed environments, the consumer's vulnerability goes beyond that. It is also semiotic. The perception of loss, of degradation and of the relevance of the downgrade depends on signs, interfaces, metrics, rarities, narratives of prestige, showcases and framings controlled by the supplier itself.

The problem is not merely knowing less about the internal functioning of the system, but depending on the platform's mediation to form expectations, measure value, perceive functional erosion and name what was actually lost. Where the platform controls not only the object, but also the references by which the loss can be perceived and contested, vulnerability does not fit entirely within the classic schemes of informational deficit.

p. 573 That is also why the critique of the rhetoric of the “revocable license” remains valuable, but not sufficient. The doctrine of digital assets is right to block the corporate attempt to requalify, by simple contractual nominalism, objects already integrated into the user's sphere as permissions dissolvable by unilateral will. When there is actual economic outlay or relevant incorporation of utility into the subject's sphere, the language of the license is not enough to erase the legal density of the asset, nor to neutralize the application of public-order norms Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 124; p. 193)637. This block is fundamental. Without it, the platform would always win by private dictionary. But, even after barring this trick, the central problem of the IOC still remains: how to control, within an enjoyment that continues to exist, the ordinary mutations that corrode its functional and symbolic integrity?

The IOC regime recognizes that abusiveness is not necessarily exhausted in a clause or in an event of ostensive blocking. In certain cases, it is incorporated into the very design of the supply, when the architecture organizes inducement, extraction and normalization of losses as a principle of operation. The doctrine of digital assets helps a great deal in seeing contractual nullities, arbitrary blocks and suppressions of assets. The IOC incorporates this layer, but adds another: the reading of architecture as conduct and of abusive teleology as a design defect of the regime of governed enjoyment. This makes it possible to confront not only abrupt withdrawal, but also administered deterioration.

That is why the function of this item is one of basis and contrast, not of a competing track. The doctrine of digital assets sustains the crucial premise that digital assets are legal goods, and contractual precariousness is not the user's inevitable fate. At the same time, it shows its own limits when the problem ceases to be only ownership, transmission and availability and becomes continuous governance of enjoyment. The IOC regime takes on the civil-law gain, refuses the straw man of the legal non-existence of the digital and repositions the debate on the ground p. 574 that matters. It seeks the protection of using, the control of functional integrity and, in certain cases, the protection of being, whenever enjoyment incorporates the symbolic identity and biographical continuity of the consumer user.

This consequence is decisive for the present item, because it sets aside from the outset the fantasy that the digital asset would be legally light just because it cannot be held in the hand. If the owner can delete it, assign it, encumber it or transfer it, then one is already facing a good with sufficient density to support legal positions typical of ownership. The enjoyment of the digital asset is not, therefore, a mere gracious tolerance of technique; it can be legally organized and protected, including by assigning use to third parties as a corollary of the very exercise of the corresponding subjective right Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 82). This point is especially important because it strengthens the argument without appealing to terminological magic. The civil-law doctrine of digital assets forcefully resolves the problem of the asset's aptitude to form part of an estate, to be enjoyed, to circulate in transactions and to receive legal protection. The insufficiency would not lie in this framework's inability to recognize the asset, but in the fact that, even while recognizing appropriation and circulation, it does not exhaust the conflicts of continuous enjoyment, unilateral mutability and functional governance in a platform environment Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 151). ↩

The usefulness of the doctrine of digital assets also lies in offering an internal criterion for organizing the phenomenon, preventing all techno-digital reality from being treated as an amorphous mass. Not every digital asset has the same structure, the same function or the same regime. There are assets of clearly patrimonial content, there are assets of an existential character, and there are cases in which both dimensions present themselves in combined form. This distinction matters because it makes it possible to distribute with greater precision the legal effects of ownership, use, transmission and protection Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 56). This classificatory refinement is one of the most important gains of the civil-law framework, because it prevents the debate from being stuck in soft generalities. Knowing whether the asset is patrimonial, existential or mixed is not academic-taxonomy fussiness; it is what allows circulation, succession, the exercise of private autonomy and the protection of goods linked to the owner's personal sphere to be adequately sized. The very reception of this criterion in proposals for legislative updating shows that it is not a mere ornamental doctrinal invention, but an effectively useful tool of legal work Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. XIV). This gain needs to be honestly recognized because it strengthens, and does not weaken, the critical passage of the thesis. The doctrine of digital assets classifies the asset well, intelligently distinguishes its species and provides a safe compass for the discipline of ownership and of its circulation. The limit appears at another point: even with a good typology of the asset, the framework still does not fully resolve the problems that arise when the conflict ceases to revolve only around ownership and comes to fall on continuous enjoyment, governed mutability and the functional integrity of the experience consumed on a platform Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 146). ↩

Their function, here, is merely structural: to validate the use of the civil-law grammar of goods to recognize a minimum legal density in skins, virtual currencies and digital items, without shifting the dogmatic axis to pure civilism and without taking from the CDC the leading role in understanding and protecting the IOC. ↩
  8. The contractual language of the precarious or revocable license is not enough, by itself, to dissolve the legal density of a digital asset already incorporated into the user's sphere. When there is actual economic outlay for the acquisition of digital content or utilities, the mere corporate requalification of the relationship as a “mere license” cannot operate as a semantic trick capable of disfiguring the existence of a digital estate. In other words, it is not because the platform calls it a license that Law is obliged to pretend that it never had patrimonial consistency Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 124). The doctrine of digital assets makes it possible to state, seriously, that music libraries, video libraries, virtual libraries and other similar collections may form part of the user's estate, including for purposes of succession transmission, despite corporate rhetoric to the contrary. The legal density of the good's incorporation into the subject's sphere does not disappear by private decree Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 124). The same logic projects onto clauses that seek to neutralize access to, use of or disposal of these assets in terms incompatible with public-order norms. The insertion of provisions that absolutely prohibit access to or the legal destination of the asset may lead to nullity when it collides with the general guidelines of the system, including those relating to the prohibition of advance waiver of rights in adhesion contracts Lacerda(Lacerda, 2024, p. 193). The value of this approach, here, is central: the doctrine of digital assets blocks well the nominalist dissolution of the asset. What the thesis will need to show later is that this block, although indispensable, still does not exhaust the problem when the enjoyment of the object is continuously governed, reconfigured and strained by the platform. ↩
