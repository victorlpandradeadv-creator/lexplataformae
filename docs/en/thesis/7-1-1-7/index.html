7.1.1.7 On the parameters of abusive teleology and the unlawful act of architecture as conduct

p. 731

The controllability of the Immaterial Object of Consumption requires recognizing that not all relevant abusiveness manifests itself in the classical form of an isolated clause, a singular event or a p. 732 one-off failure of performance. In platform-governed ecosystems, there are cases in which exploitation does not stem from an episodic deviation of the system, but from the very operating program that organizes enjoyment. In these cases, the injury cannot be adequately grasped by decomposing the conflict into autonomous incidents, because it is precisely the coordination between multiple elements of the environment that makes its exploitative purpose knowable. When inducement, extraction and normalization of losses are stably incorporated into the design of the ecosystem, the problem ceases to be merely one of fragmentary non-performance and comes to constitute a teleological unlawful act of architecture.

Abusive teleology thus describes a situation in which the practical purpose of the supply arrangement departs from legally tolerable limits and comes to direct the environment toward the structural exploitation of vulnerability, toward the relevant obscuring of enjoyment or toward the recurring conversion of mutability into a technology of capture. It is not a matter of investigating the subjective psychological intention of business agents, nor of moralizing every form of monetization, persuasion or private governance. The focus lies on the functional purpose objectively inscribed in the arrangement, as revealed by its replicability, its internal coherence and its repeated impact on the consumer's experience. What is controlled is not an inner will, but a recognizable operating program.

This formulation is important because it prevents two impoverishing reductions. The first consists in dispersing the conflict into apparently modest micro-events, each of which is insufficient, in isolation, to reveal the gravity of the pattern. The second consists in turning the critique of architecture into an abstract moral judgment against business activity, losing institutional traction. The parameter of abusive teleology avoids both extremes. It makes it possible to identify cases in which micro-frictions, micro-losses, micro-opacities, discreet shifts of information, asymmetric reversibilities, manufactured urgencies, artificial scarcities, elastic equivalences and selective regimes of difficulty operate in a coordinated manner, forming a program of extraction that, seen as a whole, becomes legally relevant. Taken in isolation, such elements may seem merely tolerable rough edges of the environment. Structurally articulated, they reveal a mode of governance oriented toward the ordinary exploitation of enjoyment.

The core of the parameter lies in treating architecture as conduct. As long as the design of the environment is perceived only as a neutral technical setting, it will remain outside the main field of legal attribution. Conversely, once it is recognized that architecture organizes access, visibility, timing, friction, irreversibility, contestation, salience and modulation of the experience, it becomes possible to understand it as the supplier's way of acting. The platform p. 733 no longer appears only as the abstract holder of infrastructure or as the drafter of contractual documents. It appears as an agent that shapes, by design, the material conditions in which choice occurs, loss is normalized, reaction is hindered and extraction is stabilized. It is in this sense that the teleological unlawful act of architecture can be qualified as legally attributable conduct.

The category also prevents technical necessities from being accepted, automatically, as self-evident justifications for any arrangement of enjoyment. Technique, in environments of this nature, is not external to power. It is one of its privileged forms of exercise. For this reason, claims of efficiency, optimization, balance of the system, operational convenience or the need for updating are not enough, by themselves, to neutralize control. Whenever the operation of the environment reveals a structure oriented to capturing vulnerability, obscuring the intelligibility of the relationship, naturalizing functional degradation or imposing asymmetric losses as an ordinary routine of the experience, the analysis must go beyond the rhetoric of technical necessity and inquire into the practical purpose of the arrangement. What matters is not only whether the system can be technically explained, but whether its configuration is legally compatible with good faith, transparency, balance and the protection of the enjoyment legitimately incorporated into the relationship.

Abusive teleology takes on special relevance when exploitation depends not on one major harmful act, but on the repetition of small pressures, small losses and small asymmetries that, accumulated, shape the normality of the environment. This is one of its most important marks. Many exploitative architectures do not produce a spectacular event-harm. They produce ordinary wear, diffuse loss, selective difficulty, progressive obscuring and silent reconfiguration of the experience. Law sometimes tends to underestimate these forms of injury because it was historically better trained to react to delimited events than to operating programs. The parameter proposed here corrects this deficiency by offering a language to capture abusiveness not as an accident, but as a structural vector of supply.

The dogmatic consequence of this is significant. Once abusive teleology is recognized, the legal response must not be limited to the later repair of individual episodes. What opens up is the possibility of protection aimed at correcting the operating regime itself. If the abusiveness lies in the program, it is not enough to treat its isolated effects. It becomes appropriate to consider injunctive relief, reconfiguration of flows, review of selective frictions, duties to give reasons, more symmetrical mechanisms of reversibility, adjustments of informational visibility and other measures capable of reaching architecture as the p. 734 continuous source of the problem. The parameter, therefore, broadens the remedial horizon of the IOC regime, because it shifts the focus from the event-harm to the structure that produces and reproduces it.

This broadening, however, does not dissolve rigor. The attribution of abusive teleology cannot arise from vague discomfort with the business model or from intuitive criticism of the existence of monetization, engagement or curation. It requires demonstrating an objectively recognizable functional purpose, revealed by the articulation between governance mechanisms, the vulnerability at stake, the pattern of opacity, the frictions produced and relevant effects on the consumer's practical autonomy and enjoyment. It is this requirement of a chain of reasoning that prevents the trivialization of the category and preserves its institutional usefulness. The parameter does not serve to turn every intense architecture into an unlawful act. It serves to identify when the intensity of design ceases to be merely organizational and comes to structure exploitation incompatible with the limits of the consumer relationship.

For this reason, abusive teleology functions as a closing category of the regime of controllability. Once the object has been qualified, the vulnerability identified, the duties of information and advertising assessed, auditability required and functional equivalence tested, it becomes possible to ask whether the environment as a whole reveals only legitimate governance or whether it already operates as a program of exploitation incorporated into enjoyment itself. When the answer is the latter, architecture ceases to be a backdrop and becomes an unlawful act. It is at this moment that Law ceases to look only at what the platform says or at what it does in isolated episodes, and begins to look at what it builds as a regime.
