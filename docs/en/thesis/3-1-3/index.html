3.1.3 Technique incorporated into routines and the desymbolization of experience

p. 104

In digital environments of continued enjoyment, the relevant loss is not reduced to the alteration of a digital object, but reaches the field of meaning that sustained its enjoyment. A rare cosmetic item or a reconfigured character are valuable because they operate as a legible social sign, a marker of identity and a point of belonging within a proprietary grammar. When an update alters appearance, effects, perceived rarity or economic insertion, the compatibility between experience, narrative and recognition may come undone. The promise of distinction remains nominally in the account, but no longer finds, in the world of the game, the fit that gave value to the investment of time, money and attention.

It is at this point that Vanderburg's contribution59 becomes useful. Technique does not appear merely as a system external to lived experience, but as an everyday reorganization of experience, capable of corroding the symbolic density that makes consumption meaningful. In the illustrative scenario adopted, the player's frustration does not stem merely from the modification of an attribute, but from the disarticulation of the grammar of recognition that gave meaning to the initial p. 105 investment. The unilateral alteration60 of appearance, perceived rarity, social utility of the cosmetic or functional identity of the character thus functions as a corrosion of the symbolic value incorporated into enjoyment.

What is consumed, in this type of environment, is not only technical function, but belonging, prestige and recognition mediated by signs. When the platform rewrites these signs, the nominal permanence of the object in the account may coexist with the emptying of what it signified and delivered. Vanderburg's usefulness lies precisely in allowing this frustration to be read not as an error of technical performance, but as a practical desymbolization of the acquired good.

For Vanderburg, the fragmentation of modern experience begins in the very way knowledge and practice are organized. The scheme of disciplinary delegation distributes physical, biological, social and economic phenomena to distinct specialists; it works when one category of phenomena predominates, but becomes inadequate when several categories contribute simultaneously to the problem analyzed Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2011, p. 3). This structure helps explain why modern civilization can succeed in scientific and technical undertakings and yet fail when faced with communities and ecosystems, whose understanding requires the integration of heterogeneous dimensions Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2011, p. 3).

His reading does not arise in a vacuum. Here there is a clear continuity between Ellul, Postman and Vanderburg, and it is this continuity that gives unity to the diagnosis. Ellul offers the understanding of technique as a practical rationality that becomes autonomous and reorganizes social life, while Postman makes explicit how communicational mediations reorder meaning and degrade the cultural density of experience. Vanderburg, in turn, shows how this movement is incorporated into routines and translates into fragmentation and desymbolization of lived experience. For this reason, although there is direct continuity with the Ellulian matrix, the axis of the study in Vanderburg is the formulation of the mechanism by which technique comes to operate from within everyday experience.

This framing matters to the legal problem because it allows the injury to be described as the degradation of an enjoyment bundle whose core is semiotic and relational, and not as mere subjective frustration. When the platform governs the repertoire of signs, the criteria of visibility and the routes of internal comparison, it also governs the conditions of p. 106 verifiability of what was promised and of what was delivered. The consequence is that the conflict over a system change ceases to be a dispute about taste or preference and becomes a dispute about controllability. It is a matter of defining which parameters make it possible to identify, with minimal intersubjectivity, when a technical alteration relevantly downgrades an acquired immaterial good.

The scenario becomes even more critical when everyday life comes to be reorganized by informational technologies. Vanderburg (2011, p. 21) argues that computational information and communication technologies strengthen the technical order to the detriment of the cultural and natural orders, with direct effects on the way experience is organized and interpreted. Even when the computer operates with symbols, it treats them indifferently to the dialectical context that gives them meaning, which contributes to emptying their symbolic capacity. Moreover, computer-mediated activities tend to require reconfiguration by a technical division of labor, since practice ends up reorganized to fit the demands of the informational apparatus itself Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2011, p. 21).

In contexts of massive mediation by media and social networks, the displacement of meaning toward instrumental uses appears clearly when Vanderburg observes that, under these conditions, the meaning of words61 comes to be profoundly affected by the fact that they operate as “tools”, and not as bearers of meaning. The point is not merely that language becomes simplified. It changes function, since it ceases to integrate experiences into a shared symbolic web and comes to circulate as a device of immediate action, oriented by response, adjustment and performative adequacy.

When the sign loses density of context and comes to be valued by its use, the interpretive horizon shrinks, and the subject becomes more dependent on external mediations to attribute relevance to what they live and consume. This transformation directly concerns digital consumption because it reduces the margin for critical reading of environments mediated by signs, precisely those in which offers, interfaces and promises are presented as if they were natural to the world Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2016, p. 68).

Vanderburg's contribution matters here for making explicit a dimension frequently undertheorized in debates on digital consumption. It concerns how technique, by being incorporated into routines and into the ordinary mediations of life, displaces experience from an integrated horizon of meaning to a sequence of fragmented operations, increasingly dependent on technical-organizational systems. Desymbolization, in this framework, is not an p. 107 abstract cultural effect, but a way of life in which the interpretation of the world, including the digital world, comes to occur under impoverished conditions of contextualization, which reduces the capacity for critique, for intuitive auditing and for semiotic resistance in the face of offers, interfaces and promises.

This displacement makes it possible to name a specific, useful operational effect, symbolic captivity. It is the condition in which the repertoire of signs supplied and administered by the platform comes to function as the predominant grammar for interpreting value, belonging and the user's very social world, reducing the routes of external comparison and the capacity to re-anchor meaning outside the ecosystem. What is described here is not a psychological state of the consumer, but an architecture of mediation that selects, ranks and repeats signs, giving them practical centrality through mechanisms of visibility, reward and obsolescence. When experience is organized under this regime, promise and delivery no longer depend only on what is said and come to depend on what is made present, relevant and verifiable, which prepares the reading of continuous consumption as semiotic governance.

In the empirical laboratory referred to, symbolic captivity appears when the value of the item and of the character is recalculated exclusively by the signals that the platform itself controls, including after the unilateral alteration. The consumer has no way to “take the good outside” nor to re-anchor its meaning in an external market or grammar. All that remains is to accept the new official interpretation of the object, because it is this interpretation that organizes reputation, belonging and social legibility in the ecosystem. This helps explain why the player's frustration is not collateral noise, but a structural symptom of a regime of consumption in which the platform administers, at the same time, the promise, the experience and the criteria for validating value.

Vanderburg's contribution is especially useful for articulating three movements. First, that of describing and recognizing cultural desymbolization as the loss of the capacity to integrate experiences into a meaningful world62. Second, to indicate the role of technique and p. 108 of the entertainment industry in this process. And third, to show how advertising exploits emotional and identity vulnerabilities. Such elements directly concern digital consumption, in which immaterial objects are offered as a symbolic response to lacks that the techno-media order itself helps produce.

As regards cultural desymbolization, Vanderburg describes a process set in motion when living conditions restrict corporeality, participation, commitment and freedom, weakening the practical background that sustains symbolization Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2011, p. 77). It is not a matter of an abstract absence of symbols, but of the deterioration of the concrete conditions that make it possible to attribute meaning to lived experience, including through the bodily dimension of experience Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2011, p. 77). When this fabric is torn, there may be a loss of integrity of the self in its web of relations, accompanied by overload, powerlessness and helplessness Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2011, p. 76). The reorganization of life by demands of efficiency, control and fragmentation of tasks narrows precisely the space of participation and commitment that gives grounding to symbolization.

The corrosion of the symbolic context intensifies when informational technologies come to organize activities and experiences. In order to operate, they convert lived experiences and signs into manipulable, separable and comparable units, treated as processing inputs. In this conversion, what was ambiguous, situated and relational loses thickness and p. 109 tends to be reduced to an object of calculation. Meaning shifts from the living bond with experience to the efficiency of the procedure. The effect is not merely the adoption of new instruments, but the alteration of the conditions of interpretation, since experience reaches the subject already filtered by a logic that privileges performance, result and control. It is on this ground that desymbolization becomes a structural diagnosis: technically organized life hinders the sustaining of interpretive coherence because it restricts, reorganizes and fragments the contexts of participation.

Vanderburg argues that desymbolization is not born with the internet nor with mass culture. It runs through human history, with distinct intensities, whenever collective life loses the capacity to preserve and transmit a common horizon of meaning. In traditional societies, this horizon operated as an embodied map, internalized in the brain-mind organization, with greater symbolic elaboration in the domains in which the person effectively participated in the way of life and lesser elaboration where that participation was reduced. For this reason, traditions could function as a kind of “mental map”, with dense symbolic connections in the socially lived spheres Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2016, p. 11). What changes in technical modernity is the generalization of the process, because technical mediation ceases to operate as a set of instruments and comes to configure the very milieu of life, weakening the link between experience, narrative and shared world.

Desymbolization also corrodes the everyday capacity to verify and order lived experience. When experiences cease to be articulated with one another and with a cultural background capable of giving them resolution, the subject loses the conditions to attribute weight, hierarchy and relevance to what happens. The drift is not merely cognitive: the lack of meaning turns into practical powerlessness, weakening of bonds and diffuse anxiety. It is on this ground that technical supplements of orientation and belonging settle in with ease, coming to operate as prostheses of interpretation.

As an illustrative device, desymbolization may be compared to the progressive reduction of the chromatic repertoire available for forming an image, that is, to the loss of the capacity to see colors. Taking colors not as signs, but as structural elements of composition, one can observe that an image produced only in black and white has a smaller field of combinations than an image composed of gradations of gray; the latter, in turn, has a smaller field of possibilities than an image open to a broader spectrum of colors.

In other words, the potential structural complexity of the image is directly proportional to the number of colors available for its formation. Increasing the available repertoire broadens p. 110 compositional complexity, not because every colored image is symbolically superior, but because there are more possible differences from which to form relations. Likewise, a symbolically dense culture offers a larger repertoire of connections between experience, narrative, practice and belonging. Desymbolization impoverishes this field of possibilities: the world can still be perceived, but it has fewer mediations through which to be articulated as a world endowed with coherence.

Vanderburg defines desymbolization as the corrosion of the cultural capacity to organize human experience dialectically, that is, to maintain relations of meaning that connect elements of the world and give them compatibility Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2011, p. 17). The lower the levels of desymbolization, the greater the tendency of a culture to produce creations compatible with one another, whereas high levels favor a world in which things lose fit and coherence Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2011, p. 17). The process intensifies when the disciplinary technical approach replaces the symbolic cultural approach, which separates the technical order from the cultural order built by experience and causes expertise to drift away from human life through layers of abstraction Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2011, p. 13-14). As a subjective and social effect, this separation reconfigures roles and fractures the unity of lived life, producing divided subjects and alienated cultures under increasingly diffuse interpretive horizons Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2011, p. 88; p. 107).

The link between desymbolization and digital consumption runs through the way technique is incorporated into routines. In Vanderburg, this movement displaces internalized symbolic orientation and produces growing dependence on external mediations. On the plane of practical rationality, the author describes the passage to goal-oriented behavior, in which the context considered is restricted, since everything not directly relevant to the objective tends to be excluded, separating the relevant from the marginal and eliminating what comes to be held as irrelevant Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2011, p. 179-180). With the loss of tradition as background, brain-mind organizations cease to function as sufficient mental maps to orient life, and culture comes to operate as a radar, tracking what happens in the world and how people respond in order to adjust conduct, fit in and belong Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2011, p. 204).

If culture comes to operate as a radar, the question ceases to be merely the erosion of inherited symbols and comes to involve the engineering of the signals that replace this background. In the reference ecosystem, this displacement appears when the platform manufactures ready-made frames of meaning and distributes them as consumable novelty. The alternative “universes” import external cultural grammars, repackage them in their own vocabulary and offer the consumer not only variations of appearance, but complete cultural p. 111 framings, with aesthetics, narrative and a promise of belonging. Desire comes to be oriented by short, recombinable and replaceable cycles of meaning.

Riot itself describes this functioning when it treats skins as interpretations of alternative universes, capable of moving the champion to “a new world” and reinscribing the experience under the question “what if?”63 Riot Games(Riot Games, 2017c). The gesture is decisive for the reading of Vanderburg: in an environment that depends on external signals to orient conduct and belonging, the alternative universe functions as a provisional map of meaning. It reactivates interest in characters without requiring dense tradition or cultural continuity. The consequence is an economy of meaning on demand, in which cultural trend becomes raw material and alternative world becomes packaging.

Symbolic recycling, in this ecosystem, is not limited to aesthetics, because it becomes cosmology. The Pulsefire64 universe is revealing because its narrative core organizes transit between timelines and parallel realities, operating as a particularly revealing piece of this arrangement. When a universe is built around the crossing between times and worlds, the multiplication of versions ceases to seem like noise and comes to produce internal coherence. The multiverse, here, is not merely lore, but a cultural technology for legitimizing the catalog. It naturalizes the platform's restaging of the same characters under diverse signs, often imported from outside, without the repetition seeming mere repetition. Riot itself thickens this universe through narrative events distributed across the service's calendar Riot Games(Riot Games, 2020a).

The same mechanism appears in universes that import external cultural grammars in an organized way. Star Guardian converts the repertoire of Japanese magical p. 112 girls65 animations into an internal universe, with its own mythology, language of destiny, light and mission, presenting an aesthetic recognizable by the public without needing to originate from Riot Games(Riot Games, [202-?]a). Odyssey translates the fictional genre of space opera66 into a playable and consumable universe, mobilizing empire, frontier and space adventure as a frame that reorients the reading of the characters. The same cast becomes compatible with a new cultural grammar without the technical engine of the service needing to change in the same proportion Riot Games(Riot Games, [202-?]c). In both cases67, the platform does not merely follow culture; it operationalizes it as a series, ready for return, expansion and recombination.

This engineering of meaning is coupled to events as a practical form of circulation of the symbolic. Riot describes events as packages that bring together things to do, things to discover such as lore68, things to earn and things to buy, and also gives Pulsefire as an example of an occasion on which stories are released throughout the event to deepen the universe. The methodological value of this formulation is plain, since it reveals that the symbol functions as a functional component of the device. Lore does not come in as “decoration”, but as a signal that orients attention, creates the relevance of the period and gives the consumer a language to recognize what is happening now, why it matters and why it is worth staying.

On the legal-institutional plane, the cycle gains density because symbolic content circulates under a regime of license and of formally assumed plasticity. The contractual frame provides the institutional fit for this plasticity. Access to content is described as a personal and limited right, while the supplier reserves the possibility of excluding, altering, removing, p. 113 repackaging and repricing69 content at any time Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e). In this architecture, the recycling of external symbols ceases to be merely a marketing strategy and comes to operate as a component of a supply regime in which meaning is managed as a variable of the service. In Vanderburg's lexicon, the cultural radar is internalized by the platform, which selects and redistributes signals to renew interest, organize routines and sustain economic circulation.

It is in this sense that Vanderburg describes technique as the predominant milieu of life, that is, as the ambience through which we relate to other people, to communities and to the biosphere itself. When this mediation structures routine, it does not merely facilitate actions; it filters experience, privileging what can be expressed in operational terms and pushing the rest to the periphery of what is perceived as real. The desymbolizing pressure becomes more corrosive when it ceases to seem like pressure and comes to be lived as the nature of the world, as if life were only legible when translated into procedure, metric and protocol. In this key, technique does not merely organize doing; it reorganizes the criteria of relevance by which the subject recognizes value, belonging and loss. Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2016, p. 141).

In this framework, technical progress not only raised productivity and pushed the economic transition toward the service sector, but also repositioned entertainment as a central gear of social life. It is here that Vanderburg's reading gains traction, because, when technique infiltrates routines, enjoyment ceases to be an interval and comes to function as an extension of the same system that organizes work, information and coexistence. Entertainment becomes an infrastructure of permanence, desire and practical orientation of everyday life, not by some cultural “magic power”, but by its constancy, availability and capacity to accompany the subject at the pace at which they live. As a result, digital environments such as games cease to be merely cultural products and become places where one learns, feels and decides, which helps explain why they manage to capture attention and stabilize patterns of relevance without needing to appear as coercion.

The analysis is especially relevant in the field of digital games, in which virtual experiences may replace direct interactions with the physical world and offer mediated sociabilities, producing disconnections of a symbolic order that relate not only to violence but extend to the diverse values and meanings that structure social life.

p. 114 In Vanderburg's analysis, it is important to distinguish two registers that operate together in this impoverishment of experience. On one side, there is the lived register, in which the subject still perceives nuances, contexts, ambivalences and meanings that do not fit into simple measures; on the other, there is the technical-operational register, which tends to reorganize reality by goals, indicators, scores and measurable outputs. The point is not to deny the usefulness of metrics, but to note the structural effect when they cease to be localized instruments and become the dominant way of perceiving and deciding.

When what comes to count is only what is calculable, the world is not merely summarized. It is reformatted and, with that, experience loses layers of meaning, of memory and of critique. This displacement, from meaning to performance, from context to objective, sets the scene for the dissociation pointed out by Vanderburg and, in continuity with the Ellulian lineage mobilized, makes it possible to understand technique not merely as a means, but as a practical rationality that is incorporated into routines and comes to organize the very perception of the real.

In Vanderburg, in continuity with the Ellulian tradition, the concept of technique is what makes it possible to read interactive entertainment without falling into reductions such as “it is just fun” or “it is just culture”. Technique, here, does not designate a set of tools, but a mode of organizing the real that imposes itself as practical rationality, as the dominant criterion of what is worth something, of what counts and of what orients action. For this reason, when the digital game becomes a recurring environment of experience, it does not merely represent conflicts or produce narratives, since it trains perceptions and habits, reprogramming what the subject recognizes as relevant.

In this regime, the simulation need not be true to operate as sufficient reality, since it comes to organize conducts, affects and choices insofar as it provides a functional world in which the subject learns to act and to orient themselves Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2011, p. 102). Dissociation then proves legally interesting, since action and the evaluation of experience tend to be reduced to measurable and comparable results, shifting attention from meaning and context to performance and the indicator Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2011, p. 109). The consequence is a compressed and instrumental perception, tuned to the immediate and to the fragment, with short attention cycles and processing by minimal units of information, which impoverishes the symbolic integration of experience Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2016, p. 360).

A particularly effective way of denoting technique incorporated into routines, in the context of the empirical laboratory mobilized, is to observe how “missions” and “passes” come to organize the player's time as a sequence of tasks with their own inputs, outputs and accounting. The game remains, in appearance, a world of fantasy and competition, but p. 115 everyday experience comes to be traversed by a second regime of orientation, quieter and more constant, in which what counts is not merely playing, but meeting progress criteria, stabilizing recurrence, not missing reward windows and maximizing conversions between time and currency. Technique, on this plane, need not suppress the playful dimension to corrode it, since it suffices to install a parallel grammar of goals and indicators that reinterprets enjoyment itself as efficiency.

The regime becomes even clearer when obtaining rewards is formally tied to objectives that function as technical equivalents of the time invested. Instead of depending only on victories or narrative milestones, progress comes to be calculated by points accumulated based on the duration and result of matches, which shifts the center of gravity of the experience to a rationality of productivity. Institutional policy makes this shift visible when it openly describes missions that “earn points just by playing”70 and, above all, when the developers themselves make explicit a matrix of points per minute played, varying according to victory or defeat71 Riot Games(Riot Games, 2025f; Riot Games, 2021b).

In direct dialogue with Vanderburg's key, the symbolic consequence is that routine ceases to be organized only by expectations internal to the game and comes to be governed by an external “radar” of relevance, which selects what must be done today, this week, before the event ends, before the pass closes, before the conversion becomes less efficient. When the reward is structured as a “track” and the platform itself reinforces that certain resources expire, the horizon of experience is cut into short cycles and manageable units, pushing the rest to the periphery of what, in practice, is perceived as important. In this type of organization, technique is not only a means, since it becomes agenda, calendar and criterion of everyday rationality, including in the form of prospective loss, when institutional communication warns that certain items “will be lost forever”72 if not spent within the deadline Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024b).

p. 116 The very naming “mission” reveals this conversion, because it does not designate merely an occasional playful objective, but a repeatable operational chain that structures conduct as the fulfillment of conditions. This structure shows itself clearly when systematized descriptions list alternative objectives that encourage play in a form in which narrative performance is replaced by technical and quantifiable requirements, with parameterized and predictable rewards League of Legends Wiki(League of Legends Wiki, [202-?]f).

This reading does not depend on suggesting that the consumer is incapable of distinguishing between play and work, nor on reducing the experience to “addiction” or “weakness”. The point is structural and lies in the form. By turning progression into calculation, and reward into a regime of goals, the ecosystem produces an enjoyment that presents itself as fun, but operates as a technically administered routine, in which access to content and currencies is organized as a temporal toll73. In this framework, desymbolization does not appear as a lack of culture or an abstract loss of meaning, but as a concrete reprogramming of what orients action, filtering lived experience down to what can be converted into procedure, metric and protocol.

One of the most worrying aspects raised by Vanderburg is the impact of technique on individuals' very capacity for symbolization74, that is, on the ability to give meaning to experiences and connect them to a horizon of shared reality. The point is more radical than a “change of habits” or a generic cultural impoverishment, because it bears on the p. 117 metaconscious work that differentiates, integrates and organizes lived experiences into communicable forms of meaning. When this bond between experience and meaning loses resolution, a deficit of orientation sets in, and the subject comes to depend more intensely on external mediations to attribute relevance to what they live and consume. In this context, recurring digital environments matter as an empirical field because they condense routines of attention, stimulus and response that accelerate the conversion of lived experience into procedure and performance, further narrowing the space for symbolic elaboration.

In this scenario, digital objects and services tend to be offered not merely as utilities, but as supports for symbolic recomposition, since they promise belonging, identity continuity, recognition and direction precisely where culture no longer sustains, by itself, these functions. Vanderburg describes this supplementary role of the media in highly desymbolized societies as a condition of continuous immersion in a “bath of images”, in which the practical orientation of life comes to be, in part, outsourced to technically produced flows of meaning Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2011, p. 92). The promise, here, does not function as external decoration, since it is incorporated into the mode of experience insofar as the subject comes to seek validation and orientation within the mediated environments themselves. When sociality is filtered by platforms, the relationship with the other tends to be traversed by dynamics of visibility, comparison and self-presentation, bringing coexistence closer to performance and turning oneself into something to be administered and recognized externally Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2016, p. 231; p. 367). It is this displacement that makes intelligible the decisive passage, in which advertising and persuasive vectors cease to act only through message and come to act through ambience, that is, through technical routines that organize attention, belonging and permanence.

In this context, Vanderburg offers a forceful critique of the evolution of advertising strategies, drawing attention to the deliberately manipulative character of many of its efforts and to the exploitation of psychosocial vulnerabilities as a method. The point, for the author, is not reduced to the “influence” or “seduction” of consumption, but to the technical calibration of stimuli aimed at producing adherence and dependence, taking advantage of the prior weakening of symbolization. Advertising directed at children appears, in the argument, as an exemplary limit case of this mechanism, precisely because it operates early on the criteria of valuation and meaning, shifting the formation of judgment into a circuit of stimulus and response Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2011, p. 100-101). What matters to the central argument is its structural pattern. Persuasion ceases to be only message and comes to function as a gear of practical orientation in an already desymbolized world.

p. 118 One of the central aspects of his critique is the argument that cultural desymbolization opens room for products to be coupled to already stabilized and recognizable symbols, so that the commodity would integrate into itself values that, in themselves, it does not possess. Advertising tends to operate less by information about the object and more by the manufacture of a symbolic shortcut that connects the advertised good to a lifestyle and transfers to the purchase the promise of belonging, recognition and fulfillment. In this register, advertisements become poorer in informative content and more sophisticated in associative engineering, consolidating in the consumer's mind an equivalence between commodity and desirable symbol Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2011, p. 204). The effect is to reorder criteria of value and identity through ready-made links, instead of strengthening the capacity for situated interpretation.

The author addresses the impact of advertising on the very notion of truth in contemporary societies, indicating that, in desymbolized mass cultures, it comes to function as a technical supplement of orientation. This occurs not because it offers knowledge, but because it offers direction when tradition, bond and narrative no longer stably sustain common experience. If, on one hand, language increasingly becomes an instrument of immediate use, instead of a bearer of integrated meaning Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2016, p. 68), on the other, mass communication comes to operate through the production of affective effects by association and by displacement of contexts, which demotes the role of meaning within language itself.

Vanderburg synthesizes this mechanism by stating that it is no longer the meaning that words and phrases possess within language that structures media communication, but the feelings and emotions they can produce when associated with contexts very different from those that make up the linguistic experience of the members of communities Vanderburg(Vanderburg, 2011, p. 51). When this regime stabilizes, the consequence is not only cognitive distortion but practical alienation, because life comes to be guided by idealized constructions that replace situated experience, dilute criteria of truth and reorganize desire according to a performed reality.

As people move away from situated experiences and from values that provide grounding for everyday life, they become more susceptible to advertising influence not for lack of information, but through a deficit of orientation. When the habit of comparing, distrusting and ranking what matters is lost, desires can be manufactured as ready-made answers to diffuse anxieties, and commodities come to function as affective prostheses of belonging, self-esteem and direction. The product comes in as a pretext, but what is delivered is an emotional script for a technically accelerated existence lacking continuity. It is this weakening of p. 119 internal orientation that prepares the passage to the next problem. When symbolization is corroded, steering becomes easier and less visible, because it no longer depends on explicit persuasion.

This interpretive deficit is precisely what Vanderburg deepens when he shifts the focus from the mere presence of technology to the slow and cumulative process of erosion of symbolization. It is not “there is a screen, therefore it changed”. It is routine turning into procedure, experience turning into task, and life being lived in environments so fragmented and operationalized that meaning can no longer stitch itself together. Lived experience loses the thread that tied it to a shared horizon. What happens no longer integrates into a broader narrative, it becomes a collection of loose episodes, each closed in on itself, with no organic connection to the others. For this reason desymbolization is not only an impoverishment of cultural content. It is the corrosion of the very symbolic function that allows the subject to orient themselves, recognize themselves and situate themselves in a common world, where things do not merely happen, but mean.

In this scenario, the entertainment industry (and, in particular, interactive entertainment) becomes a privileged laboratory for observing the incorporation of technique into routines, because it turns enjoyment into a recurring, stable and operationalizable ambience. Virtual worlds and platforms of mediated sociability offer intense and constant experiences, but governed by previously designed grammars, in which rules, rewards, punishments and possible trajectories are configured by technical-organizational choices. The point, here, is not to moralize the game, but to recognize that this type of environment educates attention and relevance through repetition, and tends to bring experience closer to performance, reinforcing the symbolic impoverishment described by Vanderburg.

The link with advertising makes the picture even more forceful because it inserts, within this routinized ambience, a layer of steering by symbolic association. Technical advertising is not limited to informing characteristics. It parasitizes preexisting cultural symbols and offers goods and services as shortcuts to belonging, recognition, affection or relief from guilt. In this regime, subjective needs cease to be a presupposition of consumption and come to be produced and modulated by associative engineering, precisely because desymbolization has already weakened the internal criteria of orientation. What stands out, in this context, is the convergence between the erosion of symbolization and the offer of ready-made direction mediated by signs. It is the preparation of the ground for the passage from message to design.

Taken together, the diagnosis shifts the focus from content to form. The decisive point is not only what advertising says, but how a technical-organizational system positions the subject within routines of stimulus, reward, visibility and comparison, p. 120 making certain paths more probable and others almost impracticable. When desymbolization weakens internal orientation and advertising functions as a prosthesis of belonging, steering ceases to depend on explicit persuasion and comes to operate by design, by interface choices, by functioning arrangements, by interaction patterns and by forms of governance that structure experience even before any message. In Vanderburg, this process appears as cultural corrosion and persuasive exploitation. However, the mechanism itself points to something beyond the message: a rationality inscribed in artifacts, systems and forms of circulation. The passage from message to arrangement, from content to design, opens the bridge to Feenberg, whose contribution will make it possible to treat technology as a social construction and design as a political site of dispute.

  1. In the works Our War on Ourselves (2011) and Our Battle for the Human Spirit (2016), Willem H. Vanderburg articulates two movements that should be distinguished analytically. On the one hand, dissociation, understood as the severing of the bond between action and its human context, when repeated stimuli, metrics and simulations push the subject toward an orientation centered on performance, success and result. On the other, desymbolization, deeper and more structural, because it involves the erosion of the capacity to integrate lived experiences into shared horizons of meaning, causing experience to cease to connect to the world, the world to narrative, and narrative to reality. In digital consumption, these dimensions tend to converge, since sensorially saturated and repetitive experiences shorten the time for working through experience and reduce the space for reflection, making the subject more available to regimes of stimulus and reward. In this work, however, priority is given to desymbolization, because it is desymbolization that bears directly on the ability to interpret critically environments mediated by signs. ↩

  4. It is relevant here to point out the identifiable parallel with the paradigmatic thought of the French sociologist Émille Durkheim, specifically that developed in the works Da Divisão do Trabalho Social (1999), O Suicídio (2000) and Formas Elementares da Vida Religiosa: O sistema totêmico na Austrália (1996), originally published in 1893, 1897 and 1912, respectively. For the author, religion — and, with it, one of the fundamental matrices of moral cohesion — is not explained by simple reference to “gods”, but by the constitutive split of the social universe between sacred and profane, in which the sacred is defined by the regime of interdictions that protects and isolates it, functioning as a pole of collective ordering Durkheim(Durkheim, 1996, p. 24). In this architecture, the totem operates as a sensible support and emblem of a collective force that, being diffuse, needs to be objectified in a material figure in order to become perceived and socially binding; for this reason, when the group invests the symbol, it is not a perceptual “error”, but a way of making visible and effective a real, transfigured social energy, to the point that Durkheim states that the very idea of society constitutes the vital core of religion Durkheim(Durkheim, 1996, p. 462- 463). Social unity, however, is not maintained by inertia: it requires rites and assemblies capable of periodically reinvigorating collective feelings and ideas that, in everyday life, tend to weaken, which shows the centrality of ritual maintenance as a mechanism of social continuity Durkheim(Durkheim, 1996, p. 472). Postman's reading, taken up in the previous item to describe how the media form reorganizes criteria of relevance and credibility, helps make this parallel empirically intelligible in the contemporary register. By recoding religious experience into the language of spectacle, television does not merely “transmit” messages. It alters the ways in which authority and adherence are publicly produced, increasing dependence on regimes of visibility, charisma and performance. In this context, religious mediators may be socially perceived as celebrities, and experience tends to be more strongly modulated by the form of presentation than by narrative or doctrinal density. Within this scenario, the bridge to cultural desymbolization in the sense worked out by Vanderburg becomes especially productive. Technical-operational modernity compresses dense symbolic mediations without eliminating the structural need for symbolization; it displaces, however, its supports and criteria of validity to artifacts, devices and functional grammars, opening space for metrics, performances and access infrastructures to assume, in practice, functions of belonging, validation and sanction comparable, by analogy, to those Durkheim describes in the symbolic regime, converting technical governance into lived moral routine. A foreseeable side effect of this displacement is regulatory instability. Since nothing in the individual by itself fixes the limit of aspirations, containment depends on an external force Durkheim(Durkheim, 2000, p. 313-315) and, when there are “disturbances of the collective order”, even in cycles of prosperity, the balance that disciplines expectations and conducts is broken Durkheim(Durkheim, 2000, p. 311), producing social suffering typical of deregulation Durkheim(Durkheim, 2000, p. 329). Along the same lines, when the relations between social functions cease to be adequately regulated, the division of labor itself may enter a state of anomie, failing to produce solidarity Durkheim(Durkheim, 1999, p. 385), although morality, as a source of bonds that force the individual to reckon with others, remains a decisive criterion of integration Durkheim(Durkheim, 1999, p. 420). This articulation also prevents two inadequate readings, above all the one that symbols would be a disposable “error”, since Durkheim rejects the reduction of religion to cognitive falsehood and treats such forms as institutional responses to real human needs Durkheim(Durkheim, 1996, p. VII), and also the one that technical rationalization would replace, without remainder, the practical function of the symbolic bond, since scientific explanation may not take the place of faith as an impulse to action Durkheim(Durkheim, 1996, p. 477). ↩
