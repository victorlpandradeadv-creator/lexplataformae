3.1.4 Technology as social construction and the political rationality of design

p. 120

In digital environments, especially in games-as-a-service, the relevant changes are not natural accidents of the object, but architectural decisions that redefine what it is, how much it is worth and how it can be enjoyed. Patches, reworks, alterations of visual effects and adjustments of the internal economy do not function merely as technical maintenance; they rewrite conditions of permanence, equivalence and recognition within the ecosystem. Bringing in Feenberg75 at this point makes it possible to treat this modulation as a social rationality incorporated into infrastructure, and thus to ask about choices, purposes and responsibilities where the supplier tends to present only the “natural evolution” of the platform or of the game.

The diagnosis of technical autonomy, media reorganization and desymbolization needs to be brought back to the plane on which technique appears as a disputed social form. Ellul, Postman and Vanderburg make it possible to understand the techno-media order as an ambience that reorganizes everyday life, and Feenberg prevents this ambience from being read as destiny. Technique comes to be examined as the institutional result of choices incorporated into artifacts, systems and governance routines.

The usefulness of the perspective is immediate when the reference ecosystem is brought back. The alteration of a rare cosmetic, the reconfiguration of a character by rework and the gradation of sanctions up to banning are not natural effects of an evolving technology, p. 121 but architectural decisions that stabilize economic, disciplinary and operational purposes in the very infrastructure of enjoyment. What is presented to the consumer as a patch76, balancing or conduct policy operates, in practice, as incorporated normativity: it defines what is worth something, what appears, what gives status, what loses value and, through disciplinary mechanisms that may culminate in banning, who remains authorized to participate in the ecosystem. Feenberg matters here precisely because he makes it possible to treat this set as a political rationality materialized in a system, making knowable the teleology inscribed in interface, rules and sanction architecture, instead of reducing the conflict to mere inevitable technical variation.

A controlled example of this political rationality materialized in a system is the discipline of balancing, in which the meta77 is continually produced and corrected by technical interventions. In League of Legends, buffs78 and nerfs79 are not an accessory added after the game, but the very means by which the ecosystem defines, in regular cycles, which characters and strategies remain competitively viable and which are, for a period, downgraded to the condition of suboptimal choices. Seen in this way, the patch operates as a norm in operation. It does not merely readjust variables, but reconfigures, in practice, the competitive utility of the assets enjoyed, redistributes frequency of use, repositions preferences and alters the real “cost” of permanence in the environment, since what is functionally strong tends to concentrate attention, learning and investment of time.

The supplier's own institutional documentation treats balancing as a formalized procedure endowed with criteria, which reinforces that the design code is a p. 122 stabilized intersection between social choice and technical specification. In publicly explaining its Champion Balance Framework, Riot Games describes the objective of making buff and nerf decisions “consistent” and “easier to understand”, including by means of measurable parameters, audience segmentation and the combined use of win rate and ban rate80, as well as thresholds that function as intervention triggers Riot Games(Riot Games, 2019a). The result is a technical rationality that presents itself as transparency and predictability, but that also performs a social function, insofar as it converts statistical performance into practical orders of what should be played, what should be avoided and what loses competitive centrality. The social life of the game is organized around this grammar, because what is strong is not merely “better”, but what the system allows to be better, for a time, under certain metrics and for certain audiences.

The structural character of this governance by design becomes even more visible when one observes the very institutional cadence of updates. Riot maintains a public index of Patch Notes81, in chronological sequence, which makes it possible to reconstruct the regularity of the patch as the normal mode of operation of the service82 Riot Games(Riot Games, [202-?]b). This periodicity is relevant because it makes balancing a form of continuous administration of functional value in the ecosystem. If the meta is, to a large extent, the set of incentivized choices, then the update is not merely maintenance. It is the mechanism that makes functional obsolescence an ordinary component of enjoyment, since what was learned, mastered and chosen as the axis of performance can be downgraded, in weeks, by unilateral technical decision.

p. 123 It should also be emphasized that this institutional frame finds an explicit contractual basis. The Terms of Service state that the supplier may alter, update, suspend and even “nerf” features and parts of the services, including virtual products, as well as providing for the application of patches in the background and automatic updates, with or without prior notice83 Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e). Balancing presents itself, in this framework, as a regular exercise of governance by design: a modality of administration of the ecosystem in which technical choices cyclically reorganize the field of the possible, modulate competitive utility and reorder incentives of permanence and acquisition84.

Feenberg's contribution qualifies the techno-media order as an institutionalized social rationality. It is not a cultural background that merely influences practices, but a mode of coordination in which technically rational operations come to organize, rank and legitimize everyday conduct. By shifting rationality from the domain of an abstract ideal to that of an operating social function, Feenberg provides the passage necessary for the legal-consumerist critique not to be confused with a moralizing refusal of technique. What is reconstructed is a regime of power whose efficacy is produced by systems, patterns and decision routines incorporated into infrastructures85 Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 15-16).

Feenberg defines as the inaugural gesture the defamiliarization of what has become banal in modern societies. The point is to show that rationality, far from being limited to a technical criterion of efficiency, performs a social function by organizing practices, expectations and hierarchies and by producing an institutionalized grammar of decision Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 15- 16). With this, technique ceases to be a mere background of the social and comes to figure as a principle of ordering the lived world. This shift is decisive, since it authorizes interpreting the p. 124 architecture of platforms not as a neutral support for exchanges, but as a concretized social rationality, capable of producing asymmetries and of demanding critical-legal scrutiny.

This shift is central to the argument because it places the problem of rationality back on the plane of the concrete conditions that organize the possible. What counts as efficient, normal, safe, convenient or inevitable comes to be the result of institutional framings that operate before and, often, outside public deliberation.86 In digital consumption, this rationality operates as a silent infrastructure. It defines patterns of choice, frictions, facilities and invisibilizations and, with that, converts the consumer's decision into an act situated within a field previously architected by technical criteria that present themselves as functional naturalness Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 15-16).

The very opening of the work makes explicit that the investigation is not reduced to a descriptive sociology of technology. By interrogating the nature and role of rationality in contemporary society, Feenberg situates his project at the intersection between philosophy and sociology of technology87 Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 15). In this light, the normative problem ceases to be only that of identifying informational or contractual asymmetries and becomes that of understanding how technical rationality, by acquiring a social function, produces its own conditions of legitimacy. When this rationality organizes expectations and behaviors, it tends to replace persuasion with the structuring of the environment. Platforms become legally relevant not only for what they “say” in terms of use, but for what they “do” in their architectures.

It is within this horizon that Feenberg proposes the concept of technosystem88. Instead of treating “technology”, “market” and “administration” as autonomous spheres that occasionally influence one another, the technosystem describes an integrated field of technically rational disciplines and operations associated with these three domains Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 16). In digital p. 125 consumption, this prevents the platform from being read as a mere technical provision, because it operates simultaneously as market (models of extraction and monetization), as administration (private governance and enforcement89) and as technology (architecture of mediation). It is a self-producing and self-reinforcing arrangement, making inadequate any reading that isolates “the artifact” from the institutional rationalities that traverse it Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 16).

The triad of market, administration and technology, conceived as a technosystem, provides a precise map for locating the platform as a contemporary form of organizational power. The market appears as a rationality of monetization and extraction, administration acts as a rationality of private governance, through moderation, sanctions, internal policies and enforcement mechanisms, and technology operates as a rationality of design that materializes these purposes in interfaces, decision flows and regimes of visibility. The dogmatic relevance of this framing is clear. Supply ceases to be understood as a simple provision and comes to be seen as an integrated sociotechnical arrangement, in which what the consumer receives is inseparable from the way access, permanence, modulation and loss are administered by technically rational operations Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 16).

The definition of the technosystem comes with a structural consequence, since the dependence of systems on technical disciplines and their mutual mediation constitute essential features of modern societies Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 16). For the argument at hand, this prevents digital consumption from being explained as the contracting of an isolated object, because the form of consumption depends on the technical-organizational mediations that structure access, permanence, visibility and monetization. With this, the focus shifts from content to the architecture of enjoyment and modulation, since the technical rationality that organizes the experience is not external to consumption, but integrates the very materiality of what is enjoyed.

It is from this argument that the technosystem offers a basis for the category of the Immaterial Object of Consumption as a legal infrastructure-concept. If the consumed experience depends on continuous mediations and if these mediations are entangled in markets and administrations, then the “product” (as defined under Article 3, paragraph 1 of the CDC) cannot be isolated from the regime of visibility, accessibility and modulation that constitutes it Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 16). Immaterial consumption appears, here, as governed enjoyment. The consumer simultaneously experiences a content and the technical and organizational conditions of access to that content, conditions that operate by criteria of functioning and are not exhausted by the traditional duty of information.

p. 126 From there, Feenberg formulates a high-yield premise for the legal critique of platforms: no technology is an island90 because every technology is mediated by markets and administrations. The platform should not be naturalized as a simple means, as if it were a neutral engineering unit to which the social would be added later. If technology is always mediated by economic and administrative rationalities, then design choices must be read as socially situated decisions, open to criticism as to the purposes they realize and the asymmetries they produce, that is, as a locus of attribution and of assessment of legitimacy.

To prevent this critique from falling into a pessimistic substantivism, Feenberg anchors himself in a critical constructivism91, according to which technology is neither value-neutral nor universal and, precisely for this reason, admits an explicit theory of democratic interventions in the technosystem Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 38). The consequence is methodological. The critique of platforms must bear on concrete arrangements of rationality and power, that is, on architectural decisions that incorporate values, select ends and stabilize criteria of functioning. This framing prepares the treatment of the teleology inscribed in design as a knowable and criticizable structure of purpose, and not as a mere side effect of technical operation Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 36-38).

If technique is constructed and disputed92, requirements such as open auditability93 and functional value equivalence can be read, from the outset, as parameters for the democratization of design. They do not function as rhetorical accessories of transparency, but as minimum material conditions so that the addressee is not reduced to the position of a mere executor of p. 127 an architectural script oriented by extraction and organizational predictability Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 36-38).

The idea of democratic interventions in the technosystem94 is of objective interest because it makes it possible to understand legal criteria as forms of reorienting technical rationality without presupposing a “pure” externality of law. The requirements then come to operate as minimum material conditions so that the addressee is not reduced to the position of a mere executor of an architectural pattern oriented by extraction and organizational predictability.

In this sense, restrictions materialized in systems may determine the actions of individuals with greater efficacy than formal authority, since they bear on skills, reflexes and attitudes, which, in interface ecosystems, connects politics under design to the perspective of consumer vulnerability.

p. 128 Non-neutrality does not present itself only as an abstract thesis. Feenberg observes that arrangements that appear technically neutral may incorporate unconscious preferences95. In digital consumption, this has an immediate consequence, since the practical normativity of platforms96 (what becomes visible, recommendable, frictioned, permitted or rewarded) may operate as interface “naturalness”, masking value choices under the idiom of functionality. In the grammar of the thesis at hand, this mechanism is decisive for vulnerability, since the asymmetry and the resulting risk are not reduced to informational content, but bear on the very field of experience that precedes reading and conditions expectations.

Unconscious preference acquires particular density in interface ecosystems. Default patterns, hierarchies of choice, urgency devices and reward regimes orient conduct without needing to declare the value orientation that informs them. Even without textual lie or omission, practical normativity can induce perceptions, desires and permanences through a grammar of interaction presented as simple usability. In a Feenbergian key, vulnerability stems from the politics incorporated into design: a material asymmetry that organizes perception and expectation before any formal consent.

Feenberg formulates the normative dimension of design by describing restrictions materialized in systems97 capable of determining individual actions more effectively than political authority, bearing on reflexes, skills and attitudes Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 36). When conduct is administered by systems and interfaces, governance ceases to operate p. 129 only by explicit command and comes to operate by architecture. Platforms, in this sense, function as everyday governance, and legal responses cannot be restricted to clauses and notices when practical normativity98 is inscribed in the design99 Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 36-38).

The passage from explicit command to architecture as governance makes it possible to distinguish a regime in which the consumer decides in the face of statements from another in which they decide within an environment that organizes microstructures of choice. This shift makes the purpose incorporated into design legally relevant. When restrictions and incentives are integrated into the system to produce permanence, extraction and dependence under the appearance of enjoyment, teleology ceases to appear as a side effect and comes to compose the material form of supply Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 36-38).

The idea of code offers the conceptual mediation to describe how social choices stabilize into technical specifications. Feenberg states that the result of struggles and collaborations is a design code100, that is, the stabilized intersection between social choice and technical specification101 Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 93). For the IOC regime, this is decisive because the object consumed is not reduced to the final content; it includes the set of mediations that makes this content enjoyable, monetizable and governable. Architecture thus appears as the core of functioning and of promise. At the same time, the code can be evaluated p. 130 critically as to the purpose it realizes, opening the way for the subsequent qualification of the teleology inscribed in architecture and governance.

By being defined as the stabilized intersection between social choice and technical specification, the design code also illuminates the dynamics of normative closure typical of platforms. Once stabilized, the code tends to reproduce itself through institutional inertia, through path dependencies and through legal and economic mechanisms that make alternatives and reappropriations more costly. In this scenario, intellectual property appears as an infrastructure of semiotic and technical governance. It not only protects creations, but contributes to delimiting uses, restricting reconfigurations and consolidating the supplier's authority over the conditions of enjoyment, reinforcing the private character of the code that organizes the experience Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 93).

The political rationality of design becomes visible when the technical update ceases to be a mere maintenance procedure and comes to function as an ordinary instrument of government of the ecosystem. In League of Legends, balancing is carried out through patches that strengthen or weaken characters, reorganizing, in regular cycles, which choices remain competitively viable and which are displaced to positions of lesser efficacy. It is not merely an adjustment of parameters, but a continuous respecification of the field of the possible, with direct effects on the forms of enjoyment, on the distribution of attention and on the practical cost of permanence, since alterations in relative performance tend to reorient training, investment of time and preferences.

The supplier's institutional documentation presents this process as the application of criteria and metrics, which reinforces that the design code is not a neutral given of the system, but a stabilized mediation between social choices and technical implementation. By publicly exposing a plan to guide buff and nerf decisions, Riot describes parameters that link statistical performance and patterns of audience rejection to intervention triggers Riot Games(Riot Games, 2019a). This formalization, although presented as technical rationality, also performs a social function, insofar as it converts metrics into practical orders about what tends to be favored or discouraged in the competitive experience, influencing what is learned, what is repeated and what becomes, for a time, central in the ecosystem.

The institutional cadence of updates confirms that this is a structural routine of governance, and not an exception. The company is well known for maintaining a public index of patch notes in chronological sequence, which makes it possible to reconstruct the regularity of the patch as the ordinary mode of operation of the service Riot Games(Riot Games, [202-?]b). Periodicity matters because p. 131 it turns the update into a mechanism of continuous administration of competitive utility. In an environment in which practical value depends on a set of relations kept operative by the system, what was acquired as a position of enjoyment can be reordered at short notice by a technical decision, with effects that cannot be reduced to inevitable variation, since they directly affect the stability of performance, the predictability of the experience and the hierarchy of incentivized choices.

This picture gains further density when one observes the articulation between the technical plane and the symbolic layer of the ecosystem, including the transmedia production associated with the game's universe. The series Arcane102, distributed by Netflix and produced with Riot's participation, broadens the cultural centrality of characters and narratives, reaching audiences beyond the game and reinforcing the visibility of certain symbolic assets¹ NETFLIX(Netflix, 2024). From this perspective, the technical decision to introduce or reposition a character does not act on an abstract object, but on a figure whose meaning is continually produced by cultural and marketing strategies.

The episode involving the champion Mel shows more clearly how the technical rationality of balancing can operate coupled to the symbolic economy of the ecosystem, without any need to attribute subjective intention to the decision-maker. The official introduction of the character was presented as a narrative and temporal milestone associated with the universe of p. 132 Arcane, with an institutionally fixed release date103 Riot Games(Riot Games, 2025c). From then on, throughout 2025, the champion's presence remained at the center of governance by design for two complementary reasons.

First, her strength and the type of defensive interaction her kit allowed became a recurring object of public evaluation and technical intervention, with effects on bans, frequency of appearance and perception of competitive legitimacy, which reveals that the meta does not “happen” merely through collective preference, but is continually administered by criteria and corrections. Second, already in the year-end cycle, this centrality was connected to a prestige window linked to rare cosmetic content, with a publicly recorded opening date104 Riot Games(Riot Games, 2025e). Then, in early 2026, patch 26.3 carried out a significant respecification of the champion's mode of enjoyment by altering the central protection mechanism that sustained her interaction pattern, replacing the previous logic with a shield105 and expressly removing the damage immunity106 Riot Games(Riot Games, 2026d).

The sequence is sufficient to support the claim that design does not act merely as technical optimization, but as a social mediation that regulates centrality, acceptability and functional value, including when this regulation is synchronized with cycles of prestige and scarcity administered by the platform itself.

In Feenberg's lexicon, the decisive advance lies in the understanding that technical alteration is not merely efficiency, but social mediation. By defining which forms of protection will be permitted, which will be reconfigured and which interactions will be taken as excessively frustrating, design produces a politics incorporated into code, which governs the experience without presenting itself as an explicit legal norm. For this reason, balancing, although wrapped in the p. 133 language of “game health”, operates as continuous administration of the environment, with the capacity to redistribute functional value and reorganize patterns of conduct and investment.

Finally, it is worth recalling that this power of respecification has an express contractual basis. The Terms of Service authorize the supplier to alter, update, suspend and even “nerf” features and parts of the service, including virtual products, and to apply patches automatically, with or without prior notice Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e). Thus, governance by design does not appear as an occasional event, but as an ordinary component of the continuous provision model, in which the object enjoyed depends structurally on an administered ecosystem.

The problem of organizational power appears, in this context, as an internal dimension of the technosystem, and not as an external factor. In revisiting the theme of industrial labor, Feenberg exposes how the union between capital and science can be oriented toward reducing workers' tasks to the exercise of vigilance and dexterity107, reorganizing dependencies and reinforcing management's decision-making autonomy.

Although the example is not digital, its function here is structural. It shows that technical decisions can be guided by purposes of control and predictability and that this control materializes in technical form. The analogy with platforms becomes intelligible when one observes that the supplier's organizational autonomy tends to be converted into an architecture of capture and private governance, while the consumer operates under asymmetric conditions of auditability and contestation Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 54-55).

This diagnosis108 makes it possible109 to read the platform as a social form of organizing relations and redistributing capacities, not merely as efficiency. When this logic is transposed to consumption, the typical asymmetry of the technosystem appears as the supplier's operational autonomy110. A successful architecture is one that reduces the dependence p. 134 of the operator on the subjects who use it, converts participation into data and value and does not generate equivalent power to define purposes. In this scenario, consent tends to operate under material conditions of low contestability, since the consumer adheres to a regime of functioning whose rationality has already been decided and incorporated into the system, which deepens the distance between formal choice and practical governance Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 54-55).

It is precisely for this reason that, in dealing with the internet, Feenberg refuses to describe it as a neutral medium, since he insists that it is, above all, a technical system111 whose social meaning is inextricably intertwined with its technical character Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 99). This formulation has strategic value because it shifts the debate from the level of content to the level of architecture, since what matters is not only what circulates, but the technical conditions that make certain circulations possible, probable and profitable. In the IOC regime, this means that the object consumed is inseparable from the technical system that constitutes it, since enjoyment, permanence, sociality and monetization derive from the technical form and not only from contractual promises about content Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 99-101).

The dispute over the internet, in this reading, is organized by competing models that Feenberg calls the “consumption model” and the “community model”112. This polarization is useful for the focus on digital consumption because it shows the contingency of the platform, since the platform does not result from an internal necessity of technique, but from conflicts between purposes and rationalities. This tension makes it possible to treat platform governance as a dispute over the social meaning of technique and, at the same time, as a dispute over what is offered to the consumer, since participation, community and belonging can be mobilized both as forms of life and as an infrastructure of monetization Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 99-101).

The distinction between the consumption model and the community model113 is especially productive for interactive entertainment because it shows how community resources p. 135 (many-to-many communication, sociability and belonging) can be integrated into market rationality as an infrastructure of extraction. In other words, community does not appear merely as a “social effect” of the product, but as part of its economic value and of its monetization architecture. This is central to understanding the concept of the IOC, because it confirms that the materiality of the object is relational and semiotic. Belonging, reputation and identity continuity are technically organized components, and not spontaneous externalities Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 99-105).

This point requalifies the usual language of the sector, which tends to hide the normative core under engineering vocabulary (“adjustment”, “update”, “balancing”). When a platform unilaterally alters a rare cosmetic item or redesigns a character, it is determining, by code, the material conditions of enjoyment and the way the good will be recognized and compared in the social world of the game. If the alteration downgrades the functional and symbolic value that motivated the acquisition, the problem is not “user nostalgia”, but an architectural redefinition of the content delivered, with direct impact on auditability and on the attribution of abusiveness.

Thus, in stating that the future of the Internet depends on which actors determine its technical code114, Feenberg provides a particularly useful formula of attribution for the problem of platforms. The proposition makes it possible to treat design as a material dimension of supply. If the technical code is determined by actors, it is politically situated and economically orientable and, for this reason, its teleologies can be reconstructed and criticized. It is here that the bridge between sociotechnical diagnosis and legal-p. 136 consumerist reconstruction is stabilized. When the technical code defines conditions of enjoyment, permanence and loss, the asymmetry between supplier and consumer is not a mere informational defect, but an architectural structure and, for this reason, a field of legal relevance Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 101).

This dispute is not merely ideological, since it materializes in technical solutions that condense functions. In describing many-to-many communication as a typical concretization115, Feenberg offers a useful instrument for thinking of platforms as ambivalent infrastructures, since one and the same technical structure can sustain, simultaneously, community participation, cultural circulation and economic extraction, according to the technical code that stabilizes it. For the study of the immaterial object of consumption, this reinforces that the consumable experience is formed as architecture, because functionalities are not appendages, but convergent arrangements that organize ways of life, expectations and willingness to pay.

The notion of concretization makes it possible to read more precisely the constitutive ambivalence of certain functionalities. One and the same technical structure can concentrate multiple purposes and serve public life and commercial life simultaneously, depending on how it is stabilized by the technical code Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 99-105). For the argument under discussion, this rules out simplifications, since the point is not to assert that platforms are only capture, but to demonstrate that capture can be produced by specific reorientations of functions that also sustain sociability, expression and collective organization. It is in this ambivalence that it becomes legally decisive to ask which teleologies were incorporated into the architecture and which conditions of auditability can make this incorporation open to critique.

Finally, the example of social networks makes explicit, with precision, the ambivalence that matters to the illustrative empirical case. Feenberg observes that social networking sites serve commerce by serving community and that targeted advertising, based on data mining, constitutes a central source of revenue Feenberg(Feenberg, 2017, p. 105). The implication for platform-mediated consumption is direct, since belonging, recognition and sociability, which are affective and identity dimensions that mobilize symbolic investment, can function as an infrastructure of monetization under architectures of low auditability for the user. For the IOC regime, Feenberg reinforces two consequences. The first is that the materiality of the object is architectural and relational, because it depends on the technical form that stabilizes community and capture. The second is that consumer vulnerability must be read as factual vulnerability of a structural dimension under opaque ecosystems, in which the p. 137 correspondence between expectation and delivery, between promise and enjoyment, is not immediately verifiable.

The first consequence is that design must be treated as a material dimension of supply, because it is there that the practical rules of access, permanence and loss that define the enjoyment of the immaterial object are stabilized. The second is that, if architecture concentrates teleologies and condenses ambivalent functions, legal control cannot depend only on general clauses or advertising promises, and must reach the mechanisms by which the platform produces equivalences, downgrades values and applies modulations without sufficient transparency. It is in this sense that the dispute over “technical code” becomes, for continuous digital consumption, a dispute over minimum criteria of controllability.

With this, Feenberg makes it possible to balance Ellul's contribution without yielding to fatalism: where Ellul diagnoses the systemic expansion of technical rationality, Feenberg provides instruments to reconstruct it as a situated social form (mediated by markets, administrations and technologies), therefore open to criticism in its codes, in its concretizations and in its teleologies. The decisive point, therefore, is that designed architectures materialize practical normativity. Consumer Law cannot be limited to the verification of clauses or isolated informational duties when experience, enjoyment and loss are conditioned by technical decisions incorporated into the environment. The rationality of design must be confronted as a material dimension of supply, preparing the following synthesis: the techno-media environment is not an external setting of the IOC, but its condition of possibility.

  13. In the work Questioning Technology, Feenberg (1999) organizes his contribution as a programmatic inflection. He shifts the philosophy of technology away from the terrain in which technique appears as a sphere separate from democratic life and argues that this cut is itself antidemocratic, since it removes from public debate precisely a dimension that structures everyday life and redistributes capacities. Throughout the book, his target is twofold. On one side, to reposition technology as an object of legitimate dispute, showing how social movements and everyday conflicts make the “technical” a political question. On the other, to give conceptual form to this process through the idea of “democratic rationalization”, a concept he presents as a reformulation and maturation of his earlier notion of “subversive” rationalizations. “The argument of that book is based on the contrast between capitalist and ‘subversive’ forms of rationalization. In the context of a more developed analysis of technical politics, I now call the latter ‘democratic rationalization’ [...]” Feenberg(Feenberg, 1999, p. xvi). The force of this shift for the problem of platforms is clear. It makes it possible to treat the contestation of design and governance as a rational and politically pertinent practice, and not as a whim external to technical development; and it offers a vocabulary for thinking about the reorientation of architectures when users and the public sphere put pressure on codes of functioning that were stabilized under asymmetric rationalities. ↩

  20. The example of the contestation around AIDS, as worked out by Feenberg in Questioning Technology (1999, p. 127; p. 141; p. 192), is especially illuminating because it shifts democratic rationalization from the terrain of communication networks to the interior of a highly institutionalized technical system, medicine. In the 1980s, AIDS overburdened medical institutions that, to a large extent, tended to treat patients as “compliant” objects of clinical management, that is, as passive recipients of decisions taken by specialists. The case, however, did not develop in a social vacuum. Many patients already belonged to social networks mobilized around civil rights, particularly those of homosexuals, networks that functioned in parallel to the networks of contagion and that were accustomed to public controversy, to the production of visibility and to institutional pressure. This prior infrastructure of collective organization altered the pattern of “technical” relationship between the medical system and patients. The dispute concentrates on access to experimental treatments and, therefore, on clinical research as a technical-regulatory practice. In a technocratic model of medicine, research can be presented as residual “care” for incurable cases, but access remains strongly limited by paternalistic criteria and precautions, defined from the top down under the justification of patient welfare and of preserving the integrity of the scientific method. What collective intervention produces is a reappropriation of the technical practice itself. Instead of participating individually as objects of a procedure, the activists act as an organized interested party and reorient the system toward new purposes, pressing for experimentation to become a standard form of care for incurable patients and for the design of protocols to reflect, more responsively, the needs and urgencies of those who live with the condition. This reorientation does not operate only on the discursive plane. It involves alterations in technical rules for conducting experiments, such as the critique of the use of placebos and of the limitations of eligibility and participation in statistical studies, as well as the contestation of the way risks and benefits are distributed. The crucial point, in Feenberg, is that the conflict is institutionalized as dialogue and produces changes in governance. Regulatory bodies such as the FDA begin to open channels of “innovative dialogue” with activists, including participation in committees and decision-making bodies. The result is a significantly modified practice of experimental medicine, situated at the intersection between the interests of participants and the scientific concerns of researchers. The case thus functions as a countercurrent to the technocratic organization of medicine by reinserting, within the technical-regulatory structure itself, dimensions of care and meaning that tend to be compressed by the rationality of control. For the debate on platforms and architecture, the analytical gain is direct. First, the example shows that “democratic intervention” need not mean only external regulation; it can mean a dispute internal to the technical form and to the governance that administers it. Second, it shows that collective networks (including digital networks) can empower groups to constitute a “technical locale” from which they influence global systems, not only demanding transparency, but altering criteria, protocols and practical rules. The thesis of preference incorporated into design is especially relevant for platforms, because what becomes visible, recommendable, frictioned, permitted or rewarded can operate as interface naturalness, masking value choices under the idiom of functionality. The asymmetry, at this point, is not reduced to informational content, since it bears on the very field of experience that precedes reading, orients perception and conditions expectations. “Instead of participating in medicine individually as objects of a technical practice, they challenged it collectively and turned it to new purposes.” Feenberg(Feenberg, 1999, p. 127) ↩

  22. In Transforming Technology: A Critical Theory Revisited, a work published in 2002 (and which functions as a revision of Critical Theory of Technology, published in the previous decade) also considered by this research on a complementary basis, Feenberg seeks to reopen a question that technocratic culture tends to close prematurely. Instead of accepting the dilemma between, on one side, technology as a neutral instrument and, on the other, technology as an autonomous and fatalistic destiny, the author shifts the problem to the field of alternatives. What is at stake is not “the” technology, but the plurality of possible technologies and of trajectories of progress among which one chooses. In this key, the work argues that critique must bear on the values that govern technological development and that degrade work, education and the environment. The normative horizon is a democratic transformation of technical development itself, capable of reconciling expanded freedoms with more substantive forms of material well-being, without reducing the debate to an anti-technical moralism. It is in this register that the theory of instrumentalization appears as the central conceptual framework for describing the ambivalence of the technical phenomenon at two complementary levels. “Primary instrumentalization” designates the technical orientation that isolates the real and makes it manipulable under criteria of calculation and control: “The primary instrumentalization is the technical orientation toward reality that Heidegger identified as the technological ‘mode of revealing.’” Feenberg(Feenberg, 2002, p. 175). “Secondary instrumentalization” is not cultural ornament, but the moment in which technique is realized in devices and social systems, reintroducing context, uses and values; for this reason, “The complementarity of primary and secondary instrumentalization is a normal aspect of the technical sphere.” Feenberg(Feenberg, 2002, p. 177). The conceptual pair makes it possible to understand how, in interface ecosystems, “usability” can operate as a social stabilization of purposes and, therefore, as a regular form of governing experience. ↩

In formulating the notion of “operational autonomy”, Feenberg (2002, p. 43) seeks to show that capitalist control of the productive process is not exercised only through legal-formal command or administrative coordination, but is consolidated when the organization of work itself is technically incorporated. It is at this point that the author turns to Karl Marx (citing an edition of Capital published in 1906) to maintain that the specifically capitalist form of organizing the labor process acquires a palpable technical reality insofar as machinery and productive arrangements transform the worker into a subordinate component of a pre-constituted material system. Feenberg's reading thus emphasizes that technology is not a mere neutral instrument applied to previously given ends, but the very crystallization of a social relation in technical form, in which knowledge, initiative and margin of decision are displaced from labor to management and to infrastructure. The conceptual consequence, for the author, is that operational autonomy designates this discretionary capacity to decide and reorganize the process without depending on the point of view and interests of the subordinate actors, precisely because those interests have been downgraded and neutralized at the level of technical organization. ↩

  39. In Questioning Technology Feenberg(Feenberg, 1999, p. 126; 191), the case of Minitel (Teletel) is presented as a classic example of technical reorientation driven by user agency, that is, of democratic rationalization in operation. The system was conceived and distributed by the French telephone company (France Telecom), which put millions of terminals into circulation free of charge, designed to look like an accessory of the household telephone. This gesture of design and public policy had a relatively clear technocratic direction, that of consolidating an access infrastructure and organizing Minitel as an instrument for the centralized distribution of information and data services. The users' intervention occurs precisely when this design is taken as the starting point for an unforeseen appropriation. The fact that the terminal “disguised itself” as a telephone device suggested to some users that the network could serve for interpersonal communication. From there, users “hacked” the network in which they were inserted and altered its functioning, introducing practices of online human communication, such as anonymous chat, aimed at amusement, companionship and encounters, where the mere distribution of data had been planned. The result is a transformation of the system's social meaning. What had been stabilized as a rationalist medium of information comes to be redefined as a medium of encounter and communication, converting the “cold computer” into a “new hot medium”. The theoretical point is that this trajectory reveals the interpretive flexibility of technology. A set of devices configured to transmit information is perceived by users as a solution for human communication, and this new interpretation ends up pressing for changes and accommodations in the system itself, becoming incorporated into its functioning. The case bears directly on the debate on platforms because it shows, in concrete terms, that purposes are neither the destiny nor the essence of the artifact. They can be disputed in practice, rewritten by uses, and later consolidated by design modifications. This type of reorientation helps explain why “community” and “participation” are not mere social externalities, but dimensions that can shift the very teleology of a network and, later, be reabsorbed into arrangements of governance and monetization. “Users ‘hacked’ the network in which they were inserted and altered its functioning, introducing human communication where only the centralized distribution of information had been planned.” Feenberg(Feenberg, 1999, p. 126) ↩
