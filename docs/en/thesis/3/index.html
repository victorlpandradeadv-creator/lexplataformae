3 Technology, platforms and the techno-media economic order of digital consumption

See this section in the PDF

This section in the layers of the site

  • Foundations · Opening of chapter 3

Epigraph

Christina Aguilera, Birds of Prey (2010). What it announces · Read the epigraph in the PDF.

p. 83

Contemporary digital consumption can no longer be adequately understood as the simple acquisition of a thing or the contracting of a one-off service. In many ecosystems, the experience takes the form of permanence in a governed environment, in which the interface organizes access, induces choices, limits alternatives and distributes rewards or sanctions. The decisive shift occurs when mediation ceases to be perceived as a neutral channel and comes to operate as a form of organization of experience. In this regime, technique and media constitute practical ambiences: they frame the possible, define the relevant and stabilize certain conducts as normal through repetition, convenience and design.

This ambience is also inseparable from an economic architecture of its own. Digital platforms are not limited to connecting users and offers. They organize markets, administer access, structure dependencies and convert circulation into continuous extraction of value. Infrastructure, data, interface and operating rules come to compose a single arrangement, in which performance is prolonged, the bond is renewed by frictions and incentives, and the capture of attention and diffuse productivity is part of the ordinary functioning of the service. Economic rationality materializes in design, in curation and in execution protocols, so that competition comes to be fought also over the control of ecosystems, exit costs and the administration of visibility.

To describe this background without turning it into mere sociological impression, three conceptual sets are mobilized, each with its own function. The first reconstructs the passage of technique and media from tools to environments of life, in which efficiency, automatism and integration act as criteria of social organization, without eliminating the institutional dispute over design and governance. On this axis, Jacques Ellul28, Neil Postman29 p. 84 and Willem H. Vanderburg30 provide the diagnosis of the techno-media ambience, while Andrew Feenberg31 introduces the critical key needed to set aside determinism and preserve the controllability of technical choices.

The second set reconstructs the political economy of platforms as an arrangement of extraction, toll and dependence, in which digital infrastructures function as private territories of market administration, coordination of supply and capture of distributed value. Yanis Varoufakis32 and Ladislau Dowbor33 offer the main lenses of this reconstruction, in dialogue with the critique of political economy and with categories of Karl Marx34 mobilized to clarify the technical and economic form of extraction in digital environments.

The third set operates as a bridge between political economy and the semiotics of consumption, describing how value, belonging and recognition become organizing components of experience and practical conditions for stabilizing extraction. At this point, the anthropology of consumption, drawn from Mary Douglas35, Baron Isherwood36, Arjun p. 85 Appadurai37, Igor Kopytoff38 and Grant McCraken39, makes it possible to understand that consumed objects circulate not only on account of utility or price, but through regimes of meaning that sustain attachment, distinction and permanence.

This path yields a structural framework capable of making the legal problem visible without depending on impressionistic descriptions of particular cases. When technique operates as environment and the platform operates as market architecture, the consumer relationship comes to be traversed by regimes of access, data, interface and technical and legal exclusivities that condition delivery, enjoyment and the effective space of choice. It is this background that supports the formulation of categories capable of rendering attributable and controllable the losses and asymmetries that, under a strictly material ontology of consumption, would tend to appear as “system change”, “normal update” or “inevitable service event”.

Notes

  1. Doctor of Law from the Université de Bordeaux, Jacques Ellul was a French jurist, philosopher, sociologist and historian recognized for his pioneering critical analysis of technology. He is credited with influencing contemporary philosophers, sociologists and thinkers such as Neil Postman and Willem H. Vanderburg, and remains an important reference in debates on ethics, sustainability and technology. Among his works, the book La Technique ou l'Enjeu du siècle (“A Técnica e o Desafio do Século” in the Brazilian edition), originally published in 1954, stands out for the purposes of study in this research. ↩
  2. Doctor of Education from Columbia University, Neil Postman was an American educator, communication theorist and cultural critic. He is widely recognized for his incisive analyses of the impact of communication technologies and media on contemporary society. His works explore the connections between culture, education, media and human values, often adopting a critical perspective toward unbridled technological progress. ↩
  3. Emeritus of the Departments of Civil Engineering and Sociology and of the School of Environmental Studies at the University of Toronto, Willem H. Vanderburg was, between 1973 and 1978, a postdoctoral fellow of the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) at the Université de Bordeaux. During his postdoctoral period, Vanderburg Ellul(Ellul, 2015, [p. 1]) was supervised by Professor Jacques Ellul. ↩
  4. Doctor of Philosophy from the University of California, San Diego, Andrew Feenberg is an American philosopher recognized for having formulated and developed a “critical theory of technology”, in dialogue with the Frankfurt School tradition and with the social critique of modern technocratism. Professor emeritus of the School of Communication at Simon Fraser University, where he was also Canada Research Chair in Philosophy of Technology, Feenberg combines philosophy, social theory and science and technology studies to argue that technologies are not neutral and that their designs incorporate values and disputes, which opens space for democratic interventions in technical development. ↩
  5. Doctor of Economics from the University of Essex, Yanis Varoufakis is a Greek researcher, economist and former Minister of Finance. He is recognized for his contributions to political economy and game theory and for his critical analysis of global capitalism. ↩
  6. Doctor of Economics from the Szkoła Główna Handlowa w Warszawie (Central School of Planning and Statistics of Warsaw), Ladislau Dowbor is a Brazilian researcher and economist of Polish origin. He is recognized for his contributions in the fields of political economy, sustainable development, social inequality and public policy. ↩
  7. Doctor of Philosophy from the University of Jena, Karl Marx was a German philosopher, political economist, social theorist and journalist. He is widely recognized as one of the founders of the structural critique of modern capitalism, having decisively influenced contemporary legal, economic and sociological thought. ↩
  8. Doctor of Social Anthropology from the University of Oxford, Mary Douglas was a British anthropologist associated with the Durkheimian tradition of social anthropology. She is known for her studies on classification, symbolism, purity, pollution and risk, and particularly recognized for her treatment of goods as social language and as support for the organization of value, boundaries and belonging. ↩
  9. An economist specializing in consumer behavior, Baron Isherwood is recognized for his work with the United Kingdom's Department of Health and Social Security and for his collaboration with Mary Douglas on the work The World of Goods, originally published in 1979. ↩
  10. Doctor of Anthropology from the University of Chicago, Arjun Appadurai is an Indian-American anthropologist and researcher known for his studies on globalization and cultural circulation. He is recognized for his contributions to the anthropology of material culture and of regimes of value, having worked at institutions such as the University of Pennsylvania and New York University, where he became professor emeritus. ↩
  11. Doctor of Anthropology from Northwestern University, Igor Kopytoff was an anthropologist and professor associated with the University of Pennsylvania, whose work was marked by research in economic anthropology and material culture. He is recognized for his perspective on the cultural biography of objects to explain how status, meaning and value are transformed over time through social processes of circulation, singularization and (de)commoditization. ↩
  12. Doctor of Anthropology from the University of Chicago, Grant McCracken is a Canadian anthropologist and researcher known for his studies on consumer culture and the circulation of meaning in goods and brands, notable for describing how cultural meaning circulates among the social world, goods and consumers through instruments such as advertising, the fashion system and consumption rituals. ↩
