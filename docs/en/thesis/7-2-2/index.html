7.2.2 On IOCs of infrastructure and intermediation

See this section in the PDF

This section in the layers of the site

  • Platforms · Platform family

p. 760

The incidence of the regime of the Immaterial Object of Consumption on infrastructure and intermediation platforms is defined neither by the final object traded nor by the traditional economic classification of the sector in which the platform operates. It rests on the governance structure that organizes the very possibility of exchange, encounter, circulation and p. 761 comparison in a digitally administered environment. In this family, the utility supplied is not exhausted by the presence of a good, a piece of content, an advertiser or a user who can be singled out at one end of the relationship. What is offered, in legally relevant terms, is an infrastructure of visibility, reputation, curation, intermediation and logistics without which the final object does not become economically legible, socially rankable or practically accessible on the terms in which the platform itself presents it.

This point calls for dogmatic care because the recurrent self-description of these platforms tends to reduce them to the function of merely bringing parties together. In their formal language, they often appear as neutral meeting spaces between seller and buyer, between sender and receiver, between advertiser and audience, between content producers and the public. This description, although partly true at a superficial level, is insufficient to capture the real structure of supply. Platforms of this kind do not merely connect. They select, rank, highlight, hide, bring closer, delay, prioritize and reorganize the way in which the reality of the environment becomes perceptible to the user. The market, the offer and even the social relevance of what circulates within them reach the consumer and the user through private filters of visibility, salience and intelligibility.

This family of platforms must be treated as one of suppliers of semantic infrastructures for the exchange environments themselves, since their users do not consume only products sold by third parties, posts displayed in a feed or content that appears on their screen. Before that, they consume private frames that define what will be seen as most relevant, most reliable, most comparable, safest, most popular or most worthy of attention. Enjoyment, in this case, is strongly governed by the interface. Rankings, reputation, highlighting, recommendation, prioritization and modulation of reach are not peripheral elements. They are part of the functional utility of the ecosystem and directly influence the formation of expectations, the assessment of risk and the possibility of rational choice.

In marketplaces, this logic is especially clear. Mercado Livre's Terms and Conditions of Use describe an ecosystem in which the platform organizes not only the publication of listings, but also rules of use, visibility, intermediation, payments, reputation, logistics and dispute resolution, placing the buying and selling experience in an environment densely governed by private infrastructure MERCADO LIVRE(Mercado Livre, 2026). The same holds for Magalu's Purchase and Sale Terms, in which the platform regulates the conditions for selling in the marketplace, even distinguishing its own offers from those of partners, while reserving for itself the organization of the environment in which such offers will be p. 762 perceived, compared and consumed MAGAZINE LUIZA(Magazine Luiza, [202-?]). In both cases, the platform does more than host listings. It administers criteria for the presentation, trust, prioritization and legibility of the market accessible to the consumer.

The dogmatic consequence is significant. The consumer does not choose in a vacuum, before a transparent and symmetrical universe of offers. They choose within a field already pre-interpreted by the platform. The prominent position of an item, a seller's appearance of reliability, the way reviews are displayed, the distinction between sponsored and organically prioritized products, the integration with payment and logistics, the organization of categories and the way alternatives are displayed are all elements that materially affect the consumption experience. The platform therefore produces the perceptible market itself. It transforms a chaotic multiplicity of offers into an administered semantic environment, in which the comparability of alternatives reaches the consumer already filtered by criteria they do not control and often cannot even reconstruct in a verifiable way.

This mechanism is not limited to e-commerce in the strict sense. On social networks, the same functional structure reappears in a different form. Meta's Terms of Service describe an environment in which the use of Facebook is subject to private rules on permanence, visibility, sharing, moderation and organization of the content in circulation, so that the user's experience is mediated by internal criteria for distributing and managing social interaction FACEBOOK(Facebook, [202-?]). X's Rules and policies, in turn, show a platform likewise oriented by private discipline over circulation, authenticity, safety and standards of participation, which shows that visibility, permanence and reputation do not appear as neutral data, but as results of concentrated governance X CORP(X Corp, [202-?]). Here too, the platform does not merely “let appear” content produced by third parties. It administers the way in which this content enters the user's field of perception and how its relevance is socially stabilized.

This parallel between marketplaces and social networks does not seek to erase obvious sectoral differences between buying and selling, on the one hand, and the social circulation of content, on the other. What matters, for the typology developed here, is the functional equivalence of the predominant mechanism. In both cases, the platform governs the environment in which things appear. In both, it administers salience, reputation, comparability and intelligibility. In both, the user depends on a private interface that pre-organizes the space in which the decision will take place, whether that decision is a purchase, a choice between sellers, a reputational endorsement, a click, an interaction or the very formation of judgment about what is socially relevant. The utility supplied, therefore, is not reduced to the final object circulating in the environment. It includes the very p. 763 architecture that transforms circulation into ordered experience and converts an abundance of possibilities into a governed field of perception and choice.

It is precisely here that the central vulnerability of this subtype emerges. It is semiotic vulnerability intensified by structural dependence on the interface as the producer of accessible reality. The user faces not only information asymmetry in a quantitative sense. They face asymmetry in the constitution of the relevant field of decision. The platform defines what stands out, what is hidden, what seems most reliable, what acquires the appearance of consensus, what appears comparable and what becomes practically invisible. In such environments, the supplier's promise lies not only in the explicit discourse of neutrality, efficiency or convenience. It also lies in the implicit promise that the market or social circulation is intelligible. When this intelligibility is governed by opaque criteria, undisclosed internal interests or non-auditable ranking standards, enjoyment is affected in its very core of legibility.

The evidence suited to this type of conflict requires a more careful institutional reconstruction than the one normally mobilized in classic litigation over an isolated offer. It is not enough to examine the final product purchased or the individual content removed, prioritized or hidden. The architecture of the experience must be reconstructed. Depending on the case, this includes the way offers are presented, the distinction between organic and paid results, the role of reviews and reputations, the integration between visibility and logistics, the reliability signals provided by the interface, the context in which the content appears, the relevant moderation criteria and the mechanisms by which the platform induces a perception of market, consensus or priority. On social networks, this reconstruction may also involve examining how the feed, recommendation, reach and reputational management condition the very perception of what is in circulation. In marketplaces, it may involve the way in which ranking, highlighting, seller classification and sponsorship logic produce a perceptible market that has already been preselected.

This subtype has recurrent blind spots that must be avoided. The first is the tendency to treat the interface as a mere visual support, and not as a constitutive part of supply. The second is the tendency to imagine that the platform would be legally answerable only for what it said expressly in a clause or in advertising, as if the effective governance of visibility were legally irrelevant. The third is the tendency to reduce the dispute to the final act, such as the frustrated purchase, the removal of a piece of content or the alleged preference for a given offer, without investigating the private structure of salience that conditioned the user's entire experience. These blind spots are especially dangerous p. 764 because they allow the platform to preserve, under an appearance of technical neutrality, decisive power over the material conditions of choice and appearance.

The possible dogmatic consequence, in this environment, tends to revolve around procedural duties of controllability over interface governance. When the platform organizes the very perceptible field of exchange or social circulation, it is not enough to discuss only liability for the final object or for a third party's isolated conduct. It becomes necessary to examine, from the standpoint of the IOC, whether there was adequate information about the nature of the intermediation, whether the distinction between organic and sponsored priority was materially intelligible, whether the reputation displayed was functionally verifiable, whether the opacity of the criteria compromised relevant comparability and whether the interface itself produced deceptiveness, obfuscation or structural asymmetry incompatible with the consumer-law regime. In more serious cases, especially when the platform acts at once as arbiter of the environment and as an economic agent with an interest in its outcomes, the opacity of ranking may also connect with broader abusive practices and with competition repercussions of their own.

In this family, open auditability and functional equivalence operate as cross-cutting filters. Open auditability requires minimum institutional verifiability over the relevant criteria of presentation, prioritization, moderation, reputation and circulation, without this implying unrestricted opening of internal systems. What is required is enough intelligibility to understand how the environment organizes the user's experience and how certain choices by the platform affect the formation of expectations, the comparability of alternatives and the possibility of contestation. Functional equivalence, in turn, applies when changes to the intermediation regime, the logic of visibility, the reputational criteria or the rules of circulation significantly degrade the utility legitimately incorporated into the relationship, without compatible restoration. This may occur both in e-commerce and in the social circulation of content, whenever the platform substantially alters the functional role of the infrastructure it offered as an environment of exchange, reach or recognition.

The subtype thus shows that the infrastructure and intermediation platform is not a mere neutral corridor through which previously formed wills circulate. It is an agent in building the field in which these wills become possible, perceptible and actionable. That is why the IOC regime applies with particular force in this environment. What is controlled is not only the correctness of the final object traded or of the content ultimately presented. What is controlled is the semiotic governance of the very space of p. 765 circulation, that is, the way in which the supplier administers relevance, trust, comparability and appearance. When this administration takes place under structural opacity, non-verifiable criteria or implicit promises of neutrality incompatible with the ecosystem's practice, the application of consumer law ceases to be a rhetorical expansion and becomes a simple requirement of institutional controllability of supply.
