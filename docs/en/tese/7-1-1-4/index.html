7.1.1.4 On the parameters of advertising and offer as controllable management of meaning

See this section in the PDF

  • Hypercuratorial anticipation

p. 724

In ecosystems in which enjoyment is preceded, prepared and guided by continuous cycles of anticipation, advertising and offer cannot be treated as an external, ornamental or legally neutral layer of consumption. In many immaterial objects of consumption, what is acquired is not only a statically described functional utility, but an experience framed in advance by signs, promises, narratives of distinction, projections of value and expectations administered by the supplier itself. The offer, in these cases, is not limited to the final purchase statement. It is built progressively through an economy of signals that precedes enjoyment, organizes its horizon of meaning and conditions the way the consumer interprets what is being acquired.

This particularity demands a material reading of advertising. Teasers, showcases, promotional events, announcement cycles, previews, countdowns, suggestions of rarity, insinuated equivalences, promises of prestige, belonging or performance and narrative models of scarcity do not operate, in these environments, as mere accessory persuasion. They make up the offer program insofar as they take part in the formation of legitimate expectation and in the determination of the value attributed to the object. What is presented to the consumer is not merely a piece of content or an isolated feature, but an experience of enjoyment shaped in advance by a carefully curated regime of signs. For this reason, the legal analysis of the offer cannot remain restricted to the most immediate literal statement, as if the relevant promise were exhausted in a sentence formally detached from the rest of the communicational environment.

This perspective makes it possible to recognize forms of misleadingness that cannot be captured solely by the classical logic of outright falsehood. In immaterial objects of consumption, inducement p. 725 into error may result from constructed ambiguity, from strategically distributed relevant omission or from a mismatch between the overall meaning of the promise and the real conditions of enjoyment under administered mutability. Advertising may suggest stability where there is structural precariousness, equivalence where there is relevant asymmetry, rarity where there is programmed artificiality, freedom of use where there is asymmetric reversibility, continuity where there is intense mutability. In these cases, the nonconformity lies not only in an isolated factual lie, but in the management of meaning by which the supplier produces a favorable reading of the object and shifts the cognitive costs of actual comprehension onto the consumer.

Hypercuratorial anticipation is thus part of the very legal economy of the offer. When the supplier administers cycles of expectation, projects models of value and structures the visibility of what will be perceived as central or secondary in the acquisition, it is not merely announcing something that will exist later. It is taking part in the very shaping of the economically enjoyed object. This becomes even more evident in environments in which the utility consumed has a relevant symbolic, relational or reputational dimension. In such cases, use value and meaning value intertwine. The consumer acquires not only access or functionality, but also a place in a network of distinction, recognition, belonging or identity projection modeled in advance by the supplier's communication. Advertising, in this context, is no longer a mere external discourse about the object and becomes a material part of its regime of enjoyment.

The dogmatic consequence is clear. Control of the offer must bear on what was promised as value and as function, on what was concealed as cost, limitation or condition of precariousness, and on the correspondence between this semiotic frame and the real enjoyment made available to the consumer. This requires understanding advertising as controllable management of meaning. It is not a matter of denying the persuasive function proper to economic activity, but of recognizing that, in platform-governed ecosystems, persuasion may turn into a technique of structural shaping of expectation and, for that reason, must be subjected to the limits of the consumer-law regime. Whenever promotional communication takes part in determining what the consumer legitimately understands he is acquiring, it is incorporated into the normative field of the offer and becomes susceptible to control for misleadingness, ambiguity or relevant omission.

This parameter also prevents the examination of advertising from being reduced to the isolated reading of sentences or individual pieces. The legally relevant promise is not always concentrated in a single statement. It frequently emerges from the articulation between multiple elements of the communicational environment, some explicit, others insinuated, still others p. 726 merely suggested by salience, timing, symbolic association or strategic repetition. Control must, for this reason, capture the overall regime of signification produced by the supplier. This requirement is particularly important in immaterial objects of consumption whose enjoyment depends on visibility, reputation, collective recognition or insertion in an ecosystem of symbolic value. In them, the mismatch between promise and delivery may not appear as an evident technical defect, but as a relevant frustration of the symbolic or relational position presented as part of the core of the acquisition.

For this reason too, advertising takes on a function of semiotic hygiene of the offer. The expression is important because it shifts the debate from the simplistic idea of advertising as an inevitably exaggerated adornment to a more demanding understanding of the consumption environment. When communication is designed to produce ambiguity, artificially enhance the favorable, obscure the onerous or push the essential into zones of low visibility, it ceases to be a mere persuasive frame and becomes part of the supply program itself. In this condition, it may take part not only in the formation of inadequate expectation, but in the very abusive teleology of the ecosystem, above all when it is combined with later mechanisms of mutability, artificial scarcity, manufactured urgency or deliberate opacity.

The parameter, therefore, requires that offer and advertising be read in light of their concrete function in the governance of enjoyment. What matters is not only what was formally said, but what was legally promised through the management of signs, expectations and saliences. Where prior communication relevantly shapes the reading of the object, forms part of the perception of its value and conditions the consumer's adhesion, there will be an offer in a materially controllable sense. And where this management of meaning operates to produce relevant ambiguity, shift cognitive costs, conceal precariousness or artificially inflate perceived utility, there will be sufficient grounds for the application of the protective regime, not as abstract repression of advertising, but as control of the promise in environments of governed enjoyment.
