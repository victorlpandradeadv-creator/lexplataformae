6.4.2 On the techniques of capture by scarcity, risk and dependence

See this section in the PDF

This section in the layers of the site

  • Dogmatics · Sections of chapter 6

p. 669

p. 670 The techniques of capture by scarcity, risk and dependence are of interest here neither as a moral catalog of “aggressive strategies” nor as a sociological inventory of engagement mechanisms. Their interest is dogmatic and evidentiary. In IOC ecosystems, exploitation rarely presents itself in the clean form of a scandalous clause or of an advertisement that is false in isolation. It tends to emerge as a program of functioning, that is, as an articulation between multiple mechanisms that compress reflection time, reorganize expectation, increase exit cost, hinder reversal and make enjoyment progressively dependent on the very environment that governs it. What is sought, then, is a controllable vocabulary for recognizing recurring patterns of capture, without psychologizing the consumer and without dissolving the problem into vague impressions about design.

This vocabulary is necessary precisely because exploitation, in continuous digital services, usually operates by accumulation. The consumer is rarely constrained by a single expedient. What occurs, more frequently, is the combination of staged scarcity, manufactured urgency, promise of progression, diffuse threat of loss, gradual downgrades of utility and symbolic anchoring of permanence. Seen in isolation, these elements may seem trivial, tolerable or even inherent to the ecosystem. Considered together, they reveal a clearer rationale of capture of choice and of administration of tolerance to harm. It is for this reason that atomization must be avoided. The exploitative program does not disappear just because it is distributed in small repeated incidences.

The first family of these techniques is that of artificial scarcity and manufactured urgency. Its mechanism is well known. Short windows, accelerated rotation of offers, countdown timers, “limited editions”, ephemeral events and temporary benefits are used to shift the decision from the field of rational comparison to that of reactive response. The legally relevant effect lies not only in making the purchase more attractive, but in deliberately compressing reflection time and inducing hasty consent, often when essential information remains hidden, dispersed or hierarchically downgraded. The problem is not the isolated use of temporality in the market, but its articulation with opacity and with the practical impossibility of comparison. The minimum sign of proof, here, does not depend on intuition about manipulation. It appears in time-remaining screens, in a history of repeated temporary campaigns, in programmed rotation of offers and in the very visual architecture that anticipates benefit and postpones relevant conditions.

The second family is that of temporal pressure through event structures and cycles of promise. The mechanism ceases to operate only through one-off rarity and comes to organize a directed sequence of anticipation, purchase, partial delivery, frustration and repurchase. Teasers, pre-sales, p. 671 conditioned progressions, passes, serialized rewards and temporary goals build a trajectory in which value ceases to reside only in the object and comes to reside in the cost of “falling behind”. The relevant legal effect is the conversion of enjoyment into a directed path, in which giving up ceases to mean a mere interruption of consumption and comes to represent loss of accumulated value, regression of position and discontinuity of an expectation already installed. In continuing relationships, this dynamic gains special relevance because the consumer finds themselves progressively more captive to the maintenance of the relationship and less free to resist new impositions by the supplier, which renews its power of influence precisely through the continuity of the relationship Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 109)760. The minimum sign of proof emerges in progression structures, event rules, time requirements, modifications introduced mid-cycle and comparison between the initial promise and the actual delivery along the trajectory.

This logic may acquire additional gravity when the ecosystem engages audiences especially exposed to the pressure to stay and to the repeated promise of reward. In electronic game environments, keeping engagement going over long periods through new content, continuous character development and appeals to collective dynamics reinforces commitment to staying and broadens tolerance of opaque retention structures. The combination of promised progression, group belonging and marketing built into the play environment shows that capture does not operate only on the abstract individual, but may radiate effects onto children, adolescents and the family unit itself Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 19; p. 10-11; p. 17)761. This does not shift the axis of the subsection, but confirms that these techniques have enough materiality to exceed ordinary commercial persuasion.

p. 672 The third family is that of the manufacture of risk and the permanent threat of loss. Here the mechanism depends not so much on the promise of gain as on the continuous administration of the possibility of loss. Programmed expiry, unilateral discontinuation, opaque banning, functional downgrades, loss of access to account or inventory, removals without a verifiable trail and unintelligible challenge regimes compose an environment in which trust is replaced by vigilance. The consumer ceases to choose in a space of minimum stability and comes to consume under a diffuse threat of deterioration, of sanction or of partial disappearance of what already makes up their position of enjoyment. The relevant legal effect lies precisely in this replacement of trust by discipline. Pressure ceases to be a mere psychological datum and becomes part of the legally relevant relational framework itself, because the supplier exercises its power in an environment in which the continuity of the relationship becomes a good in itself for the consumer and the threat of rupture or impoverishment of this continuity comes to operate as a method of subjection Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 285-286)762. The minimum sign of proof appears in applied policies, sanction communications, absence of verifiable reasoning, decisional inconsistencies and concentration of evidentiary trails in the supplier, which connects this family directly to the problem of open auditability.

The fourth family is that of the programmed degradation of use value and the downgrading of equivalence. Its mechanism is not mere change, but oriented change. Nerfs, reconfigurations, balancing adjustments, gradual suppressions of function, shifts of utility to new layers of acquisition and ordinary redefinitions of equivalence operate as techniques of extraction by erosion. The object does not leave the scene completely. It remains there, but worse, weaker, less central, less useful or less equivalent to what justified the acquisition. The relevant legal effect lies in the violation of the legitimate expectation of continued enjoyment. The consumer does not lose merely “a feature”; they lose functional substance of what they bought and is frequently pushed to seek substitutes within the same ecosystem. The minimum sign of proof requires functional comparison between before and after, examination of relevant versions, identification of the core of utility affected and assessment of the object's role in the experience, exactly as the standard of functional value equivalence already requires. Legal p. 673 relevance, here, does not depend on total suppression, but on relevant and repeated erosion of enjoyment.

The fifth family is that of existential captivity and identity or social dependence. Here capture is anchored in the symbolic value produced by the system itself. Inventories, titles, history, ranking, rarities, emblems, status signs and marks of trajectory function as ties between the IOC and the consumer's social position in the ecosystem. The relevant legal effect is evident. The loss or degradation of the object ceases to operate only as a patrimonial reduction and comes to bear on belonging, recognition, memory of participation and symbolic identity, in the terms already set for the reference legal interests. The minimum sign of proof does not lie in a subjective account of sadness or frustration, but in the objective demonstration of how the system itself attributes social value to the object, through internal markers, visibility, participation requirements, access restrictions and reputational effects. When the environment attributes this value in an institutionalized way, it also institutionalizes the exit cost and raises the consumer's tolerance to capture.

This fifth family also helps to understand why certain retention and reward techniques cannot be read merely as innocent “engagement” tactics. In contexts of strong dependence on permanence, the cycles of challenge, reward and progression may intensify ties of permanence to a degree that goes beyond mere playful prolongation and comes to affect the user more deeply and, in sensitive situations, the family environment itself. The legal interest here is not to pathologize every intense relationship with the game, but to recognize that the dynamic of retention and reward may function as an objective mechanism for raising the exit cost and broadening dependence on the continuity of the relationship Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 20; p. 21; p. 23-24)763. The usefulness of this observation, in this item, is only to reinforce that dependence and capture are not metaphorical abstractions when the very design of the ecosystem organizes permanence as a socially charged necessity.

Considered together, these five families show that abusive teleology need not appear as a single, dramatic and conspicuous gesture. It may manifest itself through p. 674 the accumulation of smaller incidences, each of low perceptibility when isolated, but all converging on the same functional result: compressing the consumer's practical freedom, increasing their tolerance to loss, hindering comparison, raising the exit cost and turning the continuity of the relationship into an instrument of extraction. For this reason, the vocabulary proposed here is predominantly descriptive-operational. Its role is not yet to close the definitive dogmatic qualification, but to make visible the exploitative program in its recurring forms of manifestation.

The internal consequence of the subsection is, then, quite precise. Scarcity, urgency, serialized promise, threat of loss, functional erosion and symbolic dependence are not unlawful for existing in the abstract nor for appearing in isolation. They become teleologically abusive when they operate in combination with opacity and permanent asymmetry, in a stable and replicable pattern, orienting enjoyment toward ends incompatible with good faith, balance and controllability of supply. Seen in this way, they produce fragmentary injuries that may seem small when examined separately, but that, reconstructed together, reveal economically relevant extraction and progressive reconfiguration of the consumer's position in the ecosystem. What is prepared, from here on, is not a category competing with abusive teleology, but the functional repertoire through which it becomes recognizable before being explicitly brought back to the framing doors of the CDC.

Notes

  1. Capture techniques founded on scarcity, risk and dependence constitute legally relevant forms of exploitation of consumer vulnerability in continuing contractual relationships, taking advantage of established ties and of the user's practical need for permanence to broaden the supplier's power of imposition. Certain services and complex long-term relationships bind the consumer in such a way that they become dependent on the maintenance of the contractual relationship itself, on pain of seeing frustrated the expectations that led them to join and remain in the relationship: such relationships “bind the consumer in such a way that, over the years of duration of the complex contractual relationship, they become a 'captive' client of that supplier or chain of suppliers, becoming dependent even on the maintenance of that contractual relationship or they will see all their expectations frustrated.” Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 109). This dependence reduces the consumer's real capacity to resist new impositions, guaranteeing the supplier a “power of imposition” constantly renewed over the course of the long-term obligation Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 109). ↩
  2. Capture techniques based on scarcity, dependence and pressure of permanence may acquire even greater gravity when they bear on children, adolescents and family dynamics mediated by the platform. In ecosystems such as Fortnite, the structure of the game aims to keep players connected for long periods through the continuous promise of new content, characters and game modes, a strategy that “generates long-term expectation among players and commitment to the development of the user's character” Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 19). The attraction of children is reinforced by partnerships with celebrities and brands, turning the play environment into a space of recurring stimulus to consumption, while the multiplayer logic “creates a team spirit forcing the player to continue in the game to help the other players” Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 10-11, 17). ↩
  3. The pressure exerted on the consumer in lasting contractual relationships forms part of the legally relevant relational framework for assessing good faith and abusiveness, ceasing to be a merely psychological or accidental datum. In contracts for increasingly essential services, “pressure” takes on growing relevance in resolving contractual conflicts, such that the exercise of certain rights by the professional in a position of power may constitute abuse of right or an act contrary to the good faith required in legal dealings, this structural-social factor of pressure having new legal relevance in captive relationships Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 285). The legitimate interest of the consumer is directed to the continuity of the contractual relationship, while pressure may lie in the sales method itself, in marketing or in the object of the contract, being reflected in the final engineering of the relationship Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 285-286). ↩
  4. Engagement and retention techniques in electronic games may intensify the dependence and exposure of the family unit itself, going beyond the mere prolongation of playful engagement. The dynamic of challenges and rewards may prevent children and adolescents from facing stress factors, “creating a vicious cycle of anxiety and depression” Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 20). This loss of control is linked to the “neural circuit established in the face of the reward provided”, similar to the way brain reward systems function in chemical dependencies Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 21). The effects radiate onto the family, generating treatment costs, requiring greater attention from parents and guardians and interfering with family income Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 23-24). ↩
