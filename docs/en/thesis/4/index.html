Section 4 · pp. 261 to 264

4 Semiotics and governance of enjoyment in continuous immaterial consumption

See this section in the PDF

This section in the layers of the site

  • Foundations · Opening of chapter 4

Epigraph

MARINA, Oh No! (2010). What it announces · Read the epigraph in the PDF.

p. 261

The techno-media economic order of digital consumption does not operate only as a market structure, infrastructure and continuous extraction of value. It projects itself, with equal force, onto the plane of signs and attention, where value and delivery are no longer perceived as stable attributes of a “good” and come instead to be constituted by administered visibility, reputation, metrics and belonging. In platform ecosystems, enjoyment is not reduced to using a feature. It involves participating in a governed symbolic grammar, in which prestige, rarity, recognition and experience are part of the content delivered as perceived, and in which the promise is fulfilled through codes of reading and through equivalences built within the environment.

To make this regime knowable, semiotics is treated here as a material dimension of the legal problem, not as interpretive ornament. The consumption promise is stabilized by signs, interfaces and regimes of representation that define what “counts” as delivery and which equivalences are socially recognized as value. In this vein, the contribution of Umberto Eco280 makes it possible to understand how codes and conventions make the performance legible and organize the consumer's expectations, whereas Guy Debord281 brings out the centrality of the spectacle and of the circulation of images in shaping desire and adherence. Jean Baudrillard282 completes the axis by showing how, in attention economies, simulacrum and hyperreality shift the center of the commodity to its symbolic efficacy, so that consumption presents itself as access to experiences and positions, and not merely as the acquisition of discrete utilities.

p. 262 This semiotic plane, however, does not operate in a vacuum. It is governed. The platform appears as an instance of private administration that organizes enjoyment through architecture, rules and enforcement, defining access, permanence and visibility. This includes both explicit decisions, such as policies and sanctions, and decisions embedded in design, in curation and in the metrics that select what will be seen, rewarded and reproduced. Governance, in this sense, is not an “accessory” to consumption, but an operational condition of the performance itself. The contribution of Julie E. Cohen283 is drawn on to describe the platform as a form of organizing power that produces dependencies, structures conduct and naturalizes patterns of participation through technical and institutional means not exhausted by the contract.

The asymmetry that matters on this plane is not exhausted by a deficit of available information. It takes shape as an inequality of codes and language regimes that condition what the consumer can perceive, compare and contest within the environment. Luis Alberto Warat284 and Maurizio Gnerre285 are drawn on for this precise function, developing and fixing the perspective according to which vulnerability may arise from the gap between operational languages and languages of enjoyment, between vocabularies of promise and vocabularies of control, and between system metrics and expectations of experience. Semiotic vulnerability thus remains a structural category, tied to intelligibility and agency, and not an individual pathology or a mere absence of “enlightenment”.

The legal problem deepens when this governance comes to have total effects on the consumer's presence and reputation within the ecosystem. The prudential reference of Samuel p. 263 D. Warren II286 and Louis D. Brandeis287 serves as a methodological model for recalibrating categories in the face of technical mutations that reconfigure the scope of rights and expectations, preventing the legal response from remaining trapped in material analogies that no longer reach the real experience of enjoyment. Next, Byung-Chul Han288 is drawn on to name psychopolitical dimensions of adherence and induced self-government, in which the bond is sustained by the pressure of performance, comparison and continuous validation. Eros Roberto Grau289 helps to recognize that normativity can operate as an effective order imposed by an organization endowed with power, making it possible to characterize as practical governance what presents itself as a “condition of use” or “internal rule” but produces structural effects on ways of life and social positions in the digital environment.

In this context, the asymmetry between supplier and consumer is not merely informational. It is structural, semiotic and institutional. It is structural because it stems from concentrated control over infrastructure, rules and channels of visibility. It is semiotic because it bears on the production of the very codes of value, reputation and delivery equivalence. It is institutional because it is stabilized by private procedures of adjudication, sanction and unilateral modulation, often opaque and difficult to contest. The relevant vulnerability is not exhausted by a “lack of information”. It involves an asymmetry of intelligibility and agency in an environment in which the consumer depends, in order to enjoy and to exist socially, on grammars, metrics and access regimes administered by the supplier.

The aim here is to make explicit how the capture of meaning and governed participation become constitutive components of continuous immaterial consumption, making legally visible losses and frictions that do not appear as classic nonperformance, but as degradation of enjoyment, a lowering of perceived value and the erosion of belonging through p. 264 technical and governance decisions. Semiotic vulnerability is thereby stabilized as a structural asymmetry of intelligibility and agency in platform ecosystems, and the mechanisms by which enjoyment comes to depend on regimes of visibility, metrics and private sanction are outlined, to a degree sufficient to allow later legal qualification without relying on a demonstrative empirical narrative.

Notes

  1. A graduate in Philosophy of the Università di Torino, Umberto Eco was an Italian semiotician, philosopher, medievalist and cultural critic, known both for his academic contribution to semiotics and the theory of interpretation and for his public profile as a writer. In 1975, he took up the chair of Semiotics at the Università di Bologna, where he also held academic leadership positions in communication and performing arts studies. ↩
  2. A graduate of the undergraduate program in Philosophy at the Université de Paris (Sorbonne), Guy Debord was a Marxist theorist, filmmaker and one of the leading figures of the Situationist International, an artistic and political movement active from the 1950s to the 1970s. Although his career was not academic in the strict sense, he is recognized for his role in the critique of consumer society, of symbolic alienation and of the use of the image as an instrument of social domination. His work offers a radical analysis of the logic of the spectacle as a form of organization of life under late capitalism, and of particular relevance to this research is the book La Société du Spectacle (“A Sociedade do Espetáculo”, in the Brazilian edition), originally published in 1967. ↩
  3. Holder of a doctorate in Sociology from the Université Paris X (Nanterre), Jean Baudrillard was a French philosopher, sociologist and cultural theorist with broad influence in communication studies, postmodern critique and the philosophy of language. He is known for his critique of the culture of simulation and of the dissolution of reality into regimes of signs, especially in advanced consumer societies. ↩
  4. A graduate of Harvard-Radcliffe with a J.D. from Harvard Law School, Julie E. Cohen is an American legal scholar and a leading reference in law and technology, with prominent work on privacy, surveillance, intellectual property and platform governance. She is a professor at Georgetown University Law Center, where she researches how informational infrastructures and legal arrangements reconfigure institutions and power relations in the contemporary economy. ↩
  5. Holder of a doctorate in Law from the Universidad de Buenos Aires, Luis Alberto Warat was an Argentine jurist and legal philosopher based in Brazil, with a decisive influence on Brazilian graduate legal education through a critical and avowedly interdisciplinary approach. His work challenges traditional dogmatics and draws on epistemology, language, semiology and legal pedagogy, while also engaging with psychoanalysis, art and political ecology. In the work “O direito e sua linguagem”, consulted for this research, Warat worked with Leonel Severo Rocha, who holds a doctorate from the École des Hautes Études en Sciences Sociales (EHESS), Paris, and a postdoctorate in Sociology of Law from the Università degli Studi di Lecce, a Brazilian jurist and researcher in legal sociology and general legal theory, with emphasis on systemic approaches and on the observation of law in contexts of high social complexity. ↩
  6. Holder of a doctorate in Letters and Philosophy from the Università di Roma (La Sapienza), with further training in anthropology at the University of California (Berkeley), Maurizio Gnerre is an Italian linguistic anthropologist known for research in ethnolinguistics and language contact in contexts of colonial and missionary expansion, as well as studies on language ideologies and the semantics of unwritten indigenous languages. ↩
  7. A law graduate of Harvard Law School, Samuel D. Warren II was an American lawyer and businessman connected to the Boston elite and to the paper industry in Maine. With Louis D. Brandeis he founded the firm Warren & Brandeis (1879), predecessor of today's Nutter, McClennen & Fish. Also with Louis D. Brandeis, he published in 1890 the article The Right to Privacy, which systematizes civil protection against undue intrusion into private life, a protection that became an essential reference in the history of the right to privacy. ↩
  8. A law graduate of Harvard Law School, Louis D. Brandeis was an American lawyer and, later, Associate Justice of the Supreme Court of the United States (1916-1939), known for his influence on progressive legal thought in the early twentieth century. ↩
  9. Holder of a doctorate in Philosophy from the Albert-Ludwigs-Universität Freiburg, Byung-Chul Han is a South Korean philosopher based in Germany and a professor at the Universität der Künste Berlin. His work critically examines the culture of productivity, positivity and self-exploitation in neoliberal society. Reflecting on contemporary psychological exhaustion and the dissolution of the boundaries between life and work, Han offers a critique of capitalist temporality and of invisible forms of domination. ↩
  10. Holder of a doctorate in Law and the livre-docência from the Faculty of Law of USP, Eros Roberto Grau is a Brazilian jurist and professor regarded as one of the central names in Economic Law and in the constitutional reading of the economic order. He was a full professor in the Department of Economic and Financial Law at USP and served as a Justice of the Federal Supreme Court from 2004 to 2010. ↩
