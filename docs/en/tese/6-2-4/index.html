6.2.4 On semiotic vulnerability as a contemporary form of factual vulnerability

See this section in the PDF

Concepts in this section

  • Semiotic vulnerability

This section in the layers of the site

  • Dogmatics · Sections of chapter 6

p. 579

Semiotic vulnerability, in order to have dogmatic utility, cannot be treated as a loose cultural metaphor nor as a judgment about the consumer's psychology. It designates a p. 580 contemporary form of factual vulnerability produced by the way supply is organized in platform environments. In IOCs, the relevant asymmetry does not reside only in the fact that the consumer knows less about the internal characteristics of the system. It arises because the platform controls the very instruments of intelligibility of the relationship, that is, the signs, interfaces, metrics, framings and records through which the consumer forms expectation, assesses enjoyment, perceives downgrading and tries to sustain a challenge. The consumer's vulnerability remains the pillar of the special protection of the CDC, and it is precisely for this reason that semiotic vulnerability must be understood as a contemporary specification of this structural datum, and not as a parallel or extravagant category Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 324; p. 307)650.

This point requires an important distinction. Semiotic vulnerability does not describe lesser individual discernment, psychological naivety or subjective susceptibility of the user. The problem lies in the objective way in which the environment organizes language, visibility, comparison and proof. On platforms, the consumer depends on governed mediations to understand what they acquired, perceive whether the experience was altered and identify whether there was a legally relevant loss. This means that the platform not only supplies the object, but also administers the references through which this object can be perceived and measured. The asymmetry, then, ceases to be merely informational in the classical sense and becomes also semiotic, because the supplier controls the regime of production of meaning of enjoyment itself.

It is precisely this that sustains the category under the CDC. The consumer's vulnerability does not act only as the sociological background of the system. It is normatively articulated with good faith, the duty to inform, transparency and the protection of trust. The consumer's autonomy depends on positive duties to inform, cooperate, act with fairness and care, because without this minimum level of conduct the decision to contract does not come to be rational in a p. 581 legally relevant sense Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 812; p. 1480)651. Semiotic vulnerability is anchored exactly in this gearing. It shows that, in governed environments, the duty to inform cannot be reduced to a bureaucratic dumping of data nor to rituals of acceptance. What is at stake is the production of minimum conditions for effective understanding of enjoyment and of its relevant alterations.

For this reason, informational inadequacy is not limited to pure and simple omission. The supplier can appear transparent and, even so, produce material opacity through excess, dispersion, low practical utility, excessively technical language or communicational organization that frustrates understanding. It is not enough to ensure formal access to information. It must be correct, clear, precise and conspicuous, without useless prolixity or strategic scarcity, and the supplier cannot structure barriers that interfere negatively with the consumer's capacity to understand the terms and conditions of the contracting Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 207; p. 142)652. Again, this allows semiotic vulnerability to be formulated without psychologizing the consumer. The problem is not inner weakness. The problem is a communicational architecture that appears to inform while organizing noise.

From this follows a decisive consequence: in IOCs, the platform controls not only the modulation of the object. It also controls the means by which the modulation can be perceived, named and compared. Shop windows, metrics, rarities, narratives of prestige, p. 582 reputational systems, visual feedbacks, progression histories and interface framings cease to be peripheral elements of the environment and come to make up the intelligibility of the consumed object itself. When these elements are reorganized unilaterally, the relevant loss can be obscured under an appearance of continuity. The consumer keeps nominal access, but loses functional or symbolic utility without finding clear criteria to measure the erosion. It is this type of asymmetry that the category seeks to make visible.

The CDC already offers instruments for this because the problem is not limited to the abstract plane of communication. Abusive practices involve precisely taking advantage of the supplier's position of power to impose behaviors on the consumer in violation of the duties of fairness, collaboration and respect for legitimate expectations. Abusiveness can also arise from the asymmetrical exploitation of the relevant meanings of the consumer relationship, especially when extensive, obscure or surprising clauses compromise the quality of the information and the consent of the adhering party Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 328-329; p. 388)653. Semiotic vulnerability remains, thus, in the trunk of consumer law. It merely names with more precision a current form of exploitation of vulnerability already intuited by the institutes of the system.

This reading also clarifies why the “governed environment” does not compete with the “immaterial product”. In the IOC regime, the governed environment is a condition of legal existence of the consumed product itself. The object is only enjoyable while the platform maintains, administers and modulates access, signs, rules of use and parameters of intelligibility. This means that the environment does not figure as an external frame of the performance, but as a constitutive element of the product as legally consumed. Relevant alterations in the architecture of enjoyment can correspond, in dogmatic terms, to alterations of the object of consumption itself. p. 583 Semiotic vulnerability makes intelligible precisely the asymmetry generated by this arrangement, because the platform simultaneously administers the object and the means of reading the object.

This category also reorganizes classic problems of the CDC. If enjoyment depends on continuous maintenance and modulation, delivery cannot be thought of as a pure one-off act. If utility is reconfigurable, inadequacy is not limited to the ostensible defect of a static thing. If there is no sharp final milestone of execution, the identification of the quality defect needs to dialogue with the real moment in which the degradation becomes perceptible and verifiable. If the promise circulates through signs, advertising, narratives and interfaces, the correspondence between offer and enjoyment cannot be measured only by the literalness of a contractual text. Semiotic vulnerability functions, then, as a category of attribution of the structural asymmetry that justifies reconstructing the duty to inform, trust, adequacy and abusiveness in an opaque and mutable environment.

This problem directly reaches the plane of proof. In technically sophisticated ecosystems, the supplier controls not only the service, but also the documents, the records, the technique and the very intelligibility of the relationship. Often, the consumer's difficulty is not only economic, but factual and technical. The necessary evidence is not in their power. It is with whoever administers the environment, the logs, the parameterizations and the relevant histories.

Facilitating the defense and redistributing the burden of proof become decisive in relationships of this nature. The technological specialization of the service and the dominant position of the supplier make the reversal of the burden of proof, in many cases, the only instrument capable of effectively enabling the protection of the consumer Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 854; p. 283)654. Semiotic vulnerability is, at this point, also evidentiary vulnerability.

The protection of the consumer, then, is not satisfied with formally available information. It requires informed and conscious contracting to a sufficient degree for the consumer to be able to know, decide and use the product or service adequately. The information p. 584 protected by the system is worth its practical utility in compensating vulnerability, and not as a bureaucratic ritual of unloading data Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 211; p. 143)655. On platforms, this means that intelligibility is not communicational ornament. It is a functional presupposition of the very decisional utility of information and, therefore, of the legitimacy of the supply.

The foundational basis of the dogmatic utility of the category is thus identified. Semiotic vulnerability does not replace the standards formulated nor is it confused with them. It identifies the relevant structural asymmetry in IOCs and allows the problem to be brought back to classic categories of the CDC without reducing the controversy to a “mere environmental problem” external to the object. From it, the following steps of the argument become manageable, in particular the requirement of institutional verifiability, the reconstruction of adequacy in a key of functional integrity and a more realistic distribution of the burden of proof in the face of opaque and governed mutations of enjoyment.

Notes

  1. The formulation of semiotic vulnerability, in order to remain dogmatically useful, must be understood as a contemporary development of a structural datum that already organizes the consumer-law microsystem: the consumer's vulnerability as the permanent foundation of the protective interpretation of the CDC. It is not a matter of creating a parallel category, disconnected from the grammar of the system, but of specifying, in the context of platforms, one of the ways in which the imbalance of forces between supplier and consumer manifests itself today. Marques states this quite directly in affirming that, in consumer contracts, "the problem is the flagrant imbalance of forces of the contracting parties. One of the parties is vulnerable (art. 4º. I), it is the weakest pole of the contractual relationship, since it cannot discuss the content of the contract", being left, as a rule, only to accept or reject the predisposed package Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 324). The author adds that the definition of consumer constitutes "the pillar that sustains the special protection", because this exists only by reason of the vulnerability of the consumer party in market relations Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 307). Semiotic vulnerability, in this key, appears as a contemporary form of factual vulnerability, traceable to good faith, the duty to inform, the protection of trust and contractual balance. ↩
  2. The consumer's vulnerability, in the CDC, does not act only as a sociological premise of the protective regime; it is normatively articulated with objective good faith and with the duty to inform, forming an operative core for containing the typical asymmetries of the market. This means that the structural fragility of the consumer is legally confronted through the imposition of positive duties on the supplier, above all at the level of the formation and conduct of the contractual relationship. Marques makes this gearing explicit in highlighting that the idea of "rational autonomy" depends precisely on the incidence of ancillary duties to inform, cooperate, treat the consumer with fairness and care, since only with this "new level of conduct in the market" can one achieve a "truly reflected, autonomous and 'rational" will of the consumer Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 812). In a convergent sense, the author states that the CDC seeks "transparency and harmony in consumer relations", imposing good faith as a requirement to prevent market practices from "making use of the vulnerability of consumers", the duty to inform being "a basic duty of suppliers" Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 1480). This keeps semiotic vulnerability anchored in the normative ground of the microsystem. ↩
  3. In this sense, Wada (20216) allows one to show that informational inadequacy, in consumer law, is not reduced to the pure and simple absence of data. The failure can also arise when the supplier ensures only formal access to information, but does so under conditions that frustrate its practical intelligibility. The problem, then, is not only omitting, but communicating in a materially insufficient way, through excess, dispersion, complexity or low concrete utility. Along these lines, the author expressly states that “it is not enough to ensure formal access to information”, it being necessary that it have the legally required attributes of “correctness, clarity, precision and conspicuousness” Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 207). The requirement is qualitative, not merely quantitative. This key allows one to understand that informational noise also makes vulnerable. Information must be “precise (= not prolix or scarce)”, precisely so that it remains comprehensible and functional to the consumer's decision process Wada(Marques apud Wada, 2016, p. 207). Furthermore, the author observes that the supplier cannot interfere “significantly and negatively with the consumer's capacity to understand the terms or conditions” of the contracting, nor establish “barriers or difficulties” to understanding Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 142). Hence the pertinence of treating semiotic vulnerability as a contemporary form of factual vulnerability: not because the consumer is psychologically weak, but because they can be exposed to communicational architectures that appear transparent while producing material opacity. ↩
  4. The formulation of semiotic vulnerability remains internal to the CDC when it is read from the articulation between vulnerability, good faith, the duty to inform, trust and abusive practices. Abusive practices involve a violation of the supplier's duty of fairness toward the consumer, standing out, in many cases, the exploitation of the supplier's position and power to impose a certain behavior on the consumer. In these cases, there is a violation of the duties of fairness, collaboration and respect for legitimate expectations arising from objective good faith, which includes positive duties of clarification and transparent counseling, as well as a duty to abstain from conduct that takes advantage of vulnerability and affects the consumer's freedom to negotiate Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 328-329). This key is reinforced in the treatment of surprise clauses. Their abusiveness results, even before the breach of balance in the strict sense, from the violation of the duty to inform the consumer, above all in overly extensive or complex contracts entered into by a lay consumer. In these situations, the supplier is bound by a duty of adequate clarification, on pain of informational insufficiency being presumed and the quality of consent being compromised Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 388). The category of semiotic vulnerability, thus, does not abandon the trunk of consumer law; it merely names, with more precision, a contemporary form of factual vulnerability already intuited by its own institutes: the asymmetrical exploitation of the relevant meanings of the consumer relationship. ↩
  5. The CDC's response to contemporary asymmetries is not limited to the material plane of information and abusiveness; it already reaches the procedural plane of proof as well. The specialization and technological sophistication of products and services, added to the consumer's economic or technical weakness, make the reversal of the burden of proof, in many cases, the only resource apt to make the claim succeed. This is explained by the dominant position of the supplier, which holds in-depth technical knowledge and controls documents, records and the contracting process itself Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 854). Often, the consumer's impossibility of demonstrating their reasons results from the simple fact that the necessary evidence is not in their power, but with the supplier. This is not only an economic obstacle, but a factual impossibility linked to the absence of conditions, including technical ones, for producing evidence, because the power of direction and specialized knowledge belong, as a rule, to the supplier Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 283). Semiotic vulnerability, in this key, can also be perceived as a contemporary form of evidentiary vulnerability: whoever controls not only the service, but the records, the technique and the intelligibility of the relationship, also controls a good part of the conditions for challenging it. The CDC already reacts to this by redistributing burdens and facilitating the defense. ↩
  6. The protection of the consumer is directly linked to the requirement of information that is not merely formally available, but materially apt to guide a decision. Legally adequate communication needs to produce effectively informed and conscious contracting, that is, a state of understanding sufficient for the consumer to know what they are binding themselves to and to be able to deliberate in a minimally autonomous way. For this reason, Wada states that, for the normative program of protection against abusive commercial practices, it is essential to ensure the consumer “an informed and conscious contracting”, since “only thus will they be able to make their decision and assume the commitment with the supplier” Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 211). This requirement has a clearly rebalancing function. The information protected by the consumer-law system is not worth anything in itself, as a bureaucratic rite of unloading data, but for its practical utility in compensating the consumer's vulnerability. The author observes that this limit imposed on the supplier seeks precisely to “rebalance the consumer relationship”, providing the consumer with the opportunity to “know, negotiate and decide” Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 211). In a convergent sense, the author maintains that, given the consumer's lack of capacity to choose or use the product or service adequately, the supplier has the duty to provide the performance under conditions that allow the consumer to “make their choices and use the product or service adequately” Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 143). Intelligibility, thus, is not communicational adornment: it is a functional presupposition of the decisional utility of information. ↩
