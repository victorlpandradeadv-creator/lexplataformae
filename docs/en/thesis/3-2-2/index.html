3.2.2 Informational rentierism under platforms

p. 183

Under the structure of the hypothesis on which the logical path of this discussion is built, informational rentierism is treated as a way of organizing consumption in platform environments, and not merely as a macroeconomic diagnosis. In Dowbor192, the focus shifts from the image of the fief to the toll economy and to the demand monopoly, that is, to the control of circuits of access, visibility and transaction that converts connectivity into p. 184 recurring rent. This lens makes it possible to reconstruct, in consumerist language, why the protection of the CDC cannot be limited to the initial delivery, or even to a single moment of supply, since the utility of the IOC depends on continued governance and on conditions whose auditability is structurally asymmetric.

The analysis of platform capitalism, from the perspective of Ladislau Dowbor, requires recognizing a structural inflection in the regime of accumulation that is not to be confused with a mere technological update of the industrial paradigm. Dowbor describes the consolidation of a “new mode of production” and informational accumulation193, in which the axis of social command shifts from physical manufacture to the strategic governance of intangible assets, of financialization and of technology. In his historical typology, the modern digital economy not only instrumentalizes information, but converts it into the most valuable resource of the regime of accumulation, instituting a pattern of appropriation that operates above the level of the effective producer and repositions the bases of a private economic sovereignty, understood as the capacity to define rules of access, extraction and monetization through infrastructures controlled by private agents.

Dowbor's interpretive key matters here because it reconstructs, at the micro level, what is often naturalized as an update routine or mere “evolution of the service”. In platform ecosystems, the utility of the immaterial good depends on recognition, visibility and permanence governed by the supplier itself, so that unilateral changes are not lateral noise, but an instrument of capture. The relevant asymmetry is not only informational. It is infrastructural, because it makes it possible to impose frictions, urgencies and calibrated losses that convert permanence into an economic condition of consumption, as observed in the empirical laboratory Riot Games.

In this shift, platform capitalism is characterized by the action of digital structures that operate as demand monopolies194, capable of extracting rent through concentrated and asymmetric control of informational flows, beyond the classic logic of the one-off sale of commodities. “The result is the so-called ‘demand monopolies’: p. 185 we have to use what others use, because without it we do not communicate, besides the fact that they buy any competitor that tries to open up space” Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 24). The generation of value comes to depend, primarily, on the control of interfaces, data and sign systems, that is, of the semiotically mediated material conditions that organize access, guide decision and modulate the very experience of the consumer's enjoyment.

In the scope adopted here, “informational rentierism” in Dowbor can be defined as the contemporary form of appropriation of surplus that stabilizes itself not through the proportional production of goods and services, but through the strategic administration of rights of passage over vital flows of economic and social life. It is a regime in which value is extracted through the capacity to organize and govern access to information-dense circuits, such as communication, payment, circulation of content, intermediations and decisions, converting connectivity itself into a recurring source of rent. The core of command thus shifts from the domain of manufacture to the capture of the process, since whoever controls the platform controls the points of entry and permanence and can transform each crossing into a toll Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 13; p. 20; p. 51-52).

This shift, matured over recent decades, reorganized the core of the system, since accumulation ceased to rest primarily on the production and circulation of goods and came to be structured in mechanisms of rent extraction grounded in the control of intangible assets, especially data, platforms and intellectual property regimes. It is within this horizon that Dowbor uses the term “toll economy”195, that is, the capture of essential flows and of mandatory points of passage, in which value is appropriated through control of access, intermediations and circuits of circulation on an increasing scale, in clear mismatch with any corresponding increase in social production. “Many services are useful, or even necessary, but generate disproportionate profits relative to their contribution, as in the case of communication oligopolies. Rentiers earn not so much from the services they provide, as from the obligation for everyone to pass through their turnstiles.” Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 21).

In the informational environment, the toll economy is realized with particular effectiveness through legal-technical enclosures, since intellectual property rights, p. 186 license agreements, account architecture and access control mechanisms become governance infrastructure and transform access to and permanence in the ecosystem into a recurring source of extraction Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 7; p. 21; p. 41). The very transit through signs, such as interfaces, reputation, digital inventories, profile identities, currencies and content, comes to be captured as an economically exploitable flow.

The IOC, as the concretization of the immaterial product in the sense of Article 3, paragraph 1, of the CDC, appears here not as neutral content, but as a commodity form whose enjoyment is subordinated to contractual and technological turnstiles, so that one does not buy merely an item, but the revocable permission to remain within an ecosystem of signs. It is the same legal-technical enclosure that allows the platform to administer internal inflation by controlling the practical unit of account, the conversion mechanisms and the price anchors, shifting the formation of values to a regime of opaque calibration by design, with low comparability and high dependence on permanence.

Rentier capture through governance intensifies the user's vulnerability in the practice of digital consumption, described here as a dimension of factual vulnerability. Inserted into ecosystems of signs whose permanence is conditioned by contractual and technological turnstiles, the consumer is induced by anticipation and by promises of availability, continuity and functionality that they cannot reconstruct in their decisive elements, since delivery is not accompanied by transparency criteria about its limitations and, in practice, is fully and unilaterally modulable by the supplier Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 19-21).

In this scenario, the very justification of intellectual property tends to operate in an instrumental key, tied to the stimulus of future creations Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 46), so that the hypertrophy of duration and scope may produce a mismatch between incentive and blockage. In copyright, the realization of profits tends to be concentrated in the first cycles of exploitation, around five or, at most, ten years after publication, while exclusivity may last for decades, becoming a fence that restricts access Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 43). For the economic analysis developed here, the decisive point is that intellectual property, when coupled to the platform, ceases to appear merely as an external legal title and comes to operate as a material component of the closure of access, that is, as a functional gear of the toll economy in the informational environment.

The toll economy described by Dowbor is not limited to extraction from end consumers, since it reorganizes the entire chain by subordinating traditional companies to private infrastructures of intermediation and converting the intangible into a general condition of circulation. The social surplus comes to be appropriated less through production and more through the p. 187 capacity to impose rights of passage over activities and flows already socially produced, on a planetary scale, without proportional counterpart in goods, services or jobs. For this reason, Dowbor (2024, p. 13) maintains that rentierism appropriates the surplus without corresponding productive contribution. It is precisely this decoupling between contribution and capture that makes enclosure legally sensitive, because, when platforms and titles of exclusivity operate as permanent turnstiles, the rationale of incentive loses descriptive centrality and the problem of access control emerges as an instrument of extraction, with informational asymmetry and operational opacity relevant to consumer protection.

In this toll economy, the dominant mechanism of surplus extraction shifts. It moves away from a productive regime in which accumulation is organized above all through the exploitation of labor and the discipline of production and approaches an arrangement in which capture is structured by financial and communication platforms and by platforms for the appropriation of private data, with the prevalence of rent over productive accumulation196. The disproportion between effective productive contribution and observed enrichment ceases to indicate a peripheral distortion and comes to point to a qualitative shift in systemic dynamics, in which rentier logic becomes the organizing principle Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 8-9; p. 20). In this direction, profit on production has evolved into rentierism on intermediation Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 68).

The logic of the toll presents itself in a harsher key when power ceases to operate only through network effects and comes to rest on the control of critical infrastructure of the digital economy. By consolidating services and circuits of computing, logistics and intermediation on which third parties come to depend in order to operate, an infrastructure rentierism is formed, sustained by recurring tolls and by structurally irreplicable barriers to entry, anchored in scale, integration and systemic dependence Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 31).

Thus, the shift from productive capitalism to a rentier regime can be described as a break in proportionality between productive contribution and appropriation of surplus. The extraction of value stabilizes itself through position and through control of points of passage, such as networks, infrastructures and intermediations, and not through a correlated increase in social utility, p. 188 reorganizing accumulation as a systematic drain on social wealth Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 13; p. 20).

In the scope adopted here, Dowbor's critique matters above all for the way in which the platform converts access into rent through the control of informational flows and of decisions on visibility, access and permanence. In this movement, intellectual property regimes cease to operate as simple titles of authorship and come to integrate the infrastructure of control, delimiting possibilities of use, circulation and recombination of digital assets. A regime of authorization and blockage is thus sustained, which structures the consumer's experience as a process that is entirely capturable from an economic point of view.

In the informational paradigm, knowledge assumes the position of central factor of production, but its economic logic is not to be confused with that of physical goods, since, unlike material goods, shared knowledge is not lost to the one who shares it, as it is not a rival good and access to it can be generalized Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 11). This non-rivalry shifts the axis of conflict to the forms of access control, which makes intelligible why immaterial consumer goods and digital experiences, as IOCs, can be technically reproducible and, even so, legally and technically made scarce through regimes of authorization and blockage that organize consumption.

By emphasizing the centrality of knowledge as a factor of production and the platform as the hegemonic form of economic organization, Dowbor suggests that the twenty-first century is structured by an economy in which globalized and digitalized technical infrastructure reconfigures time and space, while the surplus is appropriated by actors capable of managing signals, algorithms and intermediations on a planetary scale. From this follows his diagnosis of a system qualitatively more extractive and unproductive than industrial capitalism, insofar as capture stabilizes itself through control of access and mediations. The decisive point for the present analysis is not to deny the persistence of the productive base, but to show its structural subordination to informational-financial command, which comes to define who accesses the market, through which mediations and under which conditions of subjection Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 3; p. 27).

Within this closure typical of demand monopolies, the network is converted into economic coercion and produces structural dependence. The consumer's individual choice is neutralized by the inevitability of the technological standard, so that the platform comes to operate as a coercive infrastructure of everyday life. In this arrangement, the algorithm articulates economic calculation and social modeling, organizing micro-dynamics of capture under decision-making opacity, while human attention is elevated to the category of raw material and absorbed by p. 189 an interface design that governs signs, hierarchizes visibilities and maximizes permanence and emotional excitability as economically measurable variables.

From Dowbor's perspective, algorithmic capillarity not only expands scale, but reconfigures the user as a unit treatable by calculation197, that is, as an individual isolable and governable by inference, converted simultaneously into a source of information, risk and return. Along these lines, the author states that billions of people cease to represent an incalculable mass and become isolable individuals, sources of information and customers, whether they like it or not Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 29). Within this horizon, the offer ceases to operate as a uniform pattern knowable in advance and comes to function as a contingent set of conditions modulated by continuous segmentation and optimization Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 28). The consumer's experience thus becomes a field of silent adjustments of frictions, permissions and benefits, whose practical effect is the directed instability of the promised functional value, with enough opacity to frustrate auditing and to shift control of enjoyment to the platform.

These silent adjustments of frictions, permissions and benefits rest on the normative and technical reintroduction of scarcity, which does not operate as simple “pricing”, but as a programmed restriction of circulation. Economic freedom is thus preserved for the agent who controls the infrastructure, while costs of freedom and autonomy are transferred to the user, reconfiguring the space of consumption as a zone of dependence and management. The asymmetry is particularly sensitive in IOCs, in which experience, access and rights of use take place under opaque and unilateral mediations, creating conditions for an extractive rationality of value capture through access control.

This reading, however, is not reduced to a denunciation of the technical potential of digitalization, precisely because the scarcity described here is socially imposed and not a given of the nature of the informational. Dowbor insists on the ambivalence of knowledge as an immaterial good, since, not being rival in nature, it can be shared at no additional marginal cost, which opens the objective possibility of collaborative reorganization and of generalization of access to cultural and educational goods. The legal-political problem emerges when rentier capture converts this technical abundance into scarcity, reintroducing artificial barriers and mechanisms p. 190 of appropriation to preserve the logic of rent in an environment that would allow the expansion of the commons. It is this tension between emancipatory potential and rentier appropriation that makes the ontological transition described by Dowbor at once a critique of the present and a dispute over the institutional destiny of the digital Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 10-11; p. 42; p. 45).

The decisive point in Dowbor lies in understanding that digital monetization does not depend only on the sale of commodities, but on the capture of continuous flows of data and payments on a planetary scale and at low granularity, through mechanisms that operate below the user's radar and frequently outside public intelligibility. This diagnosis is condensed in the description of everyday life mediated by platforms and payment systems, which leads the author to conclude that a system of micro-drainage of the resources and personal information of billions of people has been generated198 Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 29). “Micro-drainage” designates a fractional and repeated extraction, made possible by algorithmic capillarity and by inference technologies that make each individual addressable as an economic target, and operationalized by tracking devices such as cookies and related identifiers.

Monetization, therefore, is not episodic, but permanent, cumulative and automated, converting private information into an asset and connectivity itself into a condition of economic subjection. Beyond the economic aspect, micro-drainage is legally sensitive because it produces an asymmetry of auditability. The platform centralizes and exclusively controls logs, metrics and modulation criteria, such as profiling, ranking and recommendation, while the consumer sees only the interface and its effects. As a result, the problem ceases to be merely one of “cost” and comes to involve evidence and material transparency, since the user has no means to reconstruct the link between capture, processing and consequences in the offer and in the experience.

Dowbor (2024) presents digital rentierism as the central economic vector of the twenty-first century and identifies, at the top of this machinery, platform conglomerates that occupy a dominant position in the organization of markets, in informational circulation and in demand itself. Taking companies such as Google, Amazon and Facebook as examples, he emphasizes that these structures operate as global monopolies capable of capturing surpluses through intermediation, data and access control, and not through a proportional relationship with the traditional production of goods and p. 191 services. It is from this position of command that one understands why micro-drainage and the asymmetry of auditability are not accidents of digital consumption, but foreseeable effects of a market architecture founded on opaque intermediations and unilateral modulations. In addressing the subject, Dowbor shifts the focus from individual luxury to the dynamic of reinvestment and expansion of capacities, stating that the point is not to have yachts and airplanes, but to have a company generating profits that will be reinvested in more productive capacities Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 5). On the distributive plane, the shift is expressed in the rupture between appropriation of surplus and productive contribution and in the drain on social wealth typical of unproductive rentierism, breaking the minimum of proportionality between how much one contributes and how much one becomes rich Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 13; p. 20). This movement is further articulated with high finance and with investment structures that reinforce the stability and expansive capacity of platform power.

Advertising, in Dowbor's view, is not a moral problem of “bad content”, but the economic engine that governs the curation of the informational environment199. “A very important dimension is the basic logic of information systems financed by advertising: the return depends on maximizing engagement, attention, which leads the media in general, and social media in particular, to privilege the news that attracts most” Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 30-31). When remuneration depends on screen time and advertising conversion, the recommendation architecture tends to reward what captures emotions and retains attention, articulating profiling, ranking and continuous modulation of the offer under criteria opaque to the user. The same rationality that organizes the individual consumption experience also reconfigures the public sphere under a structurally interested bias; for this reason, the “maximization of profit and dividends goes poorly with the dissemination of science and culture” Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 30- 31).

Under this paradigm, Dowbor elevates attention to the category of central economic resource because, in the digital regime, it acts simultaneously as raw material and commodity. It is captured, held and resold as measurable engagement, converted into advertising income and into power of influence. The diagnosis appears when the author describes the battle p. 192 for people's attention time in the context of the predominance of the communication and information industry Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 25) and, even more incisively, when he states that, caught in the web of the system, we pay at every step, both in costs and in the time of our attention Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 29). The digital turns lifetime into an input for valorization, which makes the appropriation of that time inseparable from mechanisms of curation and choice design that maximize permanence and recurrence.

Thus, attention ceases to be mere passive “exposure” and becomes involuntary labor200 that feeds metrics, profiling and, consequently, pricing. A capture that weighs, in the author's words, “in our pockets” and “in our heads”, and which he describes as a subjective imprisonment inherent to this regime Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 30). In other words, the “information that platforms capture is free”, while “private information is appropriated free of charge” Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 66). Since this capture is carried out through governance, through interested curation, segmentation and persuasive interface architecture, consumer protection cannot be reduced to subsequent patrimonial restoration. The relevant harm may occur earlier, in the intelligibility of the offer and in the very shaping of the decision201.

The result is thus the so-called demand monopolies, in which it becomes necessary to use what others use, on pain of functional isolation202 Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 24). The p. 193 resulting compulsoriness combines with strategies of neutralizing competitors through acquisition and incorporation, reinforcing the concentration and planetary expansion of the dominant agent Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 24; p. 31).

It is for this reason that Dowbor refuses to describe these dynamics under the heading of markets in their classical sense. For the author, it is not a matter of competition to provide better services at better prices, since these structures charge what they want, from people and companies all over the planet Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 31). The demand monopoly alters the competition regime. The dispute ceases to be organized primarily by the promise of better service at a better price203 and comes to revolve around the control of technical standards, the imposition of compatibilities and the strategic blocking of exits. The replacement of WordPerfect by Word, when a specific standard became dominant, illustrates the conversion of voluntary adherence into operational necessity and, with it, the viability of arbitrary charging on a global scale, with macroeconomic effects that the author connects to inflation from monopoly profits (profit-inflation)204 Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 31).

This condition of inevitability thickens when the platform ceases to be merely a final service and comes to act as infrastructure for third parties, as critical support without which other economic activities cannot be carried out. Dowbor identifies in Amazon a telling example of this transition, since, by converting internal tools into services rentable to other agents, the company transforms its dominance into the capacity to become p. 194 a mandatory intermediary205, operating as an infrastructure rentier. In this design, economic strength does not reside only in the sale of goods, but in the control of intermediation layers of the digital ecosystem, sustained by structures of very high complexity and cost, difficult for new entrants to replicate and, for that reason, apt to stabilize value capture through recurring tolls. In this key, “infrastructure” ceases to be a figure of speech and becomes the material form of economic command in the digital environment, determining where circulation must effectively take place.

From this, infrastructure, already converted into unavoidable intermediation, assumes the function of a toll. The consumption of IOCs comes to be lived as governed access, and the legal-technical enclosure that sustains this governance reopens, from within, the discussion on the legitimacy of blocking and on which parameters may justify it at the level of consumer protection.

At the macroeconomic level, digital rentierism can be described as a regime in which the capture of surplus is stabilized less by the proportional production of goods and services and more by the administration of rights of passage over strategic circuits, financial, informational, logistical and legal, which become conditions of possibility of everyday life. The distributive consequence is the structural intensification of inequalities, insofar as income is realized as a repeated toll, through commissions, fees, interest, licenses and access blocks, while at the same time the asymmetry widens between agents who control infrastructures and users who merely circulate within them. In the scope adopted here206, this picture suffices to situate platform power as an informational and semiotic toll economy, p. 195 in which access is governed and the circulation of signs is converted into a recurring unit of extraction.

The figure of the technofief reaches, with Dowbor, a sharper economic determination by situating the passage from industrial capitalism to a platform capitalism marked by digital rentierism. The axis of accumulation ceases to gravitate around profit derived from material production and shifts to the capture of flows207 (of payment, of data, of decisions and of visibility) converted into value by the continuous measurement and modulation of circulation and behavior in a governed environment Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 28-31). At this stage, the platform no longer presents itself as a “producing company”, but as infrastructure of mandatory passage, apt to stabilize income through a toll on each movement and interaction carried out within the ecosystem.

At the level of digital business, this rentierism materializes as structured recurrence, in which the platform converts ordinary mediations of use into points of flow capture and stabilizes income through the governance of access. The empirical forms vary, but the common trait is the transformation of enjoyment into an architecture deliberately designed to enable the continuous extraction of value, shifting the IOC from the register of “content” to that of a functional arrangement of permissions and restrictions.

Dowbor treats the algorithm as the instrument of command par excellence, precisely because it operationalizes this grammar of tolls by articulating economic calculation and social modeling. The algorithm selects, orders and directs the experience with the explicit purpose of financial return, which authorizes reading rules of visibility (what appears to whom), of access (what requires authorization) and of conduction (incentives to purchase and loyalty) as the expression of a codified governance208.

p. 196 This is a practical normativity inscribed in the technical architecture, in interfaces, permissions and compatibilities, which precedes and conditions the contract itself. By stating that to the rentierism that drains resources one must add the algorithmic control of people, the author makes explicit that algorithmic curation is not neutral. It operates as a device through which the extraction of value is converted into social control and subordinates even traditional companies to the logic of value for the shareholder, that is, to shareholder value Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 27-28).

The reason why this regime shows such resilience, in Dowbor's view, is also cognitive-institutional in nature. The mechanisms of extraction become progressively more complex and, for that reason, less understood Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 3), causing financial-digital exploitation to remain “a nebulous reality”209 for almost the entire population Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 13). This opacity is not merely technical, but political, insofar as the asymmetry of understanding disarms social resistance and postpones state regulation.

Dowbor makes explicit the gap between a digital economic base and still analog institutions by stating that the economic base has shifted and the institutions remain in the last century Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 8). He also observes that immaterial money allows sophisticated forms of exploitation that are difficult to understand Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 84). In this perspective, opacity operates as a technology of power that reduces contestation, hinders accountability and produces a field of regulatory impunity in which algorithmic control and rentier extraction reproduce themselves without proportional democratic counterweights.

In this architecture, the control of interfaces and signs ceases to be a marketing expedient and becomes infrastructure for value generation, regulating the visibility of what counts as opportunity and the very intelligibility of what counts as choice. It is p. 197 precisely in this mediation by signs that the consumer's factual vulnerability (Article 4, item I, of the CDC) takes on its semiotic feature: one decides under a regime of representations and promises whose correspondence with actual delivery is structurally opaque and difficult to audit.

The analytical key lies in perceiving that such strategies do not operate as marketing “accessories” coupled to a core of enjoyment presupposed as stable, but as devices that constitute the Immaterial Object of Consumption (IOC) itself as an architecture of enjoyment. If, in the immaterial domain, the good is inseparable from what it does and how it does it, that is, from the actual experience that emerges from its technical organization and its mediation by signs, then the deliberate decomposition of that experience into toll points, such as access, progression, time and rarity, is not an external form of charging. It is the very form of production of enjoyment as an instrument of continuous extraction.

This rentier constitution of the IOC operates through the institutional conversion of technical abundance into administered scarcity. Contents and signs that could circulate widely come to be subjected to calibrated restrictions, windows and conditionings, so that the platform administers the very conditions of possibility of enjoyment in real time. What is delivered to the consumer becomes inseparable from the way in which delivery is conditioned, since income is realized as repeated charging for permanence, progression and circulation in the ecosystem.

The conversion of digital abundance into administered scarcity does not need “production” in the classical sense. The curation of access is enough. In a market of non-rival goods, rarity does not emerge as a natural limit, but as a decision of government, that is, as design and calendar that transform circulation into conditioned passage. Within the Riot Games ecosystem, rarefaction is not an accident. It appears as an operationally assumed and repeatable policy, which removes items from the ordinary flow of acquisition and returns them under selective windows, calibrating urgency, expectation and availability as economic variables.210

This rarefaction by government appears in at least two modalities operationally assumed by Riot Games. A telling example lies in the so-called Legacy skins. They are presented as relatively rare not because of technical characteristics of the good, but because they “are not always available” in the store, reappearing intermittently in bundles, p. 198 promotions and events Riot Games(Riot Games, 2025h).211 The operation is typical of what Dowbor describes as a toll economy. The platform does not charge for the object as an isolated commodity, but for the turnstile of access to the catalog, that is, for control of the point of passage that decides when the good can be acquired and under what conditions. Digital abundance, which would allow permanent availability, is reconfigured into “scarcity by schedule”, converting time into price and access into income.

This same mechanism gains sophistication when scarcity is not only temporal, but also personalized. The “Your Shop” feature is not limited to offering discounts, since it acts as a segmentation device that transforms the history of use into a criterion of curation of what appears as opportunity. The official description itself states that the offers are produced by comparing the player's history with similar patterns, seeking to create offers that “you will like” Riot Games(Riot Games, 2023b).212 What is organized, in practice, is a form of individualized toll. Access to the catalog does not present itself to the user as a stable and knowable set of alternatives, but as a variable showcase, built from inferences that the consumer neither audits nor reconstructs. In Dowborian terms, the algorithm ceases to be a mere instrument of convenience and comes to operate as an economic technology of command, because it decides which goods enter the zone of visibility and under which frictions and urgencies consumption becomes probable.

Administered scarcity, from this perspective, reveals that the “good” is less the isolated virtual item and more the access regime that surrounds it. By removing and reintroducing content through windows, by modulating what appears as an offer, and by conditioning the experience to proprietary cycles of availability, the ecosystem manufactures the bottleneck that sustains income. The content could circulate widely, but it is enclosed so that circulation is experienced as passing through turnstiles, with repeated charging of attention, permanence and payment. This design is not peripheral to enjoyment. It is enjoyment, in the sense that it organizes the experience p. 199 as an economically governed path, in which the platform administers access to access itself.213

This regime of turnstiles is not merely a marketing policy. It is anticipated as the operating structure of the service, as if the instability of the catalog and of the conditions of enjoyment were part of the “product” itself, always redesignable in the name of updating, security and improvement. The contract makes explicit that the service may be updated and modified continuously, and that the company may, to the extent permitted by law, “modify, suspend or discontinue” any part of the services, as well as “restrict your access to any feature or part of the Services” and “remove or modify Game Content, including Virtual Products” Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e).214 This type of clause is not a technical detail. It functions as a legal license to make abundance an administrable and, therefore, profitable field, because it allows the platform to convert what would be predictable into contingent, and what would be permanent into episodic, always under the argument of internal management of the ecosystem.

In this architecture, the so-called “Virtual Content” ceases to be a good “acquired” in the intuitive sense of the language of consumption and becomes a permission of access under private governance, subject to revision and, ultimately, to the very existence of the account. The same contractual section states that the account does not confer a “property right” on the user and describes Virtual Content as something that may be destroyed, deleted or modified within the services, including when the account is terminated or when the company makes reasonable and necessary changes215 Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e). It is here that the Dowborian tone finds its sharpest translation. When the supplier controls the catalog, controls time and controls the way access appears to the consumer, the economic center is not in the production of the item, but in the monopoly over the keys of the gate. Income arises from command over the conditions of p. 200 passage, and passage is continuously recalibrated. Thus, the platform converts abundance into income, since, instead of selling a good, it sells passage, and transforms each reappearance, each showcase and each window into repeated charging for the right to cross its own turnstile.

Dowbor formulates the typical operation of rentierism in the digital economy as the political and institutional conversion of technical abundance into monetizable scarcity. The synthesis appears when he states that it is always a matter of transforming threatening abundance into a new form of scarcity Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 22). In this framework, abundance refers to the intrinsic reproducibility of the immaterial, while scarcity designates the socially constructed result of locks and fences that reinstate price and income. The change is profound because it marks the passage from an era of producers and buyers to an era of suppliers and users Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 51), in which the central economic mechanism ceases to be the good and becomes the process. In this sense, today one owns a process and charges for its use216 Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 52). The analytical consequence is that access becomes structurally revisable and conditional, administered as continuous permission and not as definitive transfer, opening space for unilateral reconfigurations of the rules of enjoyment.

From this standpoint, what is delivered to the consumer is inseparable from the way in which delivery is conditioned. Access to content, features and symbols that, from a technical point of view, could circulate in abundance is deliberately limited, and they are constrained to produce monetizable scarcity. The platform, therefore, does not merely alienate a good, since it administers the scarcity that it itself manufactured, continuously and revisably, converting access into conditional permission and reorganizing enjoyment as a permanently modulable process. It is this structural revisability, supported by legal-technical enclosures and by opaque criteria of modulation, that makes the extraction of income sustainable and, at the same time, legally sensitive. It intensifies the consumer's vulnerability and places the problem of the legitimacy of the enclosure back under tension as to practical verifiability.

Through the grammar of tolls, monetization strategies cease to be mere marketing accessories and come to compose the very functional substance of the Immaterial Object p. 201 of Consumption (IOC). The platform institutes a field of choices and expectations mediated by signs, in which micro-drainage and algorithmic opacity deepen the consumer's factual vulnerability in its semiotic feature. As Dowbor suggests, the everyday governance of continuous services recalculates, in real time, parameters of cost, eligibility and access, causing the “product” to be lived as a regime of variable permissions, and not as a thing delivered once and for all. It is in this sense that the mismatch of a dogmatics forged for stable material goods becomes evident when it is confronted with objects whose enjoyment depends on incessant modulations inscribed in the very technical and semiotic architecture of consumption.

When the consumption of IOCs is analyzed with categories designed for the purchase of corporeal goods, the operative core of supply is lost, because utility does not stabilize in a thing under the acquirer's dominion, but depends on external layers under the control217 of the supplier, such as code updates, server maintenance, authentication, compatibilities and integrations. As a result, enjoyment is sustained by continuous technical-operational support and, consequently, unilateral changes in parameters of availability, compatibility or maintenance may degrade, reconfigure or extinguish the functional value delivered to the consumer, without this being reduced to mere contractual variation, since they bear directly on the practical conditions of delivery that make the experience possible over time Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 40; p. 51-52).

In Dowbor's diagnosis, this governance rests on the supplier's infrastructural dominion, which concentrates “control of access codes” and “content management”218 Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 40). In this regime, the platform does not merely intermediate a consumer relationship; it institutes, through interfaces, rules and signs, the field of choices and p. 202 expectations within which the decision is produced, which makes central the reading of the consumer's factual vulnerability in its semiotic feature.

The theoretically central form of “continuous change” consists in the fact that, in consumption based on repeated access, the conditions of use cease to be fixed and come to be renewed, modulated and charged recurrently Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 51-52). Thus, when the economic object shifts from “good” to “process”, access becomes structurally conditional and revisable, administered as continuous permission219, which makes possible the unilateral reconfiguration of the rules of enjoyment Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 51-52).

This same logic reappears, from a distributive angle, in the figure of “toll capitalism”, when Dowbor describes the shift of the economic center: if, in the twentieth century, the dispute gravitated around the ownership of the means of production, the decisive contemporary field reorganizes itself around the cost of access to information-dense processes and circuits, mediated by rights, licenses, patents and other barriers that enable charging220. In this regime, the monetization of the immaterial depends on an institutional engineering that converts technically cheap circulation into conditioned access, manufacturing scarcity and ensuring that knowledge is only realized as price when its diffusion is contained Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 22; p. 41; p. 51-52).

In immaterial objects of consumption, utility is not exhausted in the act of acquisition, since it depends on continuous decisions of support, updating and continuity under the supplier's control Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 51-52; p. 57). For this reason, the legal problem is not the mere p. 203 existence of variations over time, but the possibility that changes in the architecture of enjoyment reorder, opaquely, the level of utility actually delivered.

When the redesign of the immaterial object occurs without sufficient transparency for the consumer to understand, at least in material outline, the correspondence between what is presented in the offer and what is actually delivered, the change ceases to be perceived as a simple adjustment and comes to operate as an opacifying government of enjoyment. The platform can create structural dependence by making the experience progressively conditioned. At the same time, it can neutralize the capacity for contestation by keeping decision criteria, such as ranking, access, balancing or availability, under a black-box regime221. These are calculation procedures oriented to maximizing return and sustained by an algorithmic capillarity that the user cannot reconstruct by ordinary means Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 28-29). At this intersection of administered scarcity, functional opacity and induced dependence, the terrain of the legal problem to be qualified below takes shape.

Dowbor formulates the idea of an inverted political sign to mark that the technical base of platforms does not carry a single purpose. The same infrastructure capable of expanding cooperation, circulation of knowledge and participation can be organized, through capture of incentives and through governance, for enclosure, extraction and government of the visible. The decisive point, therefore, is not “technology”, but the legal-institutional vector that structures access and defines whether technical abundance is converted into inclusion or into toll. For this reason, regulation is not reduced to an ex post patch; in network powers and infrastructural positions, it operates as structural design of continuity, quality, auditability and material transparency. In this scope, the problem can be formulated in consumer law language (CDC), above all with regard to the material shaping of the offer and of enjoyment in governed ecosystems222.

p. 204 At the same time, Dowbor refuses to treat this systemic closure as an unavoidable technological fatality. The author records the historical possibility of breaking fences and proprietary standards, mentioning examples such as that of IBM and the migration to compatible clones, as well as the logic of open source (open source), which shifts the sale value without necessarily reducing the use value. Infrastructure thus reveals itself as a politically contestable field. The same technologies that today sustain oligopolies can have their political sign inverted and operate as tools of democratic connectivity, including through the guarantee of public and free access to the information highways, conceived more as streets than as toll instruments. The caveat is decisive to avoid a naturalizing reading of the demand monopoly, showing that the coercion of the technological standard does not stem from technical neutrality, but from a technique organized by specific regimes of property, governance and institutional design Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 39-40; p. 45; p. 56; p. 66; p. 88).

At the microeconomic level, Dowbor describes continuous reconfiguration as the capacity for dynamic and individualized adjustment of access conditions by information systems, that is, regimes in which essential parameters, such as cost, eligibility and terms, can be recalculated at every instant under proprietary criteria. In this register, he observes that the cost of credit is adjusted according to the bank's interests Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 29). What is at stake is the replacement of relatively stable rules by an operational governance that transforms each user into a unit treatable by calculation, allowing access conditions to be continuously modulated by profiling, monitoring and automated decision, deepening the asymmetry between those who administer the system and those who submit to its material updates.

Informational asymmetry, from Dowbor's perspective, is not only a classic theme of political economy on the relationship between buyer and seller, but a systemic architecture in which platforms and intermediaries accumulate hypertrophied capacities to see and calculate, while users and the State itself remain in a state of cognitive blindness Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 28-29). The example of the Serasa system makes explicit the expropriation of this power: “the Serasa system allows your bank account manager to have detailed information about your financial situation, and your cost of credit will be adjusted according to the bank's interests. And it was legalized” Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 29). This asymmetry becomes power of command because p. 205 it enables discriminatory pricing223, understood as individualized modulation of price and conditions of the same service — and, in digital ecosystems, also of the conditions of access to and enjoyment of IOCs — based on inferences about profile, urgency and propensity to pay, operated by proprietary criteria and without comparable transparency Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 28-29). By referring to Stiglitz as a reference for understanding these impacts, Dowbor inscribes the diagnosis in a consolidated tradition on informational asymmetry and inequality Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 48): in the digital environment, the ordinary capture of data from connected life radically amplifies this asymmetry and makes it structural, guiding economic decisions directed against the individuals themselves.

It is precisely at the convergence of administered scarcity, functional opacity and induced dependence that the terrain of the legal problem takes shape. The architecture can be governed to expand extraction, reordering the user's experience and straining expectations of continuity and predictability. The architecture of supply is not neutral: it incorporates its own ends of extraction, revealed in the way the service is continuously governed to expand income224, even at the cost of unbalancing the legitimately expected functional minimum. The social and regulatory effects highlighted by Dowbor, including as to the need for public containment of rentier dynamics and protection against the deepening of inequalities, make up the background for understanding that contemporary extraction depends on the governance of interfaces, data and signs as new material conditions of consumption. It is from this matrix that it becomes possible to describe, with p. 206 precision, the architectural rationality of extraction that structures value capture in digital ecosystems.

This operational gear, stated here as a mechanism for converting abundance into conditioned access, reappears, under different lexicons, in four systemically linked cores. First, in the explanation that, in the economy of the immaterial, under rentier logic, business strategy tends to maximize the locking of access to produce monopoly effects Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 22-23). Second, in the formulation that knowledge only acquires sale value when its diffusion is prevented by legal and repressive apparatuses, producing artificial scarcity articulated with the idea of a “toll” Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 41). Third, in the generalization of “toll capitalism”, in which access to processes and environments is enclosed to sustain permanent income Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 51-52). Finally, at the infrastructural level, when the capacity of private operators to dictate who accesses the information highways through “communication corrals”225 is discussed Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 49-50), that is, forms of closure that convert circulation into passage conditioned and administrable by a proprietary ecosystem. In terms of an inventory of analytical equivalents, artificial scarcity can be defined as the deliberate locking of access added to the construction of legal and contractual bottlenecks that ensure the capacity to charge for entry into the ecosystem. Once this inventory is fixed, it becomes possible to stabilize an operative vocabulary to describe contemporary rentierism as government of flows and accesses, and not as a mere form of “market”.

This closure worsens when the platform ceases to be merely a “final service” and assumes an infrastructural position for third parties, becoming critical support without which other economic activities do not operate. At this second level, power is no longer only that of the network, but that of the choke point. Whoever controls computing, distribution, means of payment, authentication or circulation of content comes to dictate access conditions and to carry out extraction through recurring tolls, sustained by costs and complexities difficult to replicate. Thus, the demand monopoly describes coercion by standard; infrastructure rentierism describes capture by unavoidable intermediation.

p. 207 The materiality of command in platform capitalism is not located in a “good” delivered once and for all, but in the capacity to govern, in real time, the practical conditions of visibility, access and circulation that structure consumption itself. It is at this level that interfaces, data and signs cease to be mere marketing ornament and come to operate as decision-making infrastructure. What is defined is what appears to whom, in what order, with which frictions, under which permissions, on what terms and at what rhythms. Rentier extraction is realized precisely because this architecture is modulable and asymmetric, allowing the supplier to retain metrics, logs and operating criteria, while the user sees only the effects on the screen and cannot reconstruct, by ordinary means, the link between capture, processing and result. Algorithmic governance, therefore, is not a neutral “technical means”, but the practical form by which the toll installs itself in everyday life, transforming the circulation of signs and permanence in the ecosystem into repeated units of monetization.

The informational toll economy translates, at the business level, into recurring forms of income that do not depend on “selling things”, but on organizing access and charging for the crossing. A first modality is capture by direct intermediation, with percentages, commissions and fees levied on transactions carried out within the ecosystem, converting the platform into a mandatory intermediary of the very economic encounters it hosts. The second is advertising and attentional income, sustained by continuous data collection and by hypersegmentation, in which attention time is captured, measured and converted into revenue through curation and choice design oriented to engagement.

The third modality includes income from access layers, when subscriptions, passes, expansions, time windows and recurrence mechanisms progressively shift relevant features and content to zones of additional payment, often combined with internal units of value226 and proprietary circuits of credit and consumption, whose p. 208 utility depends entirely on permanence in the environment. Finally, the fourth modality is legal-technical enclosure, in which Intellectual Property, license agreements and architectural restrictions operate as devices of authorization and blocking, multiplying points of permission and stabilizing the scarcity necessary for the immaterial to be realized as price and income.

This economic picture makes it intelligible why, in platform-mediated consumption, traditional categories of consumer law need to be reread in light of the operational materiality of the immaterial. If extraction depends on governing access, visibility and permanence, the “product” cannot be reduced to isolated content; it presents itself as an architecture of enjoyment and permissions that structures utility over time.

For this reason, the relevant legal core shifts from mere price variation to the material intelligibility of the offer and to control over the practical conditions of delivery, since the supplier concentrates logs, metrics and modulation criteria, while the consumer perceives only the surface of the interface and its effects and cannot reconstruct the link between data capture, curation and impacts on utility. At this point, factual vulnerability (Article 4, item I, of the CDC) takes on its semiotic form. The consumer decides on the basis of signs and promises whose correspondence with functional delivery remains structurally opaque and unilaterally reconfigurable, which makes the governance of access the decisive site where the practical predictability of enjoyment is organized — or disorganized.

  25. Dowbor uses the formula “owner of a process” to indicate that, in digital rentierism, capture no longer depends on transferring a good and instead becomes stable through continued control over the conditions of use, turning enjoyment into authorized permanence within a governed circuit Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 51-52). Seen this way, the relevant economic object is not “content” as such, but the operation of access itself — its permissions, compatibilities and continuity criteria —, so that use becomes structurally conditional and subject to unilateral revision Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 51-52). As a consequence, the guarantees intuitively associated with acquisition lose practical substance: availability, continuity and the power of enjoyment come to depend on the infrastructure holder and on the private rules that administer the process Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 51-52). ↩
  26. The architecture of access and enjoyment designates the set of technical and operational layers that sustain the utility of IOCs over time — code updates, authentication, server availability, compatibilities and integrations — and that, for that reason, move consumption from the logic of delivering (tradição) a good to continuous participation in a governed process Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 40; p. 51-52). Dowbor describes this mutation as the institutional passage from a regime of producers and buyers to one of suppliers and users, and as the replacement of concrete property by recurring control over a process, in which economic capture is achieved by charging for use Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 51-52). Since enjoyment depends on these layers under the supplier's command, unilateral changes to maintenance, compatibility or access parameters may reconfigure the functional value delivered to the consumer — through degradation, locking or discontinuation — without the classical categories of acquisition and permanence of the good adequately capturing the phenomenon Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 40; p. 51-52). ↩
  27. The reference to conditions of possibility is meant only to clarify that enjoyment of the immaterial depends on the supplier's infrastructural control over the practical regimes of access and circulation, so that the consumer's experience is structured by permissions and mediations controlled by the offeror — that is, by the administration of “access codes” and by “content management” as material dimensions of supply Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 40). ↩
  28. For Dowbor, rentier enjoyment of the digital tends to be organized as authorized permanence in a process: access can be continuously conditioned and revised by the infrastructure holder, instead of being settled by the classical logic of delivering a good Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 51-52). This precariousness does not denote an occasional failure but a functional feature of the arrangement, since the supplier retains the capacity to restrict, reconfigure or end the experience by administratively modulating permissions, normalizing social life as a circuit of passage and recurring charges: “Living ceases to be a stroll […] to become a permanent race from toll to toll.” Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 51). Taken to its limit, the very idea of access is recast as governance by instituted scarcity, as the metaphor suggests: “Free access to the sea does not fill anyone's pockets. Let us close, then, the beaches. […] we become increasingly trapped in the race to increase our monthly income, without which we will be deprived of a series of essential services.” Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 51). Within the scope of the CDC, this reinforces that signs of “service”, “plan” and “purchase” may mask structurally revocable enjoyment, increasing opacity and making it difficult, in practice, to anticipate and contest relevant changes in the conditions of use Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 51-52). ↩
  29. According to Dowbor, the “barriers that allow charging” are not limited to technical locks or to licenses taken in isolation. Rentier capture of the immaterial becomes stable when the legal order reinforces the enclosure, including through repressive means, making circumvention of the locks unlawful. It is in this sense that the author describes the corporate hardening of control as a “strategy implemented through a series of new laws intended to extend the copyright term of the work, reinforce criminal penalties for infringing copyright, and to punish the use of technologies that tried to avoid the digital obstacles placed on digital content” Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 42-43). The institutional effect is to turn the ordinary reproducibility of the digital into conditioned circulation: exclusion — through reinforced technical-legal fences — becomes a condition of possibility of the toll, that is, of income extracted through control over access rather than through the social utility of dissemination Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 22; p. 41; p. 51-52). ↩
  30. The “black-box regime”, in the reading proposed here on the basis of Dowbor, designates an institutional form of opacity in which relevant decisions are moved to proprietary layers of calculation and organization — ranking, eligibility, visibility, balancing and availability — beyond the user's ordinary understanding, in an environment marked by algorithmic capillarity and by processing geared toward maximizing economic return Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 28-29). The practical consequence is evidentiary and cognitive: the consumer has neither understandable traces nor a common language with which to reconstruct the actual criteria by which the service is modulated, even though they experience its effects directly in enjoyment Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 28-29). In such a context, even when formal terms and documents are available, transparency may remain insufficient for oversight and attribution, since the relevant decision-making mechanism remains unintelligible — a point that ties in with the diagnosis of increasing complexity and opacity and with the insufficiency of merely formal transparency Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 3; p. 13). ↩
  31. Dowbor frames the “inverted political sign” as a thesis of institutional reversibility: the same technical base that today sustains drainage and enclosure can be organized for purposes of well-being and democratization, because the decisive vector is not “technology” as such, but the arrangement of incentives and governance that structures access Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 82). The contrast emerges when the author observes that, while technology makes cultural goods more accessible, “the laws […] evolve symmetrically to make access ever more difficult”, shifting the conflict to the legal and institutional regulation of enclosure Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 42). Hence the conclusion that the “right of access to knowledge” becomes an axis of economic democratization Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 65). This diagnosis finds a parallel in the treatment of network infrastructures and of situations without effective competition, in which “private property and ‘economic freedom’, when there is no competition, lead to abuses”, that is, “it is private property, but not market”, with a loss of public purpose Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 31-32). The framework supports reading consumer law as bearing on the material shaping of the offer and of enjoyment in governed ecosystems, under requirements of transparency and loyalty that take into account the practical difficulty of reconciling profit and social objectives in hybrid structures Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 87). ↩
  32. Discriminatory pricing, in Dowbor's diagnosis, stems from the capacity for individualization at scale. Instead of appearing as public and comparable attributes of the offer, price and conditions become variables calculated per user, modulated by behavioral and socioeconomic inferences geared toward maximizing return Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 28-29). The Serasa example serves as an institutionalized illustration of this rationality, recording that “your cost of credit will be adjusted according to the bank's interests”, which shows that calibration may exploit information asymmetries and estimated needs, and not merely reflect “risk” in a neutral sense Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 29). The decisive legal point is not the existence of transparent segmentation, but silent differentiation that prevents price from being socially comparable and makes it harder to reconstruct discrimination in evidence, since the criteria and data behind the modulation remain under the supplier's control. Seen this way, signs such as “personalization” may operate as an opaque technique of extraction, putting strain on duties of material transparency and loyalty in the shaping of the offer and, when anchored in the exploitation of urgency or fragility, coming close to the logic of manifestly excessive advantage (Article 39, item V, of the CDC). As internal parameters of control, open auditability and functional value equivalence apply here to make the logic by which price and conditions are formed verifiable, and to allow judicial review of the gap between what is charged and what is delivered in IOCs. ↩
  33. In Dowbor's diagnosis, unproductive rentierism takes on a distributive expression when the proportional link between productive contribution and enrichment is broken, consolidating itself as a drain on social wealth in favor of rentier positions; for the author, this shift marks the move of the axis from capital accumulation to the logic of unproductive rentierism Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 13; p. 20). It is in this sense that the architecture of the service can be governed to expand extraction, shifting the axis from “gain” to the recurring toll embedded in the mediations. ↩
  34. The expression “communication corrals”, used by Dowbor, designates the infrastructural closure through which private operators come to control the conditions of access and circulation on the information highways themselves, consolidating the power to select, condition and administer enjoyment in proprietary environments Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 49-50). This closure is not merely a neutral technical option, because it involves the coordinated appropriation of layers ranging from “physical infrastructure” to “codes” and “content”, moving the governance of informational space into private regimes of passage Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 49). In economic terms, the “corrals” are a condition of possibility for recurring charges for access and permanence, since they turn circulation into an administered crossing and thus make it compatible with the toll rationality that runs through the diagnosis of contemporary rentierism Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 21; p. 49-50). ↩
  35. Internal units of value in closed digital ecosystems (points, proprietary “coins”, bundles and bonuses) operate as a technology for the economic governance of consumption by moving the user away from state currency and direct price comparison toward unilaterally defined denominations, with conversion tables and bundle sizes that may fragment the perception of spending and induce successive top-ups (recurring leftovers, partial recharges, conditional bonuses). The design can be read as compatible with the logic of tolls and repeated micro-extraction described by Dowbor, insofar as it brings payment closer to a regime of small, continuous captures that the individual barely notices Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 29; p. 21). The relevant structural effect is economic lock-in: once converted into an internal unit, the balance tends to lose general fungibility and become an asset held captive by the ecosystem, raising exit costs and making staying a practical condition for not losing value already internalized. At the doctrinal level, this requalifies price as a semiotic component of the offer: signs of “promotion”, “advantage” and “convenience” may conceal conversion frictions, limits on circulation and restrictions on redemption, putting strain on duties of information, transparency and loyalty and, depending on the case, on the prohibition of manifestly excessive advantage (Article 39, item V, of the CDC). When this closed circuit is combined with technical and contractual restrictions on circulation, the governance of access through turnstiles and crossing costs is reinforced, which is the core of the diagnosis of tolls and disproportionate extraction Dowbor(Dowbor, 2024, p. 21; p. 39). ↩
