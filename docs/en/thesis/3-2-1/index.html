3.2.1 Industrial capitalism and technofeudalism

Within the scope of platform-operated interactive entertainment, the passage to technofeudalism becomes visible when the consumption experience ceases to look like a one-off acquisition and reveals its dependence on an administered territory. The rare cosmetic item, the battle pass and the game's internal economy function as goods while the ecosystem preserves the grammar that attributes value, visibility and prestige to them. When unilateral updates redesign rarity, appearance, effects, balancing or display criteria, the platform shows that the economic core of the relationship lies not in the circulation of a stable commodity, but in continued control over the conditions of enjoyment. What changes, therefore, is not only the content of the product, but the structure of power that makes it possible to impose conditions of participation and to reprice, through technical governance, what the consumer took as acquired.

p. 146 Drawing on Varoufakis118, the passage from industrial capitalism to technofeudalism119 is mobilized as a lens for understanding this shift. “Cloud capital” does not designate only computational infrastructure, but a form of economic power capable of controlling channels of access, circulation, data, visibility and transaction. The platform ceases to act as a simple intermediary between market agents and comes to function as a private territory in which users, consumers, suppliers and creators depend on internal rules to exist economically. Hence the force of the feudal image: consumers and users become “cloud serfs” not because they stop consuming, but because their participation comes to reproduce the very capital that governs them.

The scenario of the player who acquires cosmetics and whose enjoyment is affected by reworks makes it possible to see, in miniature, what Varoufakis describes on a macro scale. The platform operates as an infrastructure landlord: it controls the environment, the rules, the updates and the visibility of what acquires value there. “Cloud capital” appears as dominion over access, reputation and permanence, so that the consumer, while retaining formal ownership of items linked to the account, remains subject to the unilateral power to modulate utility, prestige and conditions of enjoyment. The cost is expressed not only in new payments, but also in attention, adaptation, loss of equivalence and continuous dependence on the administered territory.

Rent is extracted not only in the act of purchase, but in the continuous management of the digital territory, where artificial scarcity, update cycles and the reconfiguration of signs reorganize desires and push the subject into new rounds of spending and status recovery. This p. 147 framing is central to ensuring that technofeudalism is read not as a loose sociological metaphor, but as a description of the same mechanism of private governance that, at the micro level, degrades enjoyment and, at the macro level, sustains rentier extraction through infrastructure.

For Varoufakis, the new system is characterized not only by a “new technology”, but by an inversion of the systemic engine of accumulation, in which profit (“beneficio”) ceases to operate as the gravitational center and is displaced by the extraction of rent (“renta”), in particular cloud rent (“renta de la nube”), charged as a condition of access to platform environments and to the digital infrastructure that sustains them. The theoretical consequence of this inversion is that the transition cannot be described as an intensification of capitalism, but as a structural substitution of pillars in which markets give way to platforms understood as fiefdoms and profit loses centrality to the rentier logic of extraction120.

Varoufakis explicitly maintains121 that technofeudalism should not be understood as an "advanced phase" of capitalism, but as a structural rupture, in which capitalist dynamics would cease to govern contemporary economies and would be replaced by a distinct system. The key to this rupture is not merely technological, but economic-institutional: markets cease to operate as the predominant organizing medium and are displaced by platforms that function as fiefdoms, while profit loses its place as systemic engine and cedes centrality to rent, especially cloud rent122 (Varoufakis, p. 148 2024, p. 9). In terms of the architecture of economic power, the argument gains density by asserting that the "pillars" of capitalism are effectively replaced by pillars proper to technofeudalism, articulating "cloud fiefdoms" and "cloud rent" as the explanatory core of a mode of accumulation and domination that reconfigures forms of dependence and access Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 9).

Within this frame, Varoufakis (2024, p. 115) expressly rejects nomenclatures of continuity (such as “hypercapitalism” or “rentier capitalism”) because, in his view, they obscure the defining feature of the present, marked by the recentralization of rent and by the transformation of the market into a territory administered by proprietary infrastructures123. At the same time, the rupture is not narrated as an appearance ex nihilo. Technofeudalism would have been born from capitalist activity itself and would impose itself by historical metamorphosis, preserving the vitality of capital even when the capitalist form ceases to function as the predominant organizing principle124.

p. 149 This terminological refusal helps to make explicit that the analytical gain of the formula “technofeudalism” lies in pointing to a change in the predominant type of power. Instead of accumulation depending mainly on the sale of commodities produced in industrial cycles, it comes to depend on proprietary positions over digital infrastructure, over means of access and over the capacity to impose conditions of participation.

In platform games, “conditions of participation” are not a metaphor. They materialize in access rules, visibility regimes, performance metrics, matchmaking architecture, progression economy and sanction protocols, all controlled unilaterally. The economic effect is that the environment ceases to operate as a market in the classic sense and comes to operate as an administered market, in which the supplier not only offers goods, but governs the parameters that make these goods exist as value. Enjoyment becomes dependent on permanence under shifting criteria, and extraction shifts from sale to recurring rent, whether through direct charging or through frictions designed to convert time, attention and status into payment.

This reconfiguration requires, in the author's own vocabulary, a conceptual distinction between profit and rent125 which is not reducible to an accounting difference, but is a qualitative one tied to the way each is obtained and to the degree of exposure to competition. Profit tends to be eroded by competition, while rent is sustained by a position of control and therefore resists competitive dynamics in a different way Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 106-107).

The point becomes clearer once one observes that a skin and its associated content do not operate as a fully alienated commodity, but as a conditional permission to enjoy within a private domain. The platform may preserve the nominal existence of the item in the p. 150 account and, even so, downgrade its enjoyment bundle by altering its appearance, its effects, its place in the internal economy or the criteria for its social display. In this dynamic, revenue that looks like “profit from sale” behaves as rent from control, since the platform captures value by administering access and by governing the scarcity, visibility and practical equivalence of what is enjoyed. The same pattern reappears in bans and graduated sanctions, which function as forms of functional expropriation and as mechanisms of economic recapture, because they force the consumer to rebuild, through a new investment of time, attention and assets, their reputation, their access and their signs of belonging within the same administered territory.

Hence the central nuance of the diagnosis. Although profit remains a widely diffused social ambition, contemporary accumulation may become decoupled from profits, so that profit126 becomes almost contingent for the new elite. The axis of economic power p. 151 comes to rest, more directly, on rentier extraction as the effective foundation of dominion and reproduction of advantage127.

Varoufakis maintains that the defining axis of technofeudalism is cloud capital, understood as a recent mutation of capital whose ownership reorganizes economic hierarchies and “kills” capitalism not through lack of capital, but through transformation of the dominant capital-form Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 9). The author reintroduces a feudal grammar to describe the present, in which the owners of cloud capital figure as new feudal lords, while the rest return to the condition of serfs.

For Varoufakis, the decisive rupture lies in the displacement of the center of economic command. In industrial capitalism, power was organized around ownership of traditional productive means and the extraction of profit through the production and circulation of commodities. In technofeudalism, cloud capital comes to control the channels through which commodities, services, information, reputation and social interaction circulate. For this reason, the author reintroduces the feudal grammar: the owners of this capital assume the position of new lords, while users and economic agents dependent on the infrastructure come to occupy subordinate positions within private territories of access and circulation.

The choice of feudal grammar does not function as a decorative metaphor, but as a description of material dependence mediated by infrastructure and access Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 78-80). Seen this way, the owners of traditional capital do not disappear, but lose strategic centrality when they depend on digital infrastructures to reach consumers, process payments, organize reputation or maintain visibility. Terrestrial capital continues to produce goods and services; cloud capital comes to command the channels through which these goods and services become economically accessible. In interactive entertainment, the same logic manifests itself when content, community, payment, ranking, updating and reputation are gathered in a proprietary ecosystem. The supplier controls the territory in which value is born, circulates and can be degraded Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 7-8).

Varoufakis names the new technofeudal elite as a specific ruling class, and not as a mere set of digital capitalists. By calling the owners of cloud capital p. 152 cloudalists128, the author signals a change at the top of the social hierarchy: this is a fraction that, by controlling the infrastructure of access and circulation, comes to hold the capacity to command the system itself129. This rise does not appear as mere business innovation, but as a reconfiguration of power130, by which whoever controls cloud capital also imposes conditions on those who control terrestrial capital, pushing traditional manufacturers, sellers and providers into relations of dependence typical of a revived feudal order.

Within this framework, political economy enters not as an external diagnosis, but as a condition of attribution. If economic power derives from control of infrastructure and from the capacity to impose tolls, injury in consumption need not appear only as an isolated defect or explicit non-performance. It may consist in the significant and unilateral degradation of the enjoyment bundle, in the imposition of costs of permanence, in the reduction of visibility, in the modulation of access or in the reconfiguration of internal equivalences. The legal problem arises precisely because the platform does not merely sell or license content: it governs the territory in which this content has value, circulates and remains enjoyable.

The IOC, in this sense, is the legal-descriptive form of apprehending, at the level of consumption, what technofeudalism describes at the macro level. The decisive distinction, in the argument, is that the enrichment of the cloudalist elite is organized less by profit-oriented competition and more by the recurring extraction of rent associated with the regime of access and infrastructural control.

p. 153 Varoufakis connects the figure of the cloudalists to the replacement of profit by rent as the engine of the system, and presents this substitution as the criterion that requires abandoning the vocabulary of “capitalism” in favor of a new description Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 7). In this frame, the cloudalists reduce conventional capitalists to a status of functional subordination, so that companies and producers are compelled to accept platform dictates and to pay for the privilege of circulating in the digital territory. In parallel, the general population is converted into cloud serfs and cloud proletariat, according to the degree of dependence and capture of activity131.

The argument gains empirical density when one descends from the macro plane to the letter of the platform contract, where the feudal grammar ceases to be a theoretical figure and comes to operate as the working language of the bond. The figure of the cloudalist, when it crosses from rhetoric into the letter of the contract, reveals a point of legal humiliation that is structural to technofeudalism. The fiefdom is not a metaphor for the digital environment. It is an account. And the account, far from being the consumer's asset, is described as a private space whose “right” of use amounts to a personal and revocable permission, which is not to be confused with property. What one buys is not an object, nor even a service with minimal stability. In practice, one buys a tolerance of access that can be withdrawn, and the withdrawal need not justify itself by any external theory of legitimacy, because the internal language of the lordship suffices.132

None of this varies with the intensity or volume of the consumer's investment. The contract does not distinguish someone who spent years accumulating signs of belonging from someone who has just joined, because what is at stake is not the merit of the effort or the expenditure of resources on cosmetic items, but the platform's structure of ownership. The account does not become “more yours” p. 154 because it received ten thousand hours of dedication over fifteen years, nor because it received an investment comparable to the cost of an entry-level car. If the company decides to discard the user, exclusion does not expel them only from the game. It cuts off access to the territory and to the acquired collection. Aware of such implications, the contract objectively anticipates that the loss may occur without refund when the company considers, by its own internal criteria, that there was sufficiently just cause to terminate the bond Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e).

Cloud rent appears here in the form of civilized blackmail. Riot states that virtual content is a “personal right” and that there is no user ownership over the account, and further records that “all rights” to this account “will always be” Riot's Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e). This means that the consumer's investment is stacked within a territory whose title never belongs to the vassal. Time, attention, reputation, collection of signs, items and currencies exist while the fiefdom is “open”, and the fiefdom is open as long as governance wants it to be. The contract, then, does not merely describe precariousness. It naturalizes it, by denying monetary value to virtual content and by prohibiting conversion into “real world” money, as if the accumulated experience itself could be legally reduced to something without value, even when it was acquired for a price.133

When the technofeudal thesis calls for a moment of cruelty, it does not come from moral discourse about behavior. It comes from the written mechanism of expropriation. Riot provides for termination and suspension based on what it calls a “reasonable” determination, and lists hypotheses that include violation, but also what it itself describes as the “best interest” of the community or of its services Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e). The formula is significant, because “reasonable” and “best interest” are not neutral concepts here. They function as words of rhetorical containment that disguise the hard center of the regime. It is not the community that defines the threshold, nor a public standard of justice, nor a robust adversarial process. It is the lordship that decides when permanence ceases to be convenient.

The contract also speaks of “just cause”, but technofeudalism appears precisely in the way this expression operates. “Just cause” there is not the conceptual institute of an external dogmatics. It is “just cause” in the operative sense of the fiefdom, that is, whatever the lordship considers sufficient, at that moment, under its own reading of its rules. Once p. 155 this “just cause” is triggered, the text is explicit as to the result. Once the account is terminated, the user will no longer have access to the account, nor to the associated data, nor to the Virtual Content, including in-game currency, and the consequence is definitive loss without refund when the lordship considers that there was a reason Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e). If Varoufakis describes cloud rent as a toll for existing under someone else's infrastructure, the contract describes the disciplinary counterpart. The fiefdom does not expel only the vassal's body. It confiscates the collection the vassal deposited in the territory, and still calls this contractual normality.

The threat need not be carried out in order to govern. It needs to be credible. And Riot itself helps make it credible by publishing rules of permanence that make clear that exclusion can be total, even on a first occurrence, and that permanent suspension exists as a real possibility. In the Community Pact, it states that it may restrict access to features or suspend participation, and admits permanent suspension even on a first occurrence Riot Games(Riot Games, [202-?]d). In an official League of Legends support document, the language is even more direct in admitting that negative behavior “may result in a permanent ban at any time” and “without prior warnings or suspensions” Riot Games(Riot Games, 2026e). Cloud rent, from this angle, is also rent from obedience, since the experience takes place under a regime in which permanence is always provisional and in which the provision can be withdrawn suddenly.

The final effect is an institutional design in which the account is a revocable fiefdom and the consumer is converted into a conditional occupant. The platform reserves for itself the power to terminate, automatically cancel licenses, suppress access to content and declare that the loss generates no refund, all under categories that sound legal, but function as the internal vocabulary of private governance Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e). The negativity of the model lies not only in the fact that the door can be closed. It lies in the fact that the “castle” calls the key a “license”, the confiscation a “termination”, names its own convenience as “best interest”, and its own judgment as “just cause”.134

The contract observed is not a pathological exception. It is the legal form in which the technofeudal structure states itself in writing, and for this reason it makes it possible to recognize, at the macro level, the same institutional design that organizes the ecosystem. Therefore, in the games ecosystem, this structure p. 156 can be observed particularly clearly precisely because enjoyment does not occur through the simple acquisition of the “product” in the classic sense.

When “acquiring” a game, the player enters as assignee into a software use contract, while at the same time submitting to intermediate contractual instruments entered into with digital sales and maintenance platforms, through which the good is distributed and continuously administered. The consumer relationship, thus, is born already coupled to a private infrastructure that controls the access channel, continuity and practical conditions of enjoyment, converting the game into a governed environment and not a one-off delivery.

This coupling is reinforced by the predominant contractual form in the industry itself, exemplified by the case under study and marked by mass contracting, adhesion and consumption on a global scale, with general and generic contracts aimed at binding suppliers to a multiplicity of consumers in different jurisdictions. In this architecture, the instruments that structure the consumer-supplier relationship operate as dynamic realities oriented toward an animus of continuity of the company in the market and of the consumer-company relationship, shifting the center of the experience from “buying and owning” to “adhering and remaining”. It is this feature that gives the figure of the cloud vassal an empirical body, since access and enjoyment prove inseparable from the infrastructural and contractual mediation that organizes the very space in which consumption takes place.

The very object delivered by the interactive entertainment industry already tends to require this continuous governance, since the game takes shape as a complex audiovisual experience and as an organized compound of multiple intellectual goods and related services, exploited through an equally complex contractual network. This reinforces the point that recurring monetization is not a mere commercial adornment, but an institutional structure typical of a form of consumption whose “good” is inseparable from the ecosystem that administers it.

At the same time, Varoufakis135 introduces an important nuance. He recognizes that the cloudalists may appear, at first sight, to be intensified capitalists, because they needed to invest p. 157 massively in technology and build, from scratch, the material conditions of the new power. Even so, he insists that the purpose of the investment is no longer to compete within capitalist markets, but to make the market dispensable in practice, by replacing it with private domains of access and with rent extraction.

For Varoufakis, the concept of cloud capital136 constitutes the core of technofeudalism. The force of this category does not lie in suggesting something ethereal, since the cloud is a metaphor resting on a massive material base. Cloud capital requires, on the one hand, a planetary physical infrastructure, composed of data warehouses, servers, sensors and cables, which makes possible the continuous capture and processing of interactions.

On the other hand, it is also defined137 as an accumulation of networked machinery, communications hardware and, inseparably, AI-based software and algorithms, that is, a technical-economic architecture capable of automating decisions and modulating conduct at scale. The centrality of cloud capital, therefore, is not only quantitative, but qualitative, since it is a produced means of command and behavior modification, whose economic power derives from its infrastructural position.

In this arrangement, the reproduction mechanism of cloud capital differs from industrial capital because it can dispense with wage labor in the classic sense. It reproduces itself, to a large extent, by imposing the free contribution of users who, by interacting, posting, rating, circulating and remaining, feed the very stock of cloud capital138.

p. 158 Varoufakis makes this point even sharper by maintaining that one of the singularities of the system (which he describes as a “true revolution”139) is to convert billions of people into cloud serfs willing to “work for free” to reproduce cloud capital as profit for its owners. And, when he descends to the empirical level, the author radicalizes the thesis of value. The most valuable portion of the stock of cloud capital would lie not in its physical components, but in the content and traces generated by users themselves, such as posts, videos, photos, comments, ratings, movements and interactions of every kind, which become the raw material of platform power. The capture, therefore, is not only of “data” as an abstract object, but of patterns of behavior and interaction that sustain the capacity to command and modulate the environment.

At the same time, the author recognizes that this transformation may be poorly described by inherited labels, such as hypercapitalism, platform capitalism or rentier capitalism, because, for him, these terms do not capture the great transformation under way. The nuance is that not all elements of the social system change at the same time, since capital, labor and money persist, but come to operate under a distinct arrangement of coordination and command. Even so, Varoufakis insists on the decisive criterion that this is a system driven by rent, and not by profit, which would justify the need for a new concept to describe it.

For the author, digital platforms are not “digital markets” in the proper sense, even though they may present themselves as such. They must be read as contemporary fiefdoms because they replace the characteristic medium of capitalism (the decentralized market) with a private domain of access, in which economic coordination ceases to result from open interaction between agents and comes to be administered by a proprietary infrastructure. The feudal image gains density precisely because it describes a regime of participation. One enters, p. 159 circulates and remains under conditions imposed by the platform's lordship, which converts access into a systematic source of extraction.

The decisive difference, for Varoufakis, is that the platform-fiefdom manages to recentralize what the market would disperse. The mechanism is technical and political at the same time, since, by isolating buyers from one another and sellers from one another, algorithmic logic concentrates, in a single center of command, the power to connect, order and rank exchanges. The opposite of the decentralization that defines the market as a form. Thus, what looks like “competition” within the environment operates under a regime of visibility, ranking and permissions, in which the platform not only intermediates, but governs the territory.

By describing platforms as digital fiefdoms, Varoufakis offers a useful shift for consumption. The center of power lies not in the good supplied, but in the control of the point of passage. The fiefdom is, above all, a regime of access. Whoever controls the infrastructure controls the conditions of entry, permanence and circulation, and this includes both technical decisions (architecture and protocols) and administrative decisions (internal rules, sanctions, moderation, enforcement etc.). The rent extracted depends not only on selling something, but on administering the territory in which economic and symbolic life takes place.

This key becomes visible when Varoufakis treats140 certain ecosystems (Amazon, Google and Meta) not as peripheral examples, but as limit cases that make the logic of technofeudalism visible. He thus describes the practical replacement of the market by private domains of access, the reproduction of cloud capital through the capture of activity and information, and the extraction of value through rent and tolls rather than through competitive profit. Amazon appears as the prototype because, once inside its environment, the user no longer operates in a “market” in the capitalist sense, but in an administered territory, in which buying and selling take place under proprietary governance. The same grammar recurs in the relationship with sellers. This is not neutral intermediation, but the grant of operational space in exchange for recurring fees, watched over by an algorithmic apparatus that disciplines and collects. And, when Varoufakis invokes devices such as Alexa141, he reinforces that the asymmetry is not only economic, but p. 160 also existential. The user is reduced to a minimal node within a cloud-based network of command, operating under forces beyond their control.

Google142, in turn, shows that technofeudal power is also consolidated through infrastructures of operating system, store and distribution, capable of turning developers and producers into dependents of the channel. In the description of the construction of mobile ecosystems, the app store emerges as a pole of attraction for external production that makes it possible to extract a fixed commission as a form of access rent Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 110). Moreover, Google illustrates the surveillance and prediction arm of cloud capital. Knowing what one sees, what one reads, what one buys and whom one meets is not a detail, but a condition of command over behavior and monetization143. The Huawei-Android144 case thus functions as a geopolitical marker of technofeudal infrastructure. Control over systems and permissions reorganizes the dispute between States and turns technological restrictions into an instrument of a digital cold war.

Meta (owner of the platforms Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp), for its part, appears as a laboratory of the reproduction of cloud capital through user-generated content and traces: posts, photos, videos and interactions are treated as a decisive part of the stock that sustains p. 161 platform power. Here the examples do not operate as mere illustration. They are the very mechanics of the argument, because it is the user's continuous production of material and of patterns of behavior that allows the reproduction of the system. It is also for this reason that competition between platforms does not behave like market competition. It approaches a migration between fiefdoms that offer distinct experiences within the same logic of servitude145. Finally, Varoufakis reinforces the anthropological dimension of this capture by describing the decomposition of the liberal individual into fragments of data and manipulable identities, converting subjectivity into matter for algorithmic governance.

In this picture, exploitation also changes shape. Varoufakis likens the relationship between platform and sellers to the old relationship between suzerain and vassal, because the platform grants operational spaces within its own territory and charges, in return, a recurring fee, while an algorithmic apparatus watches, disciplines and collects as the administration of the fiefdom. The image does not function only as a descriptive device. It is formalized, in the conceptual apparatus of technofeudalism, by the figure of the capitalist vassals, producers who remain capitalists in production, but whose ability to sell comes to depend on access to proprietary digital territories.

In the ecosystem observed, the figure of the cloud vassal expands the horizon beyond the traditional figure of the consumer who pays to play for their own final benefit. It encompasses all agents whose income, visibility or economic viability depend on access to an environment governed by someone else. The streamer who produces content and monetizes an audience, the pro player whose career is conditioned by rules, calendar and permissions, the coach who sells pedagogical services about a specific game, and even advertisers and sponsors who buy exposure in the competitive circuit, all operate under the same logic of structural dependence, because their activity exists only while the platform keeps the channel open and recognizes the conditions of participation.

It is, moreover, a layered vassalage. The same agent may depend simultaneously on the owner of the ecosystem and on intermediaries that control distribution, visibility, monetization, the circulation of credit and the processing of payments. The result is a network of concurrent private subordinations, in which multiple gateways accumulate p. 162 and raise the cost of exit. The more agents depend on this infrastructure to exist economically, the more the toll ceases to be merely a price of access and comes to function as a condition for remaining in a private market.

The defining feature is dependence on the channel. Companies that previously competed for consumers on price, quality and physical distribution now compete for positions of visibility and conditions of access within the fiefdom. Terrestrial capital remains, but no longer commands the gateways of consumption. A significant part of the economic cost then shifts from production to mediation: one pays to appear, reach, convert and maintain presence. The platform, by arbitrating passage, transforms intermediation into recurring rent.

This reordering appears as hierarchical conversion. The dependence of the so-called capitalist vassals can be described as dependence on the channel, since companies that previously competed for consumers on price, quality and physical distribution come to compete for positions of visibility and conditions of access within the fiefdom. In this scenario, a relevant part of the cost ceases to be a cost of production and comes to be a cost of mediation, paid to appear, to reach and to maintain presence, while the platform, by arbitrating passage, transforms intermediation into a source of recurring rent.

Varoufakis Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 111) maintains that the category of capitalist vassal is not limited to the small producer. The decisive criterion is structural dependence on the channel, which can reach large and small agents alike whenever sale, visibility and monetization depend on ecosystems controlled by third parties. The interest for lies in the form of appropriation: part of the net income is captured by whoever controls the digital territory in which the transaction becomes possible146. The author (2024, p. 112) sharpens the diagnosis by describing the permanent threat of exclusion as a technique of government, since the mere removal of a link, or a drop in visibility in central mechanisms, can make a producer disappear from the digital world, amounting to an aseptic technical terror that sustains the p. 163 discipline of the fiefdom147. Even so, Varoufakis preserves a relevant nuance. Profit does not disappear as an ambition of economic agents and as a cultural horizon of advancement, but its systemic status changes, since accumulation may become dissociated from profit as its central fuel and shift the focus to the recurring extraction associated with the regime of access.

In this way, the extraction of value would occur through two main fronts. On the one hand, the “cloud serf” users148 assume a structural position in which the majority of humanity once again contributes to the wealth and power of the new ruling class through unpaid labor, now inscribed in the ordinary routines of use and participation in proprietary digital environments.

Servitude, here, is not defined by direct physical coercion, but by material subjection mediated by infrastructure and access, since the user remains within the fiefdom and permanence itself is converted into the production of value for third parties, in a regime in which rent is extracted through the administration of the territory and the continuous capture of activity. This shift, however, does not eliminate wage labor. It reconfigures the power of command p. 164 over the workforce by shifting it to cloud-based algorithmic governance, intensifying rhythms, fragmenting tasks and increasing the vulnerability of the working class under its control, which he calls the “cloud proletariat”149.

Precarization appears clearly when task work and the so-called gig economy - the economy derived from the well-known phenomenon of “uberization” - are coupled to the fiefdom. Platforms connect their digital territories to providers and charge a fixed commission on earnings, converting the circulation of precarized labor into cloud rent150. Varoufakis radicalizes this diagnosis by describing the so-called “algorithm-bosses” as command systems capable of reducing paid hours, accelerating rhythms to levels of exhaustion and dismissing workers for “inefficiency”, reinforcing technofeudal discipline through the permanent risk of exclusion151. On the other hand, traditional companies (“capitalist vassals”) would pay for the capacity to interact with consumers, thereby perpetuating a deep and inexhaustible system of dependence.

p. 165 Technofeudalism, however, does not precarize only by reducing guarantees or intensifying rhythms. It reorganizes the material conditions of collective action by fragmenting the working class and diluting traditional points of cohesion. This fragmentation appears, first, in the coexistence of distinct regimes of exploitation. Increasingly precarious wage workers make up a cloud proletariat, while almost everyone acts as a cloud serf, by contributing freely to the accumulation of cloud capital and to the consolidation of the cloud fiefdoms that replace markets Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 201). The consequence is a class spread across different categories, intensities and bonds, shifting the axis of resistance from the classic workplace to a diffuse ecosystem of mediation and access.

Next, Varoufakis points to a directly anti-union vector. The automation of command and management by cloud-based systems creates “algorithm-bosses” capable of redesigning chains and routes to avoid points where unions could organize workers, to the point where unions are not even in a position to form152. At the same time, discipline comes to operate through opaque and unappealable decisions, producing a “Kafkaesque spiral153” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 74) of dismissal without any human counterpart. Labor conflict is thus shifted to an environment in which control is calculated, anticipatory and embedded in the very logistical design of the system.

Varoufakis identifies an additional material barrier, since the physical isolation between cloud serfs and cloud proletarians, mediated by personal screens and by devices that supervise work, reduces opportunities for encounter and hinders the construction of solidarity and collective action154. However, he introduces a relevant nuance. The same p. 166 cloud capital that isolates also provides potential rebels with the capacity to create coalitions and act through the cloud itself, opening the possibility of resistance155. Hence the proposal of a broad coalition, bringing together the traditional and cloud proletariat, cloud serfs and even part of the capitalist vassals, as a condition for weakening technofeudalism156.

Varoufakis shifts the decisive stock of cloud capital to what users themselves produce, feed and replicate daily: content, records, interactions, ratings, traces, movements and patterns of behavior. The point is not only that data exist, but that the system depends on the daily reproduction of this material to maintain and expand its capacity for command, prediction and monetization157. Unpaid labor p. 167 ceases to be an accident and becomes a constitutive component: users produce, outside the market as a form, the most valuable elements of cloud capital.

Varoufakis adds an uncomfortable nuance: this servitude is often voluntary and even pleasurable, which does not make it any less structural. On the contrary, it tends to stabilize the regime, because daily adherence reproduces the very capital that sustains the platform. Servitude is also dependence on access. Users remain bound to devices and environments that come to direct their conduct and choices, exercising power of command over ordinary life Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 72-79). Ultimately, the capture of identity, social memory, contacts, histories, files and bonds makes exit costly, if not unfeasible, without significant loss158.

In dialogue with Marx159, the decisive difference lies in the mode in which subordination appears. It no longer needs to present itself only as the discipline of labor in the factory, p. 168 with time, space and command concentrated. On platforms, capture spreads through ordinary life, converting attention, interaction, sociability and traces into inputs for the reproduction of cloud capital. The user is not a wage worker in this circuit, but participates in the production of the value that governs them. Exploitation becomes harder to perceive because it appears as use, leisure, personal expression or convenience.

Varoufakis (2024, p. 154) maintains that technofeudalism not only shifts accumulation to rent, but dismantles the political conditions that made the social-democratic repertoire viable. If, in industrial capitalism, social-democratic governments could arbitrate conflicts because they counted on strong unions and on industrialists sensitive to the regulatory threat, in the new scenario the cloud elite ceases to fear this kind of counterweight. The cloud proletariat tends to be too weak to organize robust unions, and cloud serfs do not even perceive themselves as producers160. The consequence is that the typical distributive bargain (regulation, compromise and taxation) loses its material base.

In the same movement, Varoufakis argues that classic tools of economic regulation begin to fail because they do not fit their object. Price regulation, for example, becomes irrelevant when the key services to the end user are “free” or the cheapest on the market, because the central extraction depends not on price margins, but on tolls and rents charged for access and intermediation161. In parallel, the classic model of breaking up monopolies (in the manner of the Standard Oils162) would lose technical p. 169 and political applicability. The question of “how to divide” cloud conglomerates is not decorative rhetoric, but indicates that the architecture of cloud capital cannot be broken up the way “terrestrial” physical capital was.

For this reason, Varoufakis states directly that, under this arrangement, social democracy becomes impossible163. The impossibility is not moral, but structural. The new regime of power captures the space of decision and shifts the axis of conflict, including through political dynamics that can neutralize the redistributive agenda while keeping intact the extractive power associated with cloud capital164. In this reading, the party system may become stuck on a board in which the central forces and their alternatives end up subordinated to the new elite, reorganizing the political dispute under technofeudal dependence.

Even so, Varoufakis identifies a point of regulatory pressure that the cloud lords would genuinely fear. It is interoperability, understood as the possibility for the user to migrate between fiefdoms without losing data, histories and collections, that is, without paying the p. 170 exit cost that sustains dependence165. Here, the target is no longer “price” or “size” but the design of access and portability, that is, the mechanism that transforms infrastructure into recurring rent.

The two fronts of extraction are complementary and mutually reinforcing. The more the user remains and interacts, the more valuable access to them becomes; the more companies pay to reach this user, the more resources the platform has to intensify mechanisms of retention, personalization and segmentation. The result is a closed circuit in which attention, data and payment for visibility feed back into one another, converting everyday circulation itself into the foundation of the fiefdom's rent.

This picture echoes Marx's classic intuition about the asymmetry between those who control the conditions of production and those who depend on them to participate in economic life.166 p. 171 In technofeudalism, control is exercised not only over the factory, but over the infrastructure of access and over the rules of circulation. The legal-economic point of interest here is that dependence ceases to be only wage dependence; it also becomes dependence on permanence, visibility and reputation within private systems.

In regulatory terms, the point is not that traditional tools “disappear”, but that they miss the target. Measures designed for markets in corporeal goods and industrial chains have difficulty reaching transnational structures organized around control of infrastructure, closed ecosystems and technical standardization. The problem is no longer only to repress isolated conduct; it comes to involve intervening in the design of access and dependence, where rent is generated.

When Varoufakis speaks of the obsolescence of traditional instruments, the point can be read as a displacement of the regulatory target. Measures designed for markets in corporeal goods and industrial chains face difficulties in reaching transnational structures that operate through control of infrastructures, closed ecosystems and technical standardization. The question is no longer only to punish isolated conduct, but to intervene in the institutional design of access and dependence, where rent is generated.

In Varoufakis, the rise of technofeudalism is presented as a double political-social effect. On the one hand, there is concentration of power in the owners of platforms and of cloud capital. On the other, there is the consequent explosion of inequality, accompanied by democratic erosion and corrosion of institutional trust. The argument starts from a reconfiguration of classes in which the elite owning cloud capital becomes the dominant pole, while the rest of society regresses to positions of subordination, including through universalized forms of exploitation based on rent and on unpaid labor167. In this framing, inequality ceases to be an occasional by-product and p. 172 comes to appear as a structural consequence. The regime of exploitation itself functions as the engine of deepening inequality. Varoufakis further adds a relevant historical inflection, by suggesting that the recent increase in inequality does not derive only from the rich growing “faster”, but also from a picture in which incomes at the bottom fall while financialization and large corporations accumulate gains, reinforcing the asymmetry168.

On the democratic plane, erosion does not appear only as “capture of the State” in the classic sense, but as a reorganization of the subject and of public space. Varoufakis describes the collapse of the autonomy of the liberal individual when cloud capital comes to decompose identity into fragments of data and to manipulate choices, producing subjects less “owners of themselves” and more possessed by infrastructures of behavioral modulation169. In this environment, negative emotions become functionally profitable. Intolerance and hatred come to be described as intrinsic to the algorithmic economy, because extraction mechanisms tend to optimize rents precisely where attention is fixed by frustration and conflict170.

p. 173 Institutional corrosion, in turn, manifests itself in two layers. The first is macro. While precariousness deepens, democracies kneel before the wishes of capital, in a scenario of asymmetry that empties the promise of effective popular government. The second is micro-infrastructural, since platforms hold users' digital lives hostage, including contacts, histories and collections. This practical capture makes regulation politically more difficult, because leaving the fiefdom entails substantial losses for citizens themselves and thereby limits the reach of traditional institutional responses. Ultimately, Varoufakis rounds off the diagnosis171 with a provocative formulation, suggesting that one of the few actors capable of imposing limits on the cloud lords may emerge from a non-liberal context, which functions as a denunciation of the inability of Western democracies to contain technofeudal power.

Especially relevant to the central discussion of this work is the concept of demand monopoly addressed by Varoufakis (2024), because it names a form of power that does not come down to monopoly “of supply”, nor to the formal exclusivity typical of classical competition law, but consists, rather, in the capacity to capture and govern the path to the consumer.

Although Varoufakis does not stabilize this diagnosis solely through a traditional “competition” vocabulary, he provides instruments172 to understand it also as power on the demand side, mobilizing categories such as oligopsony and monopsony173 to p. 174 distinguish structures of concentration that operate less through price and more through control of the conditions of the encounter between supply and demand.

In the appendix174 to his work, when articulating the notion of monopsony rent in the labor market, the author reinforces this structural intuition, since the asymmetry on the demand side, marked by few employers, makes it possible to capture surplus by compressing wages below the exchange value of labor and thereby converts purchasing power into extraction.

In this sense, the demand monopoly operates as a monopoly of organized access. Endowed with the monopoly of attention and armed with the data produced within their ecosystems, the cloudalists come to erect entry barriers that operate not only through legal impediment, but through the practical closing of the routes of discovery, recommendation, payment and sociability, converting access to the public into a prerogative administered within the digital fiefdoms. The decisive point is that, by replacing markets with cloud fiefdoms, power is not limited to charging more, as a classic monopolist would, since it comes to administer the very possibility of choice, that is, the form and conditions in which buyers and sellers meet.

For this reason, Varoufakis insists that even the worst markets still preserve some degree of horizontal interaction (buyers can exchange information, form associations and even organize boycotts) whereas, in the world of the platform, algorithmic mediation blocks this common space and suppresses minimal counterweights, isolating demand and steering it along internal tracks175.

p. 175 The platform tends to concentrate, in one and the same environment, the mechanisms of visibility (search, ranking, algorithmic curation), of transaction (payment, logistics, marketplace) and of bond (network, reputation, communication), so that “competing” comes to mean disputing exposure under unilateral rules and under a topography defined by the lord of the fiefdom himself. Here, isolation is not a side effect, but algorithmically constructed isolation, in which the user does not participate in a public space of exchange and comes to circulate alone in a private territory whose architecture prevents the lateral communication typical of the market and recentralizes coordination in the owner. In consequence, demand is not only met, but organized and steered.

This dominion over demand is further projected as behavior modification. In describing connected devices and systems, Varoufakis maintains that cloud architecture acquires the capacity to direct perceived reality and to guide choices, that is, to exercise command over the user, shifting recommendation into a government of desire176. The circuit closes when the platform learns and, at the same time, conditions. It selects stimuli, exposes the user to images, texts and videos to shape preferences and, in this recursiveness, turns experience itself into a method of capture177.

Freedom of choice, therefore, does not disappear as an abstract possibility, but becomes more expensive in practice. The demand monopoly is stabilized by switching costs and by material barriers to exit, since there is no need to prohibit competitors; it is enough to make migration p. 176 burdensome by holding collections and bonds hostage, so that formal freedom to leave exists, but material freedom becomes severely more costly.

A direct development of this demand monopoly, still little explored in traditional legal language, is the capacity for internal inflationary control of the ecosystem. When the platform concentrates access, retains collections and bonds and controls the means of transaction, it not only influences prices, but comes to administer the very regime of price formation. By defining the catalog, modulating scarcity, managing windows of opportunity and operating internal currencies, bundles and conversions, the supplier comes to calibrate the general level of values charged, gradually raising price references and shifting anchors of acceptability without facing the discipline typical of markets with open supply and demand. Inflation, here, does not derive from material scarcity, but from economic governance by design. The ceiling ceases to be an impersonal market limit and comes to be the consumer's behavioral tolerance, measured and tested by the supplier itself, in an environment in which exit is burdensome, external comparison is hindered and the auditability of the equivalence between expenditure and delivery is structurally reduced.

Internal inflationary control, in this type of ecosystem, is not only an effect of economic design. It is also an effect of writing. Riot does not describe in-game currency as a simple neutral medium of exchange. It treats it as part of a closed circuit, whose equivalence can be recalibrated unilaterally. The terms admit that, “sometimes”, the company may alter the purchasing power of the Game Currency, including by increasing the number of coins needed to acquire virtual products such as skins, and place this movement under the generic promise of notice and progressiveness Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e)178. Under technofeudalism, this is the anatomy of the toll. The platform not only sells, it administers the internal regime of value, and does so from the point of view of the lordship, that is, with the power to redefine what the currency buys.

There is a second step that makes the picture darker than mere repricing. The contract expressly denies any value attributed to data residing on servers operated or controlled by Riot, including Game Content and Virtual Content associated with the account179. The denial is not symbolic. It is functional. If the collection is legally described p. 177 as having no value, internal inflation and the loss of purchasing power come to be treated as a natural variation of the fiefdom, not as the deterioration of an economic position relevant to the consumer Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e). The result is that the consumer finds themselves trapped in an economy in which the unit of account and the recognition of value are defined by the supplier itself, and the supplier further reserves the power to say that, in the last analysis, it is worth nothing180.

Technofeudalism also appears in the way the contract transforms experience into a risk assumed by the user, and this movement is coherent with the toll economy. In a disclaimer clause, Riot states that the Services are provided “as is” and “as available”, without warranties, and that the user assumes full responsibility for use Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e)181. The fiefdom presents itself as indispensable infrastructure and, at the same time, seeks to shield itself as if it provided only a contingent availability, which promises no accuracy, reliability or quality of content.

Finally, the castle tries to limit the reach of the response for harm to a metric that, in the world of administered objects, is almost a cruel joke. The limitation clause provides that total liability may be limited to the amounts paid by the user in the 12 months preceding the arising of the cause, and further excludes unforeseeable losses and indirect damages, except to the extent that it would be illegal to do so Riot Games(Riot Games, 2024e). The closure is technofeudal par excellence. The platform reserves the power to govern value, currency and access within the territory, but tries to downgrade the civil response for losses to a calculation that ignores precisely what makes the fiefdom efficient, which is prolonged accumulation, dependence and the cost of exit.

p. 178 In this context, it is appropriate to distinguish precisely between the lawful formation of prices under a competitive regime and a usurpation of pricing power through governance. It must be recognized that certain agents may, often legitimately, act as price-setters when their position derives from a verifiable combination of recognized innovation protected within the limits of the system, consolidated reputation and competitive contestability, that is, the real possibility of substitution by competitors and of consumer migration without prohibitive costs.

In material markets, price formation tends to remain subject to external brakes because demand finds alternatives182. In these markets, comparison is viable and p. 179 competitive discipline operates as a limit, even when there is differentiation through trademarks, patents, industrial designs or other intellectual property rights legitimately consolidated under the international trade regime. What is observed, however, in immaterial consumption governed by a platform, is an inversion of logic. The supplier does not limit itself to competing within a market. It designs the very world of reference in which consumption occurs, controls catalog, visibility, rules of circulation, practical unit of account and conditions of permanence, and then comes to exercise, simultaneously, functions analogous to those of the market and of normative authority, defining on its own the level of prices and the pace of internal inflation.

This is an appropriation of the position of price-setter without the counterweights that legitimize it, since exit is burdensome, external comparison is artificially hindered (potentially made impossible) and the value of the object depends on parameters controlled unilaterally by the supplier itself. The result is an escalation in which the cost of exit grows and captivity is consolidated. In extreme cases, the threat of losing belonging, reputation and perceived utility may lead the consumer to accept growing expenses or economically disadvantageous conditions to preserve their position in the ecosystem. The rationality of staying, in this picture, no longer derives only from the objective value of the item, but from the symbolic, social and functional cost of disconnection.

The consequences do not appear as a list external to the concept, but as its unfolding. There is erosion of the effective power of choice, because navigation takes place along previously configured routes. There is expansion of extraction grounded in unpaid labor, insofar as data and attention become a recurring condition of participation. There is, further, reinforcement of political asymmetries, because control of the informational flow and of visibility can hinder social organization, resistance and contestation. The platform does not compete only on price: it defines the conditions of the encounter, the switching cost and the forms of exposure.

The geopolitical dimension, in this context, constitutes an enlargement of scale of the same problem. If the economic territory comes to be the cloud, disputes between States tend to be reorganized around infrastructures, standards and monetary-technological regimes capable of sustaining or blocking transnational digital ecosystems. The legal interest of the reference does not lie in reconstructing a diplomatic history, but in recording that economic sovereignty p. 180 also comes to be exercised through technical architectures and proprietary regimes that cross borders, reconfigure dependencies and strain classic instruments of regulation.

Varoufakis places this new cold war183 at the heart of technofeudalism by maintaining that the consolidation of cloud capital reorders interstate competition in terms of infrastructural hegemony. The axis of conflict184 shifts to the command of digital ecosystems, payment regimes and currencies compatible with technofeudal extraction. The tendency, for the author, is the bifurcation of the world system into antagonistic digital superfiefdoms, with events such as the freezing of Russian reserves serving as turning points regarding the political risk of depending on monetary channels controlled by another power185 Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 136-141). At the same time, the intensification of alternatives, such as digital currencies, appears as a strategy for bypassing international transfer channels controlled by the hegemonic power186. Finally, the author suggests global distributive effects, with Europe and the Global p. 181 South pressed to choose between territories, standards and currencies, at the cost of lost autonomy and increased vulnerability187.

In conclusion, Varoufakis frames the “escape” from technofeudalism not as an incremental package of regulatory corrections, but as a change of regime. A passage to “another reality” (which he also names technodemocracy), founded on the socialization of the decisive instruments of production, distribution, collaboration and communication. The axis, therefore, is not to “contain the excesses” of platforms, but to reconfigure property rights and centers of decision so as to dismantle the logic of rent and command proper to cloud capital188.

On this horizon, resistance takes the form of radical democratization of the firm and of labor. Varoufakis proposes democratized companies in which the bond of participation is not a negotiable shareholding, but a political belonging. Each worker p. 182 would receive a single inalienable share, with one vote, so that internal governance would become a mechanism of collective decision and would reduce the structural separation between labor and corporate command189.

The same reasoning extends to the infrastructural foundations of technofeudalism. Varoufakis treats land and cloud as commons. The idea is to remove the capacity for rentier extraction that derives from exclusive control over physical territory and over digital territory, that is, infrastructure and data. In the alternative design, he imagines local governance arrangements such as municipal associations capable of administering land use and redistributing rents to finance housing and social provisions, connecting planning, property and distributive justice190.

On the strictly digital plane, Varoufakis proposes an architecture of digital rights and remuneration to replace the “deception” of free services. The core is a Digital Bill of Rights that ensures the individual the choice of which data to sell and to whom, combined with a micropayments platform (“a penny for your thoughts”) that shifts monetization from surveillance to a regime in which data and consent come to have explicit political and economic form191.

Finally, interoperability appears as a nerve point feared by the cloud lords because it reduces the cost of exit and weakens the fiefdom's power of lock-in. By p. 183 allowing migration between platforms without substantive loss of data, collections and histories, interoperability attacks the mechanism that transforms dependence into recurring rent; for this reason, it functions, in the argument, as a technical condition of possibility for any effective democratization of the ecosystem.

For all these reasons, Varoufakis concludes his study with the view that technofeudalism does not represent a transition of capitalism, but rather a complete and effective paradigmatic transformation that, in turn, will require new forms of social and political organization. Confronting the emerging system will require a deep understanding of its structural dynamics and the proposal of solutions that can rebalance relations of economic and social power and thus promote a more equitable and democratic future.

With the macroeconomic perspective of the technofeudal system established, the next step is to show how the same reality is expressed, through another theoretical lens, as an economy of rentierism and of demand monopoly. In Dowbor, the core is not the cloud as fiefdom, but the control of informational and financial circuits capable of imposing recurring extractions, not as market price, but as systematic rent. It is this passage that makes it possible to translate, in dogmatic terms, the vulnerability intrinsic to immaterial objects of consumption as an interpreted dimension of the factual vulnerability of Article 4, item I, of the CDC, justifying reinforced duties of transparency, loyalty and information. It also prepares the recognition of platform design as a potential unlawful act when the very architecture of the service is designed to exploit, repeatedly, the consumer's inability to audit the promised functional value.

En el capitalismo, entender el significado de la renta y distinguirla del beneficio es mucho más complejo; se trata de una dificultad de la que fui testigo directo cuando era profesor universitario y me esforzaba por ayudar a mis alumnos a distinguir ambas cosas. No existe una diferencia aritmética: tanto la renta como el beneficio son el dinero que queda una vez que se han pagado los costes. La diferencia es más sutil, cualitativa, casi abstracta: el beneficio es vulnerable a la competencia de mercado, y la renta no lo es. El motivo estriba en sus diferentes orígenes. La renta deriva del acceso privilegiado a bienes cuya oferta es rígida, como el suelo fértil o las tierras que contienen combustibles fósiles; no se pueden producir más recursos de este tipo, por mucho dinero que se invierta. El beneficio, por el contrario, acaba en los bolsillos de los empresarios que han invertido en cosas que de otro modo no existirían —por ejemplo, la bombilla de Edison o el iPhone de Jobs—. El hecho de que estas mercancías hayan sido inventadas y creadas por alguien, y de que, por lo tanto, otra persona pueda inventar y crear una versión mejor, es lo que hace que el beneficio sea vulnerable a la competencia.” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 106- 107). ↩

Varoufakis describes the algorithm-bosses (algo-jefes) as a qualitative mutation of precarization because command over labor no longer depends on human mediation and operates instead as automatic discipline, without the brakes of empathy, negotiation or explanation. For this thesis, the point is less the political strategy for overcoming technofeudalism and more the perception that the infrastructure that organizes access and enjoyment also organizes isolation, comparison, dispersion and difficulty of collective contestation.   42. For Marx ([2013]), the passage from manufacture to large-scale industry reorganizes the subordination of labor as a systemic effect of machinery and of the factory regime. Discipline no longer depends only on immediate personal command, but on the objective form of the productive process and its internal administration. That is why, in describing the factory, Marx ([2013], p. 608-609) observes that technical subordination to the “uniform motion” of the instrument of labor “creates a barrack-like discipline, which develops into a complete factory regime”, with its own supervision and hierarchization. In this same key, the internal normativity of the establishment appears as a kind of private juridicity of capital: “in place of the whip”, there arises the “factory supervisor's book of penalties”, that is, a regular device of command and sanction that stabilizes employer power as government of time and of the body within the productive unit. The point that matters to the argument is that, in Marx, subordination becomes legible as a form of the process: it materializes in machinery, in rhythm and in the discipline immanent to the factory system, which converts technical coordination into social command. It is precisely this intuition (command as material organization of the conditions of activity) that Varoufakis (2024, p. 80-81) moves outside the factory when he describes “cloud capital” as an infrastructure of extraction based on mediation and modification of behavior. When he states that cloud capital “automatizó […] el poder para mandar” and “transferió a los ‘algos’”, the author is naming a mutation in which the classic functions of command (over labor and consumption) cease to depend on the delimited productive space and come to operate, continuously, through digital architectures that govern flows of visibility, relevance and engagement. Hence the decisive formula for its fit with the notion of diffuse capture: “convertirnos […] en ‘siervos de la nube’”, providing “no remunerado” service to cloud capital, “sin la mediación de ningún mercado”. The “historical disruption”, therefore, is not merely the automation of tasks, but the automation of command and its redistribution through algorithmic devices that operate in ordinary everyday life. Not by chance, Varoufakis makes explicit that, by providing content and interactions, “somos nosotros quienes producimos y reproducimos […] el stock del capital en la nube”, even if voluntarily and pleasurably; and defines the “third nature” of this capital as “un medio producido de modificación del comportamiento”, capable of ordering consumers and converting social participation into reproduction of the cloud capital stock itself Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 75-76). It is the passage from disciplinary command concentrated in the factory regime to infrastructural command distributed through algorithmic mediations that sustains, in the body of the work, the idea that subordination can operate as diffuse capture of social activity, converting attention, time and behavior into continuous economic input: the platform does not “compel” through classic coercion, but governs the material and semiotic conditions under which action becomes capable of conversion into value. ↩

Varoufakis maintains that the classic political base of social democracy comes undone when the union counterweight ceases to operate as a credible threat against the new elite, since the architecture of labor under the cloud fragments and weakens the capacity for collective organization. Varoufakis argues that the classic regulatory grammar of social democracy misses the target in technofeudalism because it takes price as the pressure point in a scenario in which domination is organized precisely outside it, given that the offer to the consumer is frequently presented as “free” or as the cheapest available, while the decisive extraction occurs in the form of rent and access tolls, charged to those who depend on the fief to reach users, circulate goods or simply exist in the digital infrastructure. In this sense he states directly that “La regulación de precios es irrelevante cuando los servicios que el consumidor necesita que se protejan ya son gratuitos o los más baratos del mercado” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 155), shifting the critique to the architecture of dependence and channel capture, where power is not measured by the margin at the counter, but by the capacity to condition access, visibility and participation through a recurring rent. ↩

Varoufakis suggests that the antitrust repertoire inherited from the era of “terrestrial” capital loses traction in technofeudalism because it presupposed material and separable objects, whose division was politically difficult, but technically relatively straightforward. In the classic example offered by the author, “Standard Oil“ (a trust famously dissolved by the US Supreme Court in 1911) could be sliced into regional units because its structure presented itself as a set of distributed assets and operations, susceptible to segmentation without altering the nature of what was sold. With cloud capital, however, the core of power is the integration between infrastructure, data and algorithms, so that “dividing” ceases to be merely a legal decision and becomes a technical-political problem about how to separate networks, identities, interoperability standards and command systems without recreating, by another route, the same fief. Varoufakis formulates the thesis head-on by maintaining that, once technofeudalism is consolidated, the political engineering typical of social democracy loses its point of support, because the social configuration that allowed the State to arbitrate between “capital” and “labor” through strong unions, price regulation and antitrust discipline no longer exists. In Marx, the asymmetry is founded on the separation and control of the objective conditions of labor, which make domination economically “mute” and structural; in Varoufakis, this logic is re-enacted in an environment in which the decisive conditions are not only physical means of production, but privatized infrastructures of mediation that govern access and circulation and convert dependence into a permanent regime of permanence and visibility. The Marxian reference delimits its structural starting point in the asymmetry between those who control the objective conditions of participation in economic life and those who depend on this control to exist economically. In “O Capital” (Book I), Marx formulates the question directly by indicating that the capitalist relation “presupposes the separation between the workers and the ownership of the conditions for the realization of labor” and that, once established, it “not only preserves this separation, but reproduces it on an ever-increasing scale” ([2013], p. 961). Therefore, “the process that creates the capital relation” is precisely the process of separation that “transforms the social means of subsistence and production into capital” and, on the other side, “converts the direct producers into wage workers” ([2013], p. 961). The normative center of this description is that domination need not present itself as permanent direct violence. It stabilizes itself as structural dependence, given that the subject is “free” on the legal-formal plane, but materially compelled to adhere to the conditions imposed by those who control the means. This passage helps sustain the idea that subordination can operate through mediations that dispense with the “whip”. Marx condenses this in the formula of “mute compulsion”: “the mute compulsion exercised by economic relations seals the domination of the capitalist over the worker” ([2013], p. 983); and, although extra-economic violence “continue [...] to be employed”, it appears “only exceptionally”, since “for the usual course of things” it suffices to entrust the worker to the “natural laws of production”, that is, to the “dependence in which he himself finds himself in relation to capital” ([2013], p. 984). What matters to the argument is that, from this perspective, domination derives from differential control of the conditions of access to the social process of production and circulation, and not only from personified coercion or explicit command. It is on the plane of control of conditions and of structural dependence that Varoufakis's reading can be inserted as an update by infrastructural displacement. In technofeudalism, asymmetry is not concentrated only in the factory and in the wage contract, but in private ownership of digital infrastructures that define the economic “right of passage”: platforms function as means of access, circulation and visibility. Hence, for Varoufakis (2024, p. 154), the nubelistas do not fear unions because “los proletarios de la nube son demasiado débiles para crear uno y los siervos de la nube ni siquiera se consideran productores”, indicating that dependence ceases to be organized primarily as collective wage dependence and comes to be experienced as individualized adherence to systems of mediation. More decisive still, he describes dependence as vulnerability of permanence and of traffic: “Todo capitalista vasallo sabe que si se elimina un enlace de su sitio en la nube podría perder el acceso a la mayor parte de sus clientes […] podrían desaparecer por completo del mundo online”, and concludes that “el tecnofeudalismo se fundamenta en un tecnoterror aséptico”. In this framework, the Marxian “mute compulsion” reappears in the form of an infrastructural coercion: not necessarily physical violence or explicit command, but a continuous threat of de-indexing, de-ranking, banning and loss of network — that is, loss of visibility, reputation and access to the circuits of circulation. ↩

The structural consequence, for the argument, is that the immaterial object consumed on the platform already comes coupled to a regime of algorithmic curation that simultaneously records and manufactures dispositions, converting the experience of choice into a sequence of micro-adjustments that reinforces dependence on the channel and expands the commanding capacity of cloud capital. ↩

  65. To clarify the concepts of the argument, the thesis draws on the work of the economist Hal R. Varian. Doctor of Economics from the University of California (Berkeley), Hal R. Varian is a professor, researcher and economist recognized for his contribution to microeconomics and the economics of information. His work helps to fix in a technical-economic sense the notion of price makers employed here. In particular, the work Intermediate Microeconomics: A Modern Approach serves as the basis from which this note draws the distinctions between price-taking firm and price-making firm, as well as correlated categories of market power and mechanisms of price formation. In microeconomics, the basic distinction between competition and market power can be expressed by the difference between the agent who takes price as given and the agent who forms it. In the first case, the producer operates as a price taker, accepting price as an exogenous parameter and concentrating its decision on how much to produce Varian(Varian, 2010, p. 397). In the second case, there is a capacity to influence prices through control of the quantities supplied or demanded, which includes market power on the demand side, as occurs when the monopsonist behaves as a price maker Varian(Varian, 2010, p. 489). The analytical basis of this condition, in the limiting case of a single firm in the market, is that the demand faced by the firm coincides with market demand itself Varian(Varian, 2010, p. 396). Furthermore, a monopolistic structure, in the economic sense relevant to the analysis of market power, assumes that each firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve for its product Varian(Varian, 2010, p. 478). Taken together, these premises indicate that the firm with market power does not operate under a given price, but chooses a point on a demand curve, with direct implications for price and quantity. This structure makes it possible to formalize that the price charged can depart from marginal cost in the form of a markup, whose size depends on demand conditions, in particular on elasticity Varian(Varian, 2010, p. 443). In conceptual terms, the elasticity of demand functions as an economic brake on price formation, because the more sensitive the consumer is to price variations, the lower the capacity to sustain increases without relevant loss of quantity. For this reason, conditions that reduce effective substitution and increase switching costs tend to make demand less elastic and, with that, expand the feasible markup, shifting market power from the merely descriptive plane to a mechanism of surplus extraction through pricing. Within this frame, certain structural conditions and market instruments tend to reinforce the capacity for price formation. Barriers to entry, such as licenses and legal restrictions, limit the entry of new competitors, reduce contestability and mitigate competitive pressure even in the face of profit opportunities Varian(Varian, 2010, p. 416). Switching costs can become substantial when components function as systems and the replacement of one element requires replacing others, raising the cost of switching Varian(Varian, 2010, p. 674). When these costs are very high, lock-in (lock-in) can occur, a situation in which switching becomes virtually inconceivable, which is unfavorable to the consumer and, at the same time, attractive to the seller; in this condition, the demand of the locked-in user tends to be very inelastic, allowing the seller to raise prices to extract consumer surplus Varian(Varian, 2010, p. 674-675). Price discrimination strategies consist of selling units of the same product at different prices Varian(Varian, 2010, p. 462), and may assume different degrees according to market segmentation and the form of price differentiation between persons or between quantities purchased Varian(Varian, 2010, p. 462). Bundling (bundling) is part of this repertoire, consisting of the joint offer of related goods in a single package Varian(Varian, 2010, p. 474) and reducing the dispersion in willingness to pay, which may allow a higher price to be set for the set Varian(Varian, 2010, p. 474). Segmentation by quality can be operationalized through the deliberate reduction of the quality offered to the lower segment of the market, as a way of inducing self-selection and avoiding cannibalization of sales in the upper segment Varian(Varian, 2010, p. 467). Finally, search costs and costs of acquiring information can generate informational asymmetry and contribute to inefficient outcomes of adverse selection, as when low-quality items drive out high-quality ones because of the high cost of acquiring information Varian(Varian, 2010, p. 722). Taken together, these elements compose a framework in which price formation is not a mere abstract freedom to choose values, but a function of market structure, of the elasticity of demand, of substitution frictions and of the instruments of segmentation and surplus capture. ↩

The centrality of the geopolitical dimension is signaled in the very architecture of the book, when Varoufakis devotes an entire chapter to framing Sino-American rivalry as an expression of the technofeudal regime, titling it directly “El impacto global del tecnofeudalismo: la nueva guerra fría” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 126). In choosing this heading, he does not treat the “new cold war” as a backdrop external to the economic diagnosis, but as one of its modes of manifestation, that is, as a conflict organized around the control of cloud infrastructures and ecosystems and of the forms of dependence they produce. The author establishes a true bridge between the internal mutation of capitalism and international politics, making it possible to read state measures and technological disputes as part of the same arrangement of command and extraction that, in the previous chapters, had already displaced markets and profits in favor of fiefs and rent. ↩
  67. Varoufakis shifts the explanation of the intensification of the Sino-American confrontation from the “geographic” or merely state register to a register of political economy, suggesting that the rhetoric of “seguridad nacional” functions as a veneer for a dispute between technofeudal elites for command over infrastructures and for the preservation of systemic advantages. He makes this point explicit by stating that, “comparada con la Guerra Fría original, la nueva guerra fría tiene poco que ver con la política; está motivada por crudos intereses de clase tecnofeudales” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 135). The passage ties this motivation to the threat that the advance of Chinese “finanzas en la nube” would represent for the American poles of power, that is, for Wall Street and Silicon Valley, insofar as it would shift the “ventajas comparativas” of the previous arrangement toward the Chinese ruling class Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 135). Thus, the conflict appears less as a repetition of an ideological antagonism and more as an attempt to prevent a reconfiguration of infrastructural hegemony, in which the capacity to extract and command via the cloud becomes the decisive object of the rivalry. ↩
  68. Varoufakis describes the geopolitical bifurcation as a reorganization of the world's digital space into two blocs of command, in which rivalry does not operate only through tariffs, military alliances or classic “territorial” disputes, but through the consolidation of cloud infrastructures that function as domains of access and extraction. The author states that, under the combined impact of the war in Ukraine and the “gran inflación”, in an environment that “están empeorando la pobreza, agravando el cambio climático y generando una atmósfera de miedo”, “el mundo se está dividiendo en dos superfeudos en la nube antagónicos: uno estadounidense, el otro chino”, and that “no saldrá nada bueno y valioso de esta bifurcación” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 141). Read in continuity with the diagnosis that the new cold war is driven by “crudos intereses de clase tecnofeudales” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 135), this division into superfiefs appears as the geopolitical translation of platform power on a planetary scale, converting the dispute between States into a dispute over hegemonic digital ecosystems that condition dependencies, alignments and margins of autonomy outside the traditional terrain of market competition. ↩
  69. Varoufakis treats the freezing of Russian reserves as a turning point in the international monetary architecture because it makes visible, inescapably, that dollar hegemony is not merely a “market fact”, but a power of political blockade exercisable over sovereign wealth. The invasion of Ukraine functions as the trigger for a response that “cambió toda la ecuación”, when “la Reserva Federal congeló cientos de miles de millones de dólares que pertenecían al banco central ruso”, keeping them “dentro del circuito de pagos en dólares que Estados Unidos controla por completo” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 136). The consequence, for the author, is a systemic signal sent to the rest of the major holders of dollar assets, since “fue la primera vez en la historia capitalista que el dinero de un banco central importante era confiscado por otro banco central” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 136), making the search for alternative routes of settlement and reserve more rational and, in this way, contributing to the acceleration of the cleavage he describes as the division of the world into “dos superfeudos en la nube antagónicos” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 141). In presenting the digital yuan, Varoufakis frames it as an infrastructural response to dependence on the dollarized payments circuit and, therefore, as a piece of monetary-technological sovereignty on the technofeudal board. In the didactic example, the author contrasts the friction of “conventional” international transfers with the hypothesis of People's Bank of China wallets, in which the remittance is completed “al instante y sin coste alguno”, precisely because settlement in digital yuan eliminates cascading intermediaries and, above all, bypasses “el canal internacional de transferencia de dinero que está totalmente controlado por las autoridades estadounidenses” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 138). The political consequence of the design is explicit, since, by reducing the need to pass through banks, central banks and the dollarized corridor, the digital yuan appears as “la peor pesadilla de Washington y los banqueros privados” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 139), that is, as a direct threat to the capacity for control exercised by the US over flows and sanctions within the payments system. ↩
  70. In projecting the “new cold war” as a dispute between cloud infrastructures, Varoufakis suggests that it reduces third parties to a geopolitics of dependence, in which autonomy becomes a function of access to cloud capital and to the circuits that sustain it. In the European case, vulnerability does not stem only from diplomatic decisions, but from a material lack of its own capacity in the new regime, so that “La falta de capital en la nube de Europa significa que la nueva guerra fría, sumada a la crisis energética provocada por la guerra en Ucrania que afectó a sus fabricantes, ya han hecho que Europa sea geoestratégicamente irrelevante” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 143). For the Global South, the bifurcation appears as a forced choice between competing digital domains, because “Con el mundo dividido en dos superfeudos en la nube, uno basado en el dólar y el otro en el yuan, se ven obligados a elegir un señor feudal al que someterse” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 143), and this pressure intensifies when the old arrangement of financial circulation closes, since “La nueva crisis de deuda está obligando a las clases dirigentes del sur global a elegir un bando” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 144). The structural consequence of the argument is that US-China rivalry tends to translate, for Europeans and peripheral countries, into compulsory alignment with standards, payment channels and data ecosystems they do not control, with increased vulnerability to blockades, access conditions and tolls imposed by the respective “superfiefs”. ↩

Varoufakis makes explicit that to “exit” technofeudalism is not equivalent to adjusting incentives or correcting regulatory excesses, because the core of the problem lies in the property relations that structure social life itself as mediated by the cloud. In linking his critique to the “death of the liberal individual”, he maintains that recovering freedom as individual sovereignty requires a change of architecture, since “rescatar esa idea liberal fundacional —la libertad como soberanía individual— exigirá, por lo tanto, una reconfiguración completa de los derechos de propiedad sobre los instrumentos de producción, distribución, colaboración y comunicación, que cada vez se basan más en la nube” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 153). The consequence is avowedly disruptive, since, to reconstitute the liberal subject under technofeudal conditions, “tenemos que hacer algo que los liberales detestan: planear una nueva revolución” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 153). ↩

Varoufakis describes “democratized companies” as an arrangement in which shareholding ceases to be a tradable asset and becomes inalienable political membership, because the corporate bond merges with the employment bond. The legal form is purposely simple and egalitarian: “cada empleado tiene una única acción que recibe al ser contratado”, a share that “no se puede vender ni alquilar” and that “otorga a cada empleado un único voto” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 165). With this, decision-making power ceases to be a prerogative of share capital and is redistributed as everyday governance, since “todas las decisiones […] se adoptan de manera colectiva” and the vote is exercised through the intranet, conceived as “una asamblea permanente de accionistas” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 165). The intended institutional effect is to cut the typical mediation of finance and speculation, because the rule of “sólo una” non-tradable share per employee both dissolves the figure of the external shareholder and reconfigures the company as a space of deliberation and not as an object of purchase and sale Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 165). ↩

The alternative appears as a “plataforma de micropagos”, which he calls “Un céntimo por tus pensamientos”, combined with a “Carta de Derechos Digitales” that “garantiza a todo el mundo el derecho a elegir qué datos vender y a quién”, so that “los desarrolladores de aplicaciones que necesitan nuestros datos tienen que pagar para obtener los de los usuarios que den su consentimiento” Varoufakis(Varoufakis, 2024, p. 168).
