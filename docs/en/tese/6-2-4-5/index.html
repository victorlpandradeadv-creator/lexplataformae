6.2.4.5 On misleading advertising by semiotic architecture

See this section in the PDF

p. 623

Advertising, in IOC ecosystems, cannot be treated as a peripheral layer of the offer. It participates directly in the production of the economic meaning of the object, organizes the p. 624 consumer's expectation before enjoyment and anticipates, to a large extent, the way in which utility, value, rarity, performance and equivalence will be perceived. It is precisely for this reason that consumer-law protection needs to treat it as a controllable semiotic mechanism. The problem lies not only in literally false sentences or in gross omissions. It lies in the strategic management of the visible and the invisible, of what gains emphasis and what is relegated to the documentary background, of what appears as an immediate advantage and what recedes as a cost, limitation or ordinary risk of the supply.

In platform markets, this question gains special density because the promised experience is not static. The advertised object will later be subjected to updating, curation, parameterization, enforcement and ordinary reconfigurations of the environment. This means that the expectation is not only formed before contracting; it can also be strategically overproduced before the consumer has any real chance to perceive mutability, limits of use, equivalence criteria and ordinary possibilities of functional downgrading. In IOCs, advertising does not announce only an object. It announces a future governed experience. And, for that very reason, it can produce error not only through express falsity, but through the semiotic construction of a trust incompatible with the reality of the regime of enjoyment.

The first point is, then, to recognize advertising as market semiotics. It does not operate only by transferring objective data, but through signs, framings, visual hierarchies and inductions of reading. The relevant vulnerability here is not a lack of technical information in the narrow sense, nor a diffuse psychic weakness of the consumer. What matters is the asymmetry of meaning produced by communication designed to guide adhesion under conditions of selective visibility. The marketing message defines what appears as an advantage, what is naturalized as tolerable, what is anticipated as rarity and what is concealed as an ordinary consequence of the contracting. We are not facing promotional ornament. We are facing a structure for the production of expectation.

This conclusion is perfectly compatible with the dogmatics of the CDC. When advertising contains sufficiently precise elements about qualities, conditions, advantages or characteristics of the object, it becomes part of the offer and radiates obligational effects over the consumer relationship713. The consumer-law system breaks, therefore, with the old privatist view according to p. 625 which advertising would be a simple invitation indifferent to the contracting. In more direct language: the promotional message enters the contract through the door of trust. If the supplier organizes the market through promises, signals and sufficiently guiding descriptions, it cannot later pretend that all of that was only aesthetic ambiance and that the real bond was hidden, dry and hostile, in later clauses. Advertising participates in the very shaping of the legally enforceable performance.

Here, therefore, objective good faith does not appear as an agreeable abstraction, but as a concrete matrix of ancillary duties in marketing communication. Fair advertising is not merely advertising “without lies”. It is advertising structured in a way compatible with legitimate trust, identification of its persuasive character and consistency between what produces expectation and what will actually be delivered. Good faith requires that the supplier promise only what it can fulfill and, if it provides information about characteristics of the product, the service or the conditions of contracting, that this information be correct, truthful and apt to guide the formation of will714. This matters a great deal in IOCs because expectation is usually formed before the consumer has any real contact with the ordinary regime of mutability of the object. The promise circulates first. The frustration comes later.

From this follows the third point: advertising deceptiveness does not depend on a literal lie. The CDC does not require frontal, caricatural or gross falsity to recognize deception. Unlawfulness can arise when the message, even if it contains literally true elements, is apt to lead the consumer into error through ambiguity, relevant omission, strategic incompleteness or p. 626 simple persuasive organization of the visible715. The error need not be fabricated by the openly false sentence. It can be produced by the way the essential is hierarchized, by selective emphasis on advantages and by the displacement of relevant conditions to disclaimers, layers of low visibility, later links or discursive zones that do not usefully integrate the moment of decision.

This is the core of misleading advertising by semiotic architecture. The architecture of an advertisement becomes legally relevant when the supplier structures the consumer's understanding so as to reinforce the positive side of the experience and push the onerous, mutable or limited side out of focus. Put more simply, communication need not lie in order to deceive. It is enough that it manages the interpretive framing in a sufficiently asymmetrical way. Advertising can tell the truth in pieces and still lie as a whole. It can keep the real cost out of the visual center. It can suggest a functional equivalence that the ordinary regime of the IOC later downgrades without verifiable correspondence. It can speak of rarity, urgency, exclusivity or status without adequately integrating, on the same plane of emphasis, the precariousness, reversibility or mutability of what is being acquired.

This mode of operation intensifies when the platform organizes pre-deliveries of expectation. Teasers, storefronts, trailers, event campaigns, passes, rarity panels, countdown timers, suggested equivalences, narratives of distinction and communication through influencers are not merely advance publicity. They are mechanisms for curating expectation before actual enjoyment. In IOCs, this matters enormously because the governance of the decision begins before the purchase and before acceptance. The platform builds in advance what the consumer expects to find, to value, to fear losing and to tolerate as admissible frustration. The marketing environment thus already functions as an architecture of future tolerable disappointment. If expectation is inflated by a semiotics of value and later absorbed p. 627 by an ordinary regime of downgrading, elastic equivalence or unilateral mutability, advertising was not merely a pre-contractual stage. It was a mechanism for capturing trust.

In this context, the case of hybrid formats is especially important. In digital ecosystems, advertising, content, entertainment, recommendation and sale frequently blur into one another. Influencers, creators, “community” posts, demonstrations, in-game storefronts and events mediated by the platform's own environment blur the distinction between descriptive communication, cultural recommendation and marketing persuasion. This hybridization weakens the intelligibility of the message's advertising character and compromises communicational fairness. The consumer need not be facing a banner explicitly labeled as an advertisement to be persuasively steered toward a purchase. When the marketing character becomes harder to identify, the semiotic asymmetry deepens, because advertising is no longer perceived as advertising and comes to operate disguised as experience, recommendation or mere ecosystem content.

It is precisely for this reason that Article 38 of the CDC proves so important here. The burden of proving the truthfulness and correctness of advertising information or communication lies with whoever sponsors it716. This is no elegant evidentiary detail. It is a normative recognition that advertising is a space of power and, therefore, of responsibility. In IOCs, this provision spares the consumer from having to prove alone that the expectation produced by the communication was incompatible with the actual regime of enjoyment. If the platform advertises a certain equivalence, benefit, utility, standard of stability or implicit value of rarity, it is for the platform to demonstrate with documents that this narrative is correct in light of what is ordinarily delivered and administered in the ecosystem. The system does not presume the innocence of the marketing promise. It requires that whoever produced it back it up.

This logic is closely bound up with the protection of trust. Legally protected trust arises not only from the final contractual instrument, but also from the supplier's communicational conduct in the market717. The more precise, eloquent p. 628 and directive the advertising, the greater its capacity to generate legitimate expectation and, therefore, the greater its binding force. This is decisive because it blocks the old maneuver of capturing trust in the pre-contractual phase and then trying to drain it away in the final text of the contract or in mutable internal rules. Advertising does not stay outside, since it takes part in the legally relevant core of the consumer relationship. If it organizes expectation, it also organizes the yardstick by which frustration will be measured.

The rules on advertising to children function here as a normative limit case that reinforces this reading from within, without shifting the axis of the discussion. The point is not to equate adults with children, nor to open a new thematic front. The point is to show that the CDC itself already recognizes that abusiveness may arise from taking advantage of a structural deficiency of judgment exploited by persuasive communication. When the system deems abusive the advertising that takes advantage of a child's deficiency of judgment and experience, it explicitly recognizes that the manipulation of meaning and the exploitation of limits on decoding are legally relevant. The most important normative datum is not childhood itself, but the underlying logic. When communication takes advantage of a structural asymmetry of understanding, the control of abusiveness intensifies. This reinforces, from within the system itself, the legitimacy of treating semiotic vulnerability as a legal fact and not as a sociological metaphor.

The argumentative value of this limit case is quite elegant. It shows that the CDC already operates with gradations of vulnerability tied to real conditions of understanding and resistance, and not only to an occasional lack of data. Advertising can be abusive even without lying, simply because it exploits a structural limitation of judgment. Transposing this reasoning to IOCs in a controlled way, without infantilizing the adult consumer, one may argue that there are also situations in which architecture, curation and private governance of meaning produce real asymmetries of decoding, comparison and proof. When advertising and the platform's communicational environment systematically exploit these asymmetries, room opens for heightened control of deceptiveness and abusiveness.

The immediate dogmatic consequence is that advertising in IOCs must be treated as a controllable practice when the essential is kept out of focus and the negative is pushed into p. 629 disclaimers or useless cross-references; when marketing communication, through anticipatory curation of expectation, produces an image of value, rarity, performance or functional equivalence that the ordinary regime of mutability later frustrates without verifiable correspondence; and when the marketing character of the message is weakened by hybrid formats that conflate content, recommendation, entertainment and sale. In these cases, deceptiveness is not reduced to a literal lie. It appears as a defect in the very architecture of the promise.

It is therefore necessary to connect semiotic vulnerability, good faith, trust, deceptiveness and abusiveness without psychologizing consumption or resorting to moralism against advertising as such. The problem is not desiring, fantasizing or attributing symbolic value to the object. The problem arises when the marketing production of expectation is organized so as to steer the consumer's understanding toward an image of the object that the supply regime itself does not sustain, or sustains only under hidden reservations, opaque equivalences and silent mutability. Advertising thus ceases to be merely an announcement and becomes an objective element in the production of a legally controllable trust.

This prepares the transition to the broader decision environment in which advertising, information, interface and choice architecture operate in an integrated way. Advertising will not be the only gear of semiotic vulnerability, but it is one of its most powerful cores. It pre-shapes expectation, organizes the imaginary of value, distributes visibility and conditions what the consumer understands as risk, opportunity and loss. In IOCs, where enjoyment is governed and mutable, this makes it one of the CDC's main points of control. The supplier answers not only for what it promises in isolated sentences. It answers for the regime of meaning it produces when it advertises.

Notes

  1. Under the Consumer Protection Code, advertising does not occupy a merely peripheral or ornamental position. When the advertising message contains sufficiently precise elements about the object, its qualities, conditions or advantages, it becomes part of the offer and radiates obligational effects over the consumer relationship, binding the supplier to the content conveyed Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, p. 140-142). The consequence is direct: advertising does not remain outside the contract as a mere prior persuasive stage, but takes part in shaping the legally enforceable content of the performance. This logic is reinforced by the abandonment of the old private-law understanding that treated advertising as a harmless invitation to contract. In the consumer-law system, the marketing message is no longer legally indifferent and comes to function as a source of obligation whenever, by its degree of precision and capacity to form expectation, it makes up the content of the offer addressed to the consumer Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, p. 144). If advertising is part of the offer and creates binding obligations, then the way it organizes the consumer's understanding, ranks elements, distributes visibility and produces expectation also becomes legally relevant. The advertising message does not stand outside the relationship doing marketing; it takes part in the very obligational structure of the supply. ↩
  2. Under the CDC regime, marketing communication is subject to substantive control of fairness, truthfulness and adequacy because it can shape the consumer's legitimate expectation even before the bond is formalized. Marques states that, in matters of advertising, objective good faith requires it to be “a fair activity”, also considered from the standpoint of whoever receives the message, so that it promises only what it can deliver and, if it provides information about characteristics of the product, the service or the contractual conditions, that information is correct and truthful Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 832; p. 886). The author adds that the main characteristic of misleading advertising, under the CDC, is that it is liable to lead the consumer into error, including by “omissions”, and that the rule must be interpreted broadly, since the error consists in a “false notion of reality” potentially formed in the consumer's mind by the advertising Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 909). Applied to the problem of semiotic architecture, this guideline authorizes control of promotional messages that, without lying outright, organize the interpretive field of the offer so as to produce an illusory understanding of utility, extent, stability or conditions of enjoyment. ↩
  3. Advertising deceptiveness, under the CDC, does not depend on literal falsity or on an outright, demonstrable lie. Unlawfulness may arise whenever the message, even if partly true, is capable of leading the consumer into error, including through omission, ambiguity, relevant incompleteness or the very form in which the content is presented Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, p. 167). The decisive point is not the microscopic truthfulness of each isolated sentence, but the deceptive potential of the communication as a meaningful whole, as actually perceived by its recipient. This perception becomes especially important when the essential datum is pushed into zones of low visibility, diluted in accessory information or weakened by graphic and drafting strategies that reduce how well it is grasped in practice. Deceptiveness may emerge, for example, when the real price does not appear clearly, when charges are relegated to a visually insignificant position or when the decisive information is technically included but functionally hidden in the context of the piece Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, p. 168). Misleading advertising by semiotic architecture does not require gross falsity. It can operate through the strategic management of the visible and the invisible, through the functional ambiguity of language and through the persuasive organization of the advertisement, so as to steer the consumer's understanding away from what really matters. ↩
  4. “Art. 38. The burden of proving the truthfulness and correctness of the advertising information or communication lies with whoever sponsors it.” Brasil(Brasil, [2022]).. ↩
  5. In consumer law, advertising takes a direct part in the formation of contractual trust. This is because, in markets marked by lean, opaque or incomplete adhesion contracts, the marketing message often anticipates, completes and thickens the understanding the consumer forms of the utility, qualities and mode of enjoyment of the object offered, and becomes a relevant element in building their legitimate expectation Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, p. 142). Legally protected trust therefore arises not only from the final contractual instrument, but also from the supplier's communicational conduct in the market. This binding effect can be understood in light of the protection of the expectation created by the advertiser's own conduct. The more eloquent, precise and directive the message, the greater its capacity to generate trust worthy of protection and, therefore, the greater its binding force Pasqualotto(Pasqualotto, 1997, p. 42). Advertising does not merely describe the object; it pre-shapes the expectation of enjoyment that will later serve as the yardstick for assessing performance, inadequacy and deceptiveness. When marketing communication structures the consumer's expectation, it enters the legally relevant core of the consumer relationship and may be a source both of obligation and of legally censurable frustration. ↩
