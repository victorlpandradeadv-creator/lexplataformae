7.1.1.3 On the parameters of the duty to inform as verifiable adequacy

See this section in the PDF

p. 722

In platform ecosystems, the duty to inform cannot be understood in merely quantitative terms. The mere accumulation of texts, links, notices, policies, terms and explanatory windows does not, by itself, satisfy the requirement of adequate information. In many cases, this accumulation works precisely in the opposite direction, producing an environment of merely apparent transparency, in which the proliferation of statements takes the place of the legibility of what actually matters for deciding, adhering, remaining or contesting. Informational inadequacy, in this context, does not necessarily stem from the material absence of information. It may result from the way information is organized, fragmented, hierarchized, distributed over time and made accessible under conditions that prevent its practical usefulness.

Compliance with the duty to inform cannot be reduced to a bureaucratic logic of textual deposit. To inform is not merely to make content available. To inform, in a legally relevant sense, is to make intelligible the essential elements of enjoyment at the moment the consumer's decision is formed and, at the same time, to preserve minimal conditions for the later reconstruction of what was promised, what was shown and what was altered. Adequate information, therefore, is not measured by the volume of what was published, but by its aptitude to guide conduct and allow institutional verification. Where excess replaces clarity, noise replaces comprehension and apparent abundance conceals the essential, the duty remains violated.

The transformation of this duty on platforms is particularly important because the environment of continuous consumption tends to operate through multiple communicational layers. The consumer does not encounter only a centralized and stable contractual text. He faces successive interface flows, scattered notices, secondary menus, help centers, versioned policies, partial explanations, promotional communications, contextual notifications and later alterations that redraw the informational field without offering a material synthesis of what changed. In this architecture, opacity may be produced not by outright omission, but by the strategic distribution of information along discontinuous trails of low salience or difficult reconstruction. What presents itself as communicational diligence may, in truth, function as a technique of obscuring.

Informational inadequacy may thus take the form of excess, dispersion and flow architecture. Excess, when textual overload makes it impracticable to distinguish the essential from the accessory. Dispersion, when the relevant elements of the relationship are scattered across multiple documents, screens and paths without sufficient integration. Flow architecture, p. 723 when the informational experience is designed to enhance the attractive, simplify the favorable and push the onerous, the contingent or the restrictive into zones of low visibility, lesser salience or improbable reading. In these cases, the violation of the duty cannot be adequately described as a simple lack of information. It is functional opacity produced by design, that is, a specific form of informational non-performance in which the appearance of openness replaces the actual legibility of the regime of enjoyment.

This parameter is particularly useful because it shifts the discussion from the abstract existence of information to its usefulness for decision and its institutional verifiability. The legally relevant question is no longer whether a given piece of data was somewhere in the supplier's documentary ecosystem. It becomes whether that data was made accessible, comprehensible and relevant at the moment it could influence the legitimate formation of expectation or the consumer's reaction. Likewise, in case of later controversy, what matters is whether the informational arrangement preserves minimal trails that make it possible to reconstruct the link between offer, presentation of the environment, modulation of enjoyment and alteration of the content of the relationship. Without this possibility of reconstruction, information fails not only to guide choice, but also to allow institutional control of the conflict itself.

The centrality of verifiable adequacy also prevents the supplier's standard defense from resting on the claim that “everything was in the contract”. This formula confuses formal existence with material compliance with the duty. In environments of high informational complexity, the contract may even contain a nominal reference to the disputed risk, alteration or limitation, and still fail to inform adequately if the organization of the communicational whole produces noise, minimizes the impact of the relevant data or makes their practical connection with the consumer's decision unfeasible. The duty to inform does not protect only against direct factual lies. It also protects against the strategic displacement of what is legally relevant to regions of low intelligibility.

This formulation carries an important consequence for immaterial objects of consumption of high cognitive complexity, including those in which the very utility of the object involves guidance, filtering, prioritization or informational support. In these contexts, breach of the duty may occur without any literal falsehood and without any absolute silence. It suffices that the communicational architecture replace comprehension with an impression of completeness, or that textual delivery function as a misleading substitute for legibility. The problem then ceases to be the presence or absence of isolated data and comes to lie in the relation between promise, mode of presentation, real conditions of use and the institutional possibility of auditing the informational trajectory of the relationship.

p. 724 The parameter, therefore, demands a material reading of the duty to inform. Informational adequacy must be measured by the capacity of the communicational arrangement to make visible and comprehensible the elements that relevantly condition the formation of expectation, the preservation of enjoyment and the possibility of contestation. Where there is excess without guidance, abundance without hierarchy, documentation without legibility or transparency without reconstructability, there will be no true performance of the duty. There will be, rather, a sophisticated form of functional opacity, incompatible with the consumer-law discipline of an environment in which enjoyment depends on continuous governance and in which private power is also exercised through the administration of what can be known, perceived and proven.
