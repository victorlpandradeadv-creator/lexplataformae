5.2.3 On the Legal Framework for Electronic Games and preliminary framing

See this section in the PDF

  • Dogmatics · Sections of chapter 5

p. 493

The normative and institutional framing of the electronic games sector, at this point, has a strict and delimited function. The criteria-oriented typology has already stabilized the lenses through which platform-governed enjoyment can be observed and compared. It remains to situate, on the Brazilian plane, how the sector has been recognized and on which focal points it tends to be problematized, so that the study advances on institutionally intelligible ground and so that recurring tensions do not seem idiosyncratic contingencies of an isolated case.

The objective is not to compose a legislative inventory nor to reconstruct the regulatory debate in detail. What matters is to record the minimum context in which games operated as a service come to concentrate public and legal attention, highlighting vectors that reappear when enjoyment depends on an environment of continuous operation with intense private governance. Against this background, a double movement is observed. On one side, there is an effort to institutionalize games as a relevant economic and cultural activity. On the other, there is a recurrence of concerns linked to the protection of vulnerable publics, to monetization through microtransactions and random mechanisms, to the processing of personal data and to market and advertising practices integrated into the very experience of enjoyment.

The institutionalization of the sector matters because it reinforces, documentarily, the reading that games (above all under models operated as a service) are not limited to isolated products delivered in a single event. The offer is realized as an ecosystem sustained by continuous operation, permanent updating and enforcement of internal rules that integrate the material content of supply. A sufficient landmark to record this fact is Law 14,852/2024 Brasil(Brasil, [2024]b), taken here as a reference of institutional recognition of the electronic games industry as an economic and cultural activity with its own relevance. The central point, for purposes of framing, is the presupposition that the sectoral statute expresses in the sense that there is, operating in Brazil, a sector consolidated as a legitimate object of state attention and of public policy structuring, with language typical of promotion, development and organization of a production chain. This dispels any impression of exceptionality of the locus studied and brings it closer to other mass digital markets in which enjoyment occurs over time under variable rules.

Recognition, however, does not eliminate legal tensions, it only tends to make them more predictable. In ecosystems of continued enjoyment, expectations are constituted over time, under criteria susceptible to unilateral modulation, and relevant losses may occur without formal interruption of access, whether through gradual degradation of utility, or through p. 494 disorganization of symbolic value incorporated into what is acquired. For this reason, institutional attention is organized around vectors of friction that stem from the structure of the ecosystem, and not from isolated episodes.

The protection of children and adolescents is one of these recurring vectors. Its centrality does not derive only from concerns about content, but from the combination of interactivity, permanence of the bond and exposure to mechanisms of acquisition and permanence incorporated into the interfaces and rules of the environment. In games operated as a service, consumption decisions tend to be fragmented over time, mediated by cycles of return, signs of urgency, promises of progression, social comparison and incentives embedded in the very design of enjoyment. The cut by minors functions, thus, as an institutional trigger to make visible asymmetries of understanding and of capacity for contestation in environments governed by architecture.

In this framework, the public policy of advisory age rating plays a useful contextual role. Without functioning as prior authorization, it acts as a guiding instrument aimed at informing age suitability and relevant descriptors, and, in reaching digital products distributed by platforms, it makes explicit an important institutional premise: the experience of enjoyment is not exhausted in audiovisual “content”, since it includes conditions of interaction and circulation that can produce relevant exposures and asymmetries, above all for children and adolescents. The interest of this fact, here, is not the detail of the instrument, but what it signals about the sector. In a significant portion of cases, the risk lies in the mode of functioning of the very environment of enjoyment.

Another predictable focal point is monetization through microtransactions. In games as a service, acquisition tends to occur continuously and in segments, inserted into the very experience of enjoyment. It is not merely a payment modality. Monetization is integrated into the architecture of the environment and influences the rhythm of use, progression, access to conveniences and the very perception of value of what is acquired. The legal conflict thus shifts from the price of an isolated product to the governance of a system of offers and incentives that renews itself over time, under variable rules and with structurally accentuated informational asymmetries.

The point of greatest sensitivity appears when monetization relies on random mechanisms for obtaining items, frequently treated in public debate as loot boxes. In these cases, the utility and value of what is acquired come to depend on internal distribution regimes, on availability criteria and on limits defined by the platform, besides being articulated with signs of scarcity, time windows and rarity regimes that make decisions more dependent on anticipation, urgency and expectation. The institutional discussion p. 495 tends, then, to concentrate on minimum transparency of the conditions of acquisition, protection of vulnerable publics and control of practices of inducing consumption, not out of moralization of the sector, but because the supplier concentrates internal criteria on availability, rotation, rarity and, where applicable, probability, under conditions of low external reconstructibility.

In this context, Law No. 15,211/2025 Brasil(Brasil, [2025]c) offers sufficient normative anchoring to situate the focal point by regulating, from the standpoint of the protection of children and adolescents, the loot box functionality as a mechanism of random obtaining upon payment. The interest of the statute, here, is evidential. Certain mechanics of the ecosystem have already been identified as structurally sensitive and, for this reason, amenable to treatment by criteria. Under the IOC regime, such mechanisms condense elements such as instituted rarity, anticipation, criterial opacity and unilateral modulation, with effects that may manifest themselves as material erosion of utility and reconfiguration of value, even if access remains formally available.

There is also an institutional vector linked to the fact that games operated as a service function as informational platforms. Enjoyment is realized through an identified account, inventories, usage records, performance metrics, purchase history, interaction patterns and reputational mechanisms that depend on continuous collection and processing of data. These data are not merely administrative traces. They sustain the personalization of the experience, the offer of items, rankings, validations, fraud detection, moderation and the enforcement of internal rules. The relevant contextual consequence is the intensification of structural asymmetries of information. The supplier concentrates records and parameters decisive for reconstructing changes and governance decisions, while the consumer has only partial visibility, mediated by interfaces.

In this scenario, the Brazilian personal data protection regime operates as a normative backdrop, reinforcing the institutional sensitivity of the sector in environments of continued enjoyment, especially when children and adolescents take part. The point, here, is not to turn the framing into a compliance audit, but to record that the environment of enjoyment is also an informational environment and that, for this reason, the minimum reconstruction of what changed, to what extent it changed and with what effects on utility and value becomes a relevant component for any discussion of controllability, without requiring full technical transparency or the disclosure of business secrets.

Besides minors, monetization and data, the incidence of concerns associated with market and advertising practices integrated into the experience of enjoyment is predictable. In models operated as a service, the consumer relationship is not exhausted at the initial entry. The offer p. 496 renews itself through events, seasons, promotions, bundles and acquisition stimuli incorporated into use, so that advertising also operates as internal communication designed through storefronts, urgency messages, recommendations and highlighting mechanisms. This integration reinforces the continuous and mass character of supply and tends to activate classic consumer protection instruments aimed at adequate information, transparency and control of abusive practices.

The Consumer Protection Code, in this frame, functions as a general grammar of protection in mass relationships marked by informational asymmetry and concentration of the power to organize the offer, without this anticipating, at this point, the specific dogmatic reconstruction of the IOC regime. What is relevant, for the minimum framing, is to recognize that ecosystems of this type coexist with repeated patterns of complaints and high-scale conflicts, in which conditions of acquisition, offer criteria and effects of internal changes to the environment become central.

Finally, intellectual property appears as a latent friction because enjoyment is mediated by protected assets and by licensing regimes that delimit admissible uses, forms of access and possibilities of circulation of what is acquired. In platform-governed ecosystems, terms of use, moderation policies, transfer limits and revocation conditions are usually formulated under a logic of intangible asset management, which can intensify asymmetries of agency and reduce alternatives for reconstituting what is acquired outside the ecosystem. The usefulness of this record, here, is merely situational: consumer conflicts in games as a service frequently cross the interface between consumer protection and control prerogatives derived from licenses and intellectual property, leaving the analysis of the consequences for the proper stage of legal reconstruction.

The minimum institutional framework outlined makes it possible to understand why the electronic games sector, especially under models operated as a service, concentrates recurring legal tensions. The recognition of the sector as a relevant economic and cultural activity coexists with predictable focal points linked to the protection of children and adolescents, to monetization through microtransactions and random mechanisms, to the processing of personal data and to market and advertising practices integrated into the environment of enjoyment. These vectors do not appear as episodic accidents, but as typical effects of ecosystems in which utility is realized over time, under private governance of the environment and through rules susceptible to unilateral modulation, with structural asymmetries of intelligibility and agency.
