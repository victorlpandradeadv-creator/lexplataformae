2.6 On epigraphs as a symptomatic record and a hermeneutic resource

See this section in the PDF

This section in the layers of the site

  • Method · The protocol
  • Epigraphs · The nine epigraphs

p. 73

The reading of the legal phenomenon of platforms refuses the artificial separation between norm, technique and culture. In the platform economy, governance is not exhausted in the written rule. It is also exercised through choice architecture, interface design, brand language, curation of affects and continuous symbolic production. Updates, events, campaigns, characters, slogans and performances do not surround the object investigated as mere ambience. They form part of its mode of operation. It is in this context that the curation of epigraphs enters the method, as a way of apprehending, in an artistic and culturally situated register, tensions constitutive of contemporary digital consumption, among them anxiety, desire for belonging, disorientation, euphoria, frustration, capture and performative compulsion.

The epigraphs appear neither as ornament, nor as an authority substituting for the scientific method, nor as empirical proof of the argument. Their function lies elsewhere. They condense, in public and traceable artifacts, the affective and semiotic structure surrounding the consumer relationship in digital environments. When drawn from the very business ecosystem taken as an illustrative reference, they do not count as an institutional confession or endorsement p. 74 by the supplier, but as a public inscription of the symbolic universe in which enjoyment, trust and permanence come to be administered through language, aesthetics and performance.

The epigraph opens the section, sets its atmosphere and circumscribes the environment of experience in which the legal problem will be addressed. The analytical work of the section comes afterwards. The argument is not founded on the excerpt, nor does it draw its chain of validation from it. What is preserved, with its presence, is the perception that the phenomenon investigated does not fit entirely into the technical-normative language with which Law usually describes it. In a thesis that deals with platforms as structures for steering conduct, retaining relationships and administering meanings, ignoring the aesthetic thickness of the object in the name of a supposed rigor would only impoverish it.

Nor does the epigraphic curation import into the text the full content of the songs, nor does it presume total coincidence between the artistic work and the problem investigated. The procedure is selective. It cuts specific excerpts, repositions passages, makes deliberate omissions and, when necessary, adjusts punctuation to preserve the meaning of the excerpt in the new context, at times incorporating emphases present in the vocal performance and not in the usual transcription. It is not a matter of summarizing the work, but of assembling, with methodological discipline, an expressive core relevant to the reading of the section.

Art, in different traditions, has served as a public device for guiding reading, preparing the gaze and conducting memory. In archaic Greek literature, the invocation of the Muses, as in Hesiod24, organizes the account, delimits its tone and situates the voice that speaks. The presence of nine epigraphs emulates the nine Muses and reinforces the framing character of the resource. Each section is opened by a culturally situated lens that precedes the argumentative development. In a dramatic key, the function also approaches that of the chorus, not because the text wishes to theatricalize its object, but because it recognizes that certain conflicts require, beyond conceptual formulation, a voice that keeps their moral and affective atmosphere in circulation.

p. 75 The resource also approaches, within a disciplined scope, the lineages gathered in the field of Law and Humanities. In “Law and Literature”, “Law and Film” and “Law and Art”, the cultural work does not replace legal analysis, but expands its capacity for reading25 by placing Law again before experiences that formalism tends to reduce26. This approach matters here for a precise reason: digital consumption regulated by platforms is not merely an obligational relationship mediated by technology, since it is also a regime of perception, belonging, promise, reward, threat, visibility and opacity. Legal language, when it insists on describing it only as contract, performance or functionality, runs the risk of getting its very object wrong.

There is also a function of denaturalization that is decisive for this thesis. By bringing legal discourse closer to the symbolic universe actually inhabited by the consumer, mechanisms of power that formalism tends to leave out of the frame become more visible. A relevant part of the regulation exercised by platforms is not carried out through explicit normative statements, but through signals, stimuli, settings, metrics, rewards, frictions and interaction scripts. This is private power exercised also on the semiotic plane, in which narrative, aesthetics and design take part in shaping vulnerability and, in certain cases, the very materiality of the unlawful act. Cultural curation helps expose the concrete texture of what Law must learn to name in order to be able to control it.

p. 76 As regards the historical-cultural delimitation of the repertoire mobilized, it is worth noting that the meaning attributed by the songwriter herself to The Fear27, the general epigraph of the work, originated as a diagnosis of an inaugural moment of digital life, the one in which a “performed self” begins to be demanded and in which the liberating promise of the networks begins to reveal, almost immediately, its face of continuous exposure, falsity and capture. The most expressive fact lies precisely in the gap between the moment of composition and the later maturation of the technical ecosystem she intuited. What had emerged in a satirical key became literal. The song already recorded, in a still embryonic state, the public fabrication of versions of oneself, the anxiety for validation and the diffuse feeling of submission to a regime of appearances that would later merely consolidate.

It is along this itinerary that The Love Club, the second general epigraph and the opening of the introductory section, introduces the problem of the attraction exercised by digital communities and of the loneliness produced by closed belongings, in which entry into an ecosystem exacts, little by little, a withdrawal from external reality. The following section is received by Birds of Prey, which states the predatory face of the contemporary economic system by portraying an environment in which surveillance and extraction convert enjoyment into hunting ground. In the fourth section, Oh No! condenses the semiotic and psychopolitical capture of the achievement-subject, imprisoned between the obsession with metrified success, the mechanization of conduct and the paradoxical submission to one's own digital assets and possessions. The fifth section closes this block through a dialogue between Scarlet Envy's performance and the lyrics of Villain. The joint composition no longer merely insinuates, but personifies governance by architecture in its most assertive form, by portraying a regime in which unilateral control and manipulation of affects appear as the constitutive logic of its functioning.

In the case of Villain, an additional element of special methodological relevance stands out. Produced by Riot Games itself as part of the artistic universe of the ecosystem taken here as an illustrative reference, the composition describes, in the first person and in the language of entertainment, a power structure founded on unilateral command, affective instrumentalization, disposal of the other and deliberate absence of empathy. The ecosystem, in narrating itself culturally, projects an intelligible image of its own regime of power, and it is this p. 77 self-descriptive gesture, not a legal confession, that matters to the argument. A Lex Plataformae does not govern only by contract, code and interface. It also governs by plot, style, character and performance. When the business universe converts into a consumable cultural product a narrative compatible with the type of rationality the thesis seeks to qualify critically, this convergence forms part of the intelligibility of the phenomenon.

The alternation between English-language and Brazilian compositions is not contingent either. The English-language block records the global grammar of digital consumption in its promises of belonging, in its incentives to self-exploitation, in its normalization of capture and in its diffuse regime of opacity. The Brazilian block, in turn, signals the return to the institutional ground of national Law and the passage from the experience of the problem to the formulation of the response. The transition between languages is not a lateral aesthetic detail. It stages the central passage of the thesis itself. The problem emerges on a global scale, within private platform architectures. Legal containment, however, is built with national instruments, under the constitutional order and the Consumer Protection Code.

For this reason, opening the methodological section, Coisas Que Eu Sei marks the passage from the affective and cultural universe of the problem to the legal horizon of intelligibility and control. From there on, the Brazilian epigraphs accompany the progressive formulation of the dogmatic response. In the sixth section, Diz Nos Meus Olhos (Inclemência) expresses the violence of the expropriation of time, existential investment and subjective continuity accumulated under administered relationships.

Next, for the seventh section, Infinito Particular stages the confrontation with the technical black box and, by rejecting illegibility as destiny, mirrors the demand for open auditability. Temporal, in turn, closes the path by translating, for the eighth and last development section, the disorientation of the subject facing the loss of self-recognition under conditions of semiotic vulnerability and identity captivity. In the plea for a “fermata that can now make time obey me”, the concluding image of the epigraphic method emerges: the demand for a legal regime capable of interrupting technical arbitrariness and of subjecting the temporality of supply to criteria of stability, integrity and controllability compatible with the constitutional economic order and with the CDC.

Seen together, the epigraphs thus build a narrative arc parallel to the argumentative arc of the thesis. Each chapter opening condenses, in artistic language, the specific movement that the dogmatic development will carry out in legal language. There is direction, structure and progression. The sequence of the nine epigraphs travels, in a symbolic key, p. 78 the same passage that the thesis travels in a dogmatic key: from the finding of the problem to its legal containment, from capture to institutional response, from fear to the fermata.

There is, further, an effect that deserves precise naming. In a thesis whose central category is semiotic vulnerability, the recourse to epigraphs not only accompanies the argument but proposes to confirm it performatively, since it demonstrates, through the very medium of signs, that the symbolic plane is not peripheral, but constitutive of the phenomenon investigated. Contemporary popular culture captured the experience of digital capture before dogmatics had a vocabulary fine enough to name it. Lily Allen recorded the fear of being programmed to consume. MARINA staged self-exploitation as identity. Pitty formulated, under the image of the fermata, the desire to interrupt a temporality that has ceased to obey the subject.

That these signs are themselves products of the culture industry, and not the spontaneous expression of those who consume them, does not weaken the argument, but confirms it: it is precisely when the symbolic commodity becomes the language in which the subject feels, desires and suffers that semiotic capture becomes observable. Such records reveal that the social experience of capture already circulates as a recognizable form of suffering, estrangement and loss before its legal formulation. A study of governance by signs that refused, even in its framing, the signs actually inhabited by the consumer would not be more rigorous. It would merely be myopic.

For this reason, what the epigraphs accomplish, in the end, is not limited to accompanying the text with cultural repertoire. They sustain, in a second voice, a demonstration decisive for this thesis: that the consumer's experience under platforms is semiotically constituted, and that the relevant injury in these environments cannot be satisfactorily apprehended when Law insists on speaking a language foreign to the one in which consumption actually takes place. The symbolic register precedes the argument, frames it and intensifies it, announcing, with the means proper to art, the passage from symptom to response, in which national Law is called upon to give back, in the form of protection, what the global private regime tends to normalize as loss and to leave without appropriate legal nomenclature.

  1. Hesiod, a Greek poet associated with the archaic period, composes in the Theogonía a poem of genealogical organization of the divine, in which the origins of the cosmos and the succession of powers and lineages culminating in the Olympian order are narrated. The work functions as a narrative map of the world, gathering traditions and stabilizing a public memory about where the gods come from, how authority is structured and why certain hierarchies prevail. It is in this frame that the nine Muses appear, daughters of Zeus and Mnemosyne (Memory), as a figuration of inspiration and, above all, of the capacity to give form to the true account as the transmission of what must be remembered. The invocation of the Muses, in this context, is not devotional ornament, but a protocol of enunciation, marking that the account is mediated by a symbolic source of intelligibility, organizing the discourse and legitimizing the enunciative gesture. As a formal detail, the numerical coincidence between the nine Muses and the nine epigraphs of the work helps make explicit, without any claim to equivalence of status, the function of the epigraphs as narrative frame and key of interpretive orientation. ↩
  2. The mobilization of cultural repertoires as interpretive support is not ornament and comes close to the field of Law and Humanities as described by Maria Paola Mittica (2015, p. 25), Doctor in Sociology and Associate Professor at the Università degli Studi di Urbino, who proposes a structural distinction between Law and Humanities, “Law and Literature” and “Law and Music”, in which the first functions as “genus” and the others as “species”, alongside other cuts such as “Law and Film” and “Law and Art”. In this frame, the dialogue with the humanities does not shift the axis of validation of the argument to the aesthetic plane, since the methodological proposal is precisely to promote a controlled exchange between specialized competences, oriented toward building analytical instruments adequate to the object. Mittica summarizes the question by stating that, in Law and Humanities, “a research methodology is assumed that is intended to promote dialogue between different specialized competences” Mittica(Mittica, 2015, p. 25). Thus, the epigraphic use can be understood as a hermeneutic frame and a symptomatic record of the symbolic field, without needing to enter the evidentiary chain or replace dogmatic description, qualification and critique. ↩
  3. In this sense, André Karam Trindade, Associate Professor in the Graduate Program in Law of the Centro Universitário de Cascavel (UNIVEL), refers to José Calvo González to reinforce that the use of cultural artifacts can operate as an auxiliary instrument for reading the legal phenomenon without becoming empirical proof or citation of authority. Trindade observes that, in the field of the arts, Calvo González wrote on relations between Law and Cinema, Music, Iconography, Photography and other expressions so as to demonstrate that the dialogue between Law and artistic expressions can be methodologically fertile without reducing the legal to the aesthetic (Trindade, 2021, p. 86). Moreover, in characterizing the idea of a Literary Culture of the Law as a two-way street, his text makes explicit that the gain lies not in “proving with art”, but in expanding intelligibility, vocabulary and perception of layers of experience that technical-normative language tends to impoverish. This two-way street is formulated on the basis of two dimensions, the literary reading of law and the legal reading of literature (Trindade, 2021, p. 106). The methodological consequence is compatible with the proposed epigraphic device, since the epigraph functions as a frame and reading support for the symbolic field, while the argument is sustained by dogmatic and institutional analysis, without the cultural repertoire forming part of the grounding as an inferential premise. ↩
  4. In an interview with the British newspaper The Telegraph in 2018, Lily Allen revisited the original meaning of the song and the proportion such phenomena assumed over the following years: “It’s funny, because when I wrote it, it was meant to be quite tongue-in-cheek and sarcastic, but it’s become quite literal now, sadly. I was spending a lot of time on MySpace and I guess just starting to know Twitter and it felt like people were putting across a version of themselves that wasn’t necessarily ‘true’. In its infancy, social media felt liberating. And then it felt, quite immediately, quite false. I think ‘The Fear’ was my reaction to those falsehoods.” (Allen apud Vicent, 2018) ↩
