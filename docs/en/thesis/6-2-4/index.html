6.2.4 On semiotic vulnerability as a contemporary form of factual vulnerability

See this section in the PDF

Concepts in this section

  • Semiotic vulnerability

This section in the layers of the site

  • Dogmatics · Sections of chapter 6

p. 579

To be of dogmatic use, semiotic vulnerability cannot be treated as a loose cultural metaphor or as a judgment about the consumer's psychology. It designates a p. 580 contemporary form of factual vulnerability produced by the way supply is organized in platform environments. In IOCs, the relevant asymmetry does not lie only in the fact that the consumer knows less about the system's internal characteristics. It arises because the platform controls the very instruments that make the relationship intelligible, that is, the signs, interfaces, metrics, framings and records through which the consumer forms expectations, assesses enjoyment, perceives downgrading and tries to mount a challenge. The consumer's vulnerability remains the pillar of the CDC's special protection, and it is precisely for this reason that semiotic vulnerability must be understood as a contemporary specification of this structural datum, and not as a parallel or extravagant category Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 324; p. 307)650.

This point calls for an important distinction. Semiotic vulnerability does not describe the user's lesser individual discernment, psychological naivety or subjective susceptibility. The problem lies in the objective way the environment organizes language, visibility, comparison and proof. On platforms, the consumer depends on governed mediations to understand what they acquired, to perceive whether the experience was altered and to identify whether a legally relevant loss occurred. This means that the platform not only supplies the object, but also administers the reference points through which that object can be perceived and measured. The asymmetry, then, is no longer merely informational in the classical sense and becomes semiotic as well, because the supplier controls the regime through which the meaning of enjoyment itself is produced.

It is precisely this that sustains the category under the CDC. The consumer's vulnerability does not act merely as the system's sociological backdrop. It is normatively linked to good faith, the duty to inform, transparency and the protection of trust. The consumer's autonomy depends on positive duties to inform, to cooperate and to act with fairness and care, because without this minimum standard of conduct the decision to contract is not rational in a p. 581 legally relevant sense Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 812; p. 1480)651. Semiotic vulnerability is anchored precisely in this mechanism. It shows that, in governed environments, the duty to inform cannot be reduced to a bureaucratic dumping of data or to rituals of acceptance. What is at stake is the creation of minimum conditions for actually understanding enjoyment and its relevant alterations.

For this reason, informational inadequacy is not limited to simple omission. The supplier may appear transparent and still produce material opacity through excess, dispersion, low practical utility, overly technical language or a communicational organization that frustrates understanding. Ensuring formal access to information is not enough. It must be correct, clear, precise and conspicuous, without useless prolixity or strategic scarcity, and the supplier may not set up barriers that negatively interfere with the consumer's capacity to understand the terms and conditions of the contract Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 207; p. 142)652. Once again, this makes it possible to formulate semiotic vulnerability without psychologizing the consumer. The problem is not inner weakness. The problem is a communicational architecture that appears to inform while organizing noise.

A decisive consequence follows: in IOCs, the platform controls not only the modulation of the object. It also controls the means by which that modulation can be perceived, named and compared. Storefronts, metrics, rarities, narratives of prestige, p. 582 reputational systems, visual feedbacks, progression histories and interface framings are no longer peripheral elements of the environment and become part of the intelligibility of the consumed object itself. When these elements are reorganized unilaterally, the relevant loss may be obscured under an appearance of continuity. The consumer keeps nominal access, but loses functional or symbolic utility without finding clear criteria to measure the erosion. It is this kind of asymmetry that the category seeks to make visible.

The CDC already offers instruments for this, because the problem is not confined to the abstract plane of communication. Abusive practices involve precisely the supplier's use of its position of power to impose conduct on the consumer in violation of the duties of fairness, cooperation and respect for legitimate expectations. Abusiveness may also arise from the asymmetrical exploitation of the relevant meanings of the consumer relationship, especially when lengthy, obscure or surprising clauses compromise the quality of the information and the adhering party's consent Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 328-329; p. 388)653. Semiotic vulnerability thus remains within the trunk of consumer law. It merely names more precisely a current form of exploiting a vulnerability that the system's institutes already intuited.

This reading also clarifies why the “governed environment” does not compete with the “immaterial product”. In the IOC regime, the governed environment is a condition for the legal existence of the consumed product itself. The object can be enjoyed only while the platform maintains, administers and modulates access, signs, rules of use and parameters of intelligibility. This means that the environment is not an external frame of the performance, but a constitutive element of the product as legally consumed. Relevant alterations in the architecture of enjoyment may correspond, in dogmatic terms, to alterations of the object of consumption itself. p. 583 Semiotic vulnerability makes intelligible precisely the asymmetry this arrangement generates, because the platform administers both the object and the means of reading it.

This category also reorganizes classic problems of the CDC. If enjoyment depends on continuous maintenance and modulation, delivery cannot be conceived as a purely one-off act. If utility can be reconfigured, inadequacy is not limited to the obvious defect of a static thing. If there is no clear endpoint of performance, identifying the quality defect must take into account the real moment when the degradation becomes perceptible and verifiable. If the promise circulates through signs, advertising, narratives and interfaces, the correspondence between offer and enjoyment cannot be measured solely by the letter of a contractual text. Semiotic vulnerability thus functions as a category for attributing the structural asymmetry that justifies reconstructing the duty to inform, trust, adequacy and abusiveness in an opaque and mutable environment.

This problem bears directly on proof. In technically sophisticated ecosystems, the supplier controls not only the service, but also the documents, the records, the technique and the very intelligibility of the relationship. Often the consumer's difficulty is not only economic, but factual and technical. The necessary evidence is not in their hands. It lies with whoever administers the environment, the logs, the parameter settings and the relevant histories.

Facilitating the consumer's defense and redistributing the burden of proof become decisive in relationships of this kind. The technological specialization of the service and the supplier's dominant position make the reversal of the burden of proof, in many cases, the only instrument capable of actually enabling consumer protection Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 854; p. 283)654. Semiotic vulnerability is, at this point, also evidentiary vulnerability.

Consumer protection, then, is not satisfied by formally available information. It requires contracting that is informed and conscious to a degree sufficient for the consumer to know, decide on and properly use the product or service. The information p. 584 the system protects is valued for its practical usefulness in offsetting vulnerability, and not as a bureaucratic ritual of offloading data Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 211; p. 143)655. On platforms, this means that intelligibility is not communicational ornament. It is a functional prerequisite for the decision-making usefulness of information itself and, therefore, for the legitimacy of the supply.

The foundational basis of the category's dogmatic usefulness is thus identified. Semiotic vulnerability neither replaces the standards formulated nor merges with them. It identifies the relevant structural asymmetry in IOCs and allows the problem to be brought back to classic CDC categories without reducing the dispute to a “mere environmental problem” external to the object. On that basis, the next steps of the argument become manageable, in particular the requirement of institutional verifiability, the reconstruction of adequacy in terms of functional integrity and a more realistic allocation of the burden of proof in the face of opaque and governed mutations of enjoyment.

Notes

  1. To remain dogmatically useful, the formulation of semiotic vulnerability must be understood as a contemporary development of a structural datum that already organizes the consumer-law microsystem: the consumer's vulnerability as the permanent foundation of the CDC's protective interpretation. The point is not to create a parallel category, cut off from the system's grammar, but to specify, in the context of platforms, one of the ways in which the imbalance of power between supplier and consumer manifests itself today. Marques puts this quite directly when she states that, in consumer contracts, "the problem is the flagrant imbalance of power between the contracting parties. One of the parties is vulnerable (art. 4º. I), it is the weaker pole of the contractual relationship, since it cannot discuss the content of the contract", and as a rule can only accept or reject the pre-drafted package Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 324). The author adds that the definition of consumer is "the pillar that sustains the special protection", because that protection exists only by reason of the consumer party's vulnerability in market relations Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 307). Seen this way, semiotic vulnerability appears as a contemporary form of factual vulnerability, traceable to good faith, the duty to inform, the protection of trust and contractual balance. ↩
  2. Under the CDC, the consumer's vulnerability does not act merely as a sociological premise of the protective regime; it is normatively linked to objective good faith and the duty to inform, forming an operative core for containing the market's typical asymmetries. This means that the consumer's structural fragility is addressed in law by imposing positive duties on the supplier, above all in the formation and conduct of the contractual relationship. Marques makes this mechanism explicit when she stresses that the idea of "rational autonomy" depends precisely on ancillary duties to inform, to cooperate and to treat the consumer with fairness and care, since only with this "new standard of conduct in the market" can the consumer reach a "truly reflected, autonomous and 'rational" will Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 812). Along the same lines, the author states that the CDC seeks "transparency and harmony in consumer relations", imposing good faith as a requirement to prevent market practices from "making use of consumers' vulnerability", with the duty to inform being "a basic duty of suppliers" Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 1480). This keeps semiotic vulnerability anchored in the normative ground of the microsystem. ↩
  3. In this sense, Wada (2016) helps show that informational inadequacy, in consumer law, is not reduced to the simple absence of data. The failure may also arise when the supplier ensures only formal access to information, but does so under conditions that frustrate its practical intelligibility. The problem, then, is not only omission, but communication that is materially insufficient, through excess, dispersion, complexity or little concrete usefulness. Along these lines, the author expressly states that “it is not enough to ensure formal access to information”, and that it must have the legally required attributes of “correctness, clarity, precision and conspicuousness” Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 207). The requirement is qualitative, not merely quantitative. Seen this way, informational noise also makes the consumer vulnerable. Information must be “precise (= neither prolix nor scarce)”, precisely so that it remains comprehensible and serviceable to the consumer's decision-making Wada(Marques apud Wada, 2016, p. 207). The author further observes that the supplier may not interfere “significantly and negatively with the consumer's capacity to understand the terms or conditions” of the contract, nor set up “barriers or difficulties” to understanding Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 142). Hence the relevance of treating semiotic vulnerability as a contemporary form of factual vulnerability: not because the consumer is psychologically weak, but because they may be exposed to communicational architectures that appear transparent while producing material opacity. ↩
  4. The formulation of semiotic vulnerability remains internal to the CDC when it is read through the interplay of vulnerability, good faith, the duty to inform, trust and abusive practices. Abusive practices involve a breach of the supplier's duty of fairness toward the consumer, notably, in many cases, through the supplier's use of its position and power to impose a certain conduct on the consumer. In these cases, there is a breach of the duties of fairness, cooperation and respect for legitimate expectations arising from objective good faith, which include positive duties of clarification and transparent advice, as well as a duty to refrain from conduct that takes advantage of vulnerability and affects the consumer's freedom to negotiate Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 328-329). This reading is reinforced in the treatment of surprise clauses. Their abusiveness stems, even before any breach of balance in the strict sense, from the breach of the duty to inform the consumer, above all in overly long or complex contracts entered into by a lay consumer. In these situations, the supplier bears a duty of adequate clarification, failing which informational insufficiency is presumed and the quality of consent is compromised Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 388). The category of semiotic vulnerability thus does not abandon the trunk of consumer law; it merely names more precisely a contemporary form of factual vulnerability that its own institutes already intuited: the asymmetrical exploitation of the relevant meanings of the consumer relationship. ↩
  5. The CDC's response to contemporary asymmetries is not limited to the substantive plane of information and abusiveness; it already extends to the procedural plane of proof. The specialization and technological sophistication of products and services, combined with the consumer's economic or technical weakness, make the reversal of the burden of proof, in many cases, the only means capable of allowing the claim to succeed. This is explained by the supplier's dominant position, since it holds in-depth technical knowledge and controls documents, records and the contracting process itself Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 854). Often the consumer cannot prove their case simply because the necessary evidence is not in their hands but in the supplier's. This is not merely an economic obstacle, but a factual impossibility tied to the lack of conditions, including technical ones, for producing evidence, because the power of direction and specialized knowledge belong, as a rule, to the supplier Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 283). Seen this way, semiotic vulnerability can also be perceived as a contemporary form of evidentiary vulnerability: whoever controls not only the service, but the records, the technique and the intelligibility of the relationship, also controls much of the conditions for challenging it. The CDC already responds to this by redistributing burdens and facilitating the defense. ↩
  6. Consumer protection is directly tied to the requirement of information that is not merely formally available, but materially capable of guiding a decision. Legally adequate communication must produce genuinely informed and conscious contracting, that is, a state of understanding sufficient for the consumer to know what they are binding themselves to and to deliberate with a minimum of autonomy. For this reason, Wada states that, for the normative program of protection against abusive commercial practices, it is essential to ensure the consumer “informed and conscious contracting”, since “only thus will they be able to make their decision and enter into the commitment with the supplier” Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 211). This requirement has a clearly rebalancing function. The information the consumer-law system protects has no value in itself, as a bureaucratic rite of offloading data, but for its practical usefulness in offsetting the consumer's vulnerability. The author observes that this limit on the supplier seeks precisely to “rebalance the consumer relationship”, giving the consumer the opportunity to “know, negotiate and decide” Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 211). Along the same lines, the author maintains that, given the consumer's lack of capacity to choose or use the product or service properly, the supplier comes under a duty to provide the performance under conditions that allow the consumer to “make their choices and use the product or service properly” Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 143). Intelligibility, therefore, is not communicational adornment: it is a functional prerequisite for the decision-making usefulness of information. ↩
