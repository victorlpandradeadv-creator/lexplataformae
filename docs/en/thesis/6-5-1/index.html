6.5.1 On intellectual property rights as infrastructure of semiotic governance

See this section in the PDF

This section in the layers of the site

  • Dogmatics · Sections of chapter 6

p. 681

In IOC ecosystems, intellectual property rights take on a function that goes far beyond the traditional image of mere peripheral protection of immaterial assets. They do not operate only as patrimonial shields detached from the consumption experience, but as legal techniques for organizing the very environment in which enjoyment becomes possible, recognizable and economically exploitable. Intellectual property, in this context, structures positions of control over the circulation, exploitation and competitive differentiation of immaterial goods, conferring on the holder legally constructed exclusivities that bear on the markets, signs, works and technologies that organize the consumed ecosystem (Barbosa, 2010, p. 682 p. 73; p. 83; p. 117)771. In continuous digital services, it appears less as an external frame of the object and more as the infrastructure of the very semiotic governance of enjoyment.

This finding requires, from the outset, a distinction that business discourse tends to blur. The intellectual property infrastructure that sustains the platform is not homogeneous. Trademarks, copyrights, software, distinctive signs, interfaces and related institutes do not confer the same type or the same extent of exclusivity. The Brazilian legal order does not authorize treating all technical control of the environment as if it derived from a unitary and unrestricted sovereignty over the consumed experience. The very idea of intellectual property is born as a normative construction of artificial scarcity, created to make economically appropriable what, in its natural state, would circulate without equivalent barriers. Exclusivity is not a spontaneous quality of the immaterial good; it is the result of legal intervention that organizes flows of use, reproduction, circulation and exploitation Barbosa(Barbosa, 2010, p. 77-79; p. 113)772. This matters because it prevents naturalizing the platform's position as if all the power it exercises over enjoyment derived directly from the “nature” of the protected asset.

p. 683 The same caution applies to the legal form of IP itself. Intellectual rights are not to be confused with civil property over corporeal things and cannot be read as a mere abstract replica of ownership over material goods. They have a concrete discipline of their own, linked to the appropriation, exclusion and competitive exploitation of incorporeal goods, which is why the protection of trademarks, works and other assets also functions as an arrangement for organizing the market and for competitive differentiation Barbosa(Barbosa, 2010, p. 81; p. 116-117)773. With IOCs, this fact carries special weight, because the consumer does not relate to a stable, physically detachable and immediately inspectable object, but to an enjoyment whose existence depends on a technical and legal architecture concentrated in the supplier.

It is precisely for this reason that semiotic governance cannot be reduced to marketing communication. It operates at a structural level. Trademarks delimit belonging, authenticity and the distinction between what is official and what lies outside the ecosystem774. Copyrights and related rights organize the possibility of reproducing, circulating and transforming the expressive components of the experience775. Software law, in turn, bears directly on the execution, updating, modification and stability of the environment776. Together, these regimes show that IP does not hover above the ecosystem as an abstract title of ownership. It p. 684 takes part in defining what can be shown, reproduced, maintained, altered, authenticated, removed and contested.

This picture appears clearly in the platform's own contractual language. By reserving to itself and its licensors all rights, title and interests in the services, the game content, the accounts, the code, the characters, the logs, the recordings, the virtual goods, the in-game currency, the audiovisual effects, the methods of operation and the gameplay, and by prohibiting unlicensed uses of trademarks, content and services, Riot does not merely protect assets. It describes, in contractual terms, an ecosystem in which IP functions as a matrix for organizing the experience, the authenticity and the circulation of the enjoyed content777. The clause is revealing because it shows, bluntly, that control over signs, narratives, gameplay, visuals and content is not peripheral to the supply. It is part of the very infrastructure through which the environment is governed.

This becomes even more sensitive in the case of software. In Brazilian law, the protection of computer programs is not organized as a general patent over functionality, method of operation or user experience. Its protection is predominantly copyright-based, and even when a program takes part in obtaining a new and inventive technical effect, the patent does not extend to the software as such, or to the abstract logic underlying the consumed experience. Patent protection falls on a delimited technical solution, not on the result in the abstract or on all the functionality perceived by the user Barbosa(Barbosa, 2010b, p. 1128; p. 1116)778. p. 685 This distinction prevents the technical control exercised by the platform from being described as if it derived automatically from a broad patent dominion over the consumer's experience itself.

The containment deepens when one observes that, under the Brazilian regime, software does not authorize the projection of broad sovereignty over logic, algorithm, method or functionality in the abstract. Primary protection falls on the expression of useful solutions, and not on the user experience in itself. Even in the exceptional cases in which the program participates in a patentable technical solution, the algorithm considered in the abstract remains free, and the program as such does not become the object of a patent in the broad sense Barbosa(Barbosa, 2010b, p. 1123-1124)779. This is enough to block a very common rhetoric on platforms: that the mere fact that the environment depends on protected code would authorize, without remainder, absolute control over the mutability, auditability, functional equivalence and contestation of enjoyment.

For this reason too, the absence of broad software patentability in Brazil has an important dogmatic function in this item. It prevents the rhetorical importation of a model of exclusivity that the legal order itself does not recognize. Not all software is eligible for patent protection in our system, and even in cases in which it takes part in obtaining a new and inventive technical effect, protection does not fall on the program as such or on the abstract functionality of the governed experience Barbosa(Barbosa, 2010b, p. 1136; p. 1124)780. The p. 686 result is clear: the centrality of software in the platform's infrastructure does not authorize presenting it as a source of unlimited legal power over everything the consumer experiences in the ecosystem.

None of this means, on the other hand, that software is legally neutral or marginal. On the contrary. Its protection regime participates directly in the control over the use, execution, updating, maintenance and exploitation of the program. Running the software, using it and making it work already involves the exercise of an intellectual right, and not mere use of a physical medium, which helps explain why the computational infrastructure participates immediately in the organization of the enjoyment of the IOC Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 2015; p. 1959)781. Moreover, the way the law structures reverse engineering, compatibility, access and adaptation of software concretely interferes with competition, interoperability and the distribution of power in the technological environment, showing that the legal protection of the program also organizes practical conditions of access to and closure of the ecosystem Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 1871-1872; p. 1879)782.

p. 687 But it is precisely here that containment becomes indispensable again. The fact that software protection participates in organizing the environment does not authorize converting it into unrestricted sovereignty over the entire consumed experience. The value of the program is not to be confused with the text itself, and text and behavior are not the same thing legally. Copyright protection of software does not extend to the method of operation in itself, nor does it turn the holder of the code into the absolute holder of the functional result experienced by the user Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 1890; p. 1899)783. This distinction is central, because it prevents control over the execution, updating and modulation of the program from being expanded, by contract or rhetoric, to the point of erasing the consumer-law density of continuously supplied enjoyment.

From there, the function of IP as infrastructure of semiotic governance becomes clearer. The consumer depends on the supplier not only to access the object technically; they also depend on it to understand what is authentic, what is official, what counts as valid belonging, what may circulate, what may be kept and under what conditions the experience will be deemed equivalent to what was promised. Legal concentration over signs, code, content and channels of circulation produces semiotic vulnerability not only through an information deficit, but because the platform controls the very means by which the offer is understood, equivalence is perceived, loss is recognized and evidence is produced. The asymmetry ceases to be mere generic hyposufficiency and becomes structural dependence for meaning and verifiability.

The trademark offers, among the institutes of intellectual property, one of the clearest examples of the juridicization of the semiotic layer of the market. Its protection falls not only on a word, figure or formally registrable medium, but on the sign's aptitude to p. 688 present, mark, identify and distinguish products or services in economic traffic784. Not by chance, classical doctrine describes it precisely as the sign by which the product or service becomes known and distinguished in the market, requiring, for the fulfillment of this purpose, that it be distinctive, special and unmistakable Soares(Soares, 2000, p. 16; p. 109). In a convergent formulation, the trademark is conceived as a distinctive sign intended to identify and differentiate goods, products and services in relation to other identical or similar ones, which is why the legal order secures its exclusive use within the corresponding economic activity Domingues(Domingues, 1984, p. 81). Therefore, the Law already expressly protects a phenomenon of recognition mediated by signs, and not merely an isolated material medium.

This distinctiveness, however, does not present itself as an essential or natural quality of the thing, but as a socially stabilized relational effect of signification. The sign distinguishes because it produces perceptible difference, repeated association, orientation of expectation and minimally stable recognition among the public. For this reason, trademark protection is not exhausted by the strictly nominative or graphic aspect, and extends to the distinctive functionality p. 689 of the sign in its various forms of presentation, provided that it is suitable, clear, distinct and apt to make the product or service known among others in the market Soares(Soares, 2000, p. 109).

The very evolution of trademark doctrine reveals that the economic centrality of the trademark depends less on the physical medium than on its capacity to condense identity, fix perception and guide the consumer's choice, including through its advertising function and the constitution of symbolic universes associated with the product, the prestige and the social status it represents Domingues(Domingues, 1984, p. 70-71; p. 89). Especially in service marks, the separation between material medium and distinctive function becomes even more evident, because the sign no longer distinguishes a corporeal thing, but designates an activity and informs the public of its business origin Domingues(Domingues, 1984, p. 101). Trademark law, therefore, already operates, in its own dogmatics, with a semiotics applied to the organization of economic differentiation.

From this follows an important methodological consequence for this thesis. If the legal order recognizes that signs produce real economic, competitive and legal effects, there is no honest basis for denying that these same signs, when they come to govern enjoyment in continuous digital ecosystems, also structure the consumer's expectation, belonging, reputation, perceived functional value and vulnerability. The classical protection of trademarks and business signs has always been justified precisely because confusion, undue association and parasitic usurpation affect public trust, divert clientele and capture wealth built relationally in the market Soares · Domingues(Soares, 2000, p. 15; p. 194; Domingues, 1984, p. 104- 105). It is not, therefore, a matter of discovering a new type of phenomenon, but of correcting an asymmetry of legal visibility785. When the sign layer organizes origin, differentiation p. 690 and the capture of clientele in favor of the supplier, its relevance is promptly recognized. When this same layer comes to structure the criteria by which the consumer perceives authenticity, officiality, equivalence and relevant loss within the platform, the tendency of traditional legal discourse is to retreat before its immaterial dimension. What this thesis proposes is precisely to refuse this selective blindness.

In ecosystems such as that of League of Legends, this conclusion takes on particular density. Distinctiveness operates not only at the level of the Riot corporate brand or the brand of the game itself, but also in the administration of internal signs of officiality, rarity, authenticity, prestige, lawfulness of circulation, community recognition and valid belonging. The economic experience governed by the platform is permeated by signs whose force derives not from any materiality of their own, but from their repeated use, their insertion in circuits of visibility and their capacity to fix symbolic universes and guide consumption behavior Soares · Domingues(Soares, 2000, p. 93; Domingues, 1984, p. 70-71; p. 89).

Trademark and competition law already allows, moreover, protection in cases of parasitic exploitation of trade dress and undue appropriation of the sign dressing that attracts clientele, which shows that the legal order does not ignore the economic relevance of the overall perception organized by signs Soares(Soares, 2000, p. 97; p. 194). If so, the distinctive layer that forms part of the enjoyment of IOCs cannot be treated as mere off-the-books decoration of the experience. It is part of the very semiotic governance of the supply and, for this reason, may acquire consumer-law relevance when it operates as a technique of inducement, opacity, identity capture or relevant functional degradation.

p. 691 Among the various institutes of intellectual property, industrial design offers further evidence that the legal order protects not only the distinctiveness of signs, but also the ornamental dimension of the economically exploited form. If, in trademark law, exclusivity bears on the sign's capacity to identify, differentiate and guide public perception, in industrial design protection shifts to the visual configuration of the product itself, when that configuration gives it a new and original aesthetic result and makes it fit to occupy a specific place in competition786. The dogmatically relevant point here is that the law does not confine itself to protecting technical function, material utility or business origin. It recognizes, in its own key, the legal relevance of ornamental form as an element of market insertion, attraction and differentiation of the good.

This function is neither lateral nor accidental. The protection of industrial design starts precisely from the premise that the aesthetic configuration of the product can add to it seductive power and greater commercial value, directly inflecting the public's choice. Form, texture, motifs, lines and colors cease to appear as simple visual finishing and come to integrate the economy of competition as legally relevant assets, because they make the product more attractive, desirable and commercially competitive Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 21). For this reason too, doctrine identifies in industrial design a space of economic action of its own, different from the field of technical utility: its incidence falls on the public's choice in the space of commerce, approaching, in this respect, the market logic of trademarks, although by a different route, since here differentiation not only signals the product, but integrates its very sensory presentation Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 68). The Law of intellectual property, therefore, protects not only what distinguishes by sign reference; it also protects what seduces by aesthetic conformation.

This fact becomes even clearer when one observes that the exclusive right of industrial design does not fall on the utilitarian object itself, but on the ornamental addition that singularizes it in the market. The example is eloquent: a plate ornamented with a protected figure may be the object of exclusivity, whereas the same plate, identical in size, material and functionality, but devoid of that design, remains free for reproduction by third parties Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 50). The boundary of protection is not, therefore, in the materiality of the good nor in its practical function, but in the aesthetic investment incorporated into its external configuration. This is what makes it possible to understand why two goods equivalent in structure and utility p. 692 can become economically distinct at the moment of their acquisition by reason only of the visual form that covers them Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 39). The legal system thus recognizes that ornamentation is not an irrelevant adornment, but a factor of differentiation apt to generate preference, value and capture of choice in the market 787.

p. 693 The functional specificity of industrial design confirms this reading. Within the architecture of intellectual property, each exclusive right fulfills its own constitutionally delimited role, and the ornamental function is confused neither with the expressive function, proper to other regimes, nor with the technological function, characteristic of patents. The protection of industrial design is legitimized precisely because it remunerates and organizes a specific type of creative investment: that aimed at the aesthetic conformation of the product as a competitive differential in the market Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 45). For this very reason, the legal order requires that industrial design produce a new and original visual result in the external configuration of the object, apt to serve as a type for industrial manufacture, delimiting its scope of incidence to a precise layer of the economic value of the good 788. It is not, therefore, a generic monopoly over the thing, but legally specialized protection over its ornamental function.

If the legal order already recognizes that the visual form of the product, regardless of its technical utility, can concentrate economic value, guide preference and justify legal exclusivity, then there is no honest basis for downgrading the ornamental dimension of digital ecosystems to the condition of a mere accidental coating of the experience. In environments of continuous consumption, the visuality of interfaces, the aesthetic composition of skins, distinctive finishes, graphic effects, visually coded signs of rarity and the iconographic coherence of the universe offered to the user are not neutral elements or external to enjoyment. They participate in the very economy of belonging, attractiveness and p. 694 perceived value, functioning as components of the semiotic governance of the IOC. The difference between the ornamented corporeal object and the continuously enjoyed immaterial object does not erase this structural fact; it merely shifts its medium.

Industrial design reinforces, by another route, the central critique under discussion. In trademark law, the Law already admits that signs guide perception, association and trust in the market. In industrial design, it also admits that the aesthetic conformation of the good, as a bearer of seduction and commercial novelty, influences the public's choice and deserves specific protection. Intellectual property, thus, does not confine itself to protecting technology or abstract ownership, since it legally recognizes that distinction and ornamentation are economically operative dimensions of market life. It thus becomes even less defensible to resist seeing that, in IOC ecosystems, the private management of visuality, apparent rarity, distinctive aesthetics and the ornamental coherence of the environment forms part of the very structure of enjoyment and may, for this reason, produce effects relevant to consumer law when mobilized as a technique of inducement, opacity, identity capture or functional downgrading of the continuously supplied experience.

This ambivalence must be acknowledged soberly. On the one hand, intellectual property is a legitimate foundation for the organization of the market and for competitive predictability. The right of property is constitutionally guaranteed789, and the protection of industrial creations, trademarks and other distinctive signs is born, in the constitutional text, already linked to the social interest and to the technological and economic development of the country790. On the other hand, the same constitutional order places this exclusivity back within material limits, by subjecting the economic order to the social function of property and to consumer protection. In other words, exclusivity is legitimate, but it is not metaphysically sovereign. It is born already traversed by criteria of compatibilization.

For this reason, the dogmatically relevant problem lies not in the abstract protection of assets, but in their use as a technique of institutional opacity and of preventive blocking of control over the effects of the supply. The invocation of secrecy, license, the right to modify, dominion over the code or trademark protection cannot operate as a generic veto on minimum verifiability, on the assessment of functional loss and on the institutional reconstruction p. 695 of the dispute. What is protected is the technological and expressive asset, not the transformation of this asset into a screen for opportunistic mutability, sanction without verifiable reasoning, blocking of auditability or erasure of avenues of restoration.

Treating intellectual property as infrastructure of semiotic governance does not mean downgrading its legitimacy, nor absorbing it into the CDC as if it were a mere sectoral detail. It means recognizing that, in IOCs, it forms part of the supply regime and, for this reason, its exercise remains subject to criteria of compatibilization with adequacy, verifiability and restoration. It is in this key that the following tensions are prepared. Exclusivity is not to be confused with immunity from auditability. The right to update and modify does not become, by itself, an authorization to downgrade enjoyment significantly without equivalence. And the protection of signs, code and content does not erase the incidence of good faith, social function and consumer protection when the governance of the environment comes to operate as a technique of capture, opacity or degradation of the continuously supplied experience.

This shows that IP is not a simple patrimonial appendix of the system, but a set of specific legal positions of appropriation, exclusion and exploitation. The specificity appears clearly when the author examines trademarks and observes the logical impossibility of fully assimilating the property of physical movable goods to that of intellectual goods.   9. The protection of software, in Brazilian law, does not authorize the projection of broad technical sovereignty over abstract logic, functionality or user experience. Barbosa delimits precisely that software is primarily protected by a copyright-type regime, which falls on the expression of useful solutions, that is, on the code and certain non-literal manifestations, but does not reach solutions of technical effect as such. When software participates in producing a new and inventive technical effect, there may be a patent; even so, the patent will not cover the object already protected by Law n. 9.609/98, but only the means of obtaining the technical effect that transcend that object. That is, neither copyright protection of the code nor any possible patent protection of the technical solution authorizes general appropriation of the underlying abstract logic. This distinction is reinforced when Barbosa deals with equivalence in patent matters and warns that it does not represent appropriation of the algorithm, but only of the alternative applications of the protected technical solution; therefore, in Brazilian law, “the program as such […] will never be the object of the patent” Barbosa(Barbosa, 2010b, p. 1124). It is exactly this key that prevents treating software as a source of broad sovereignty over functionality, method of operation or user experience. ↩

This rules out any unreflective importation of more expansive models and prevents treating software, in itself, as an object naturally capturable by patent logic.   11. The protection of software participates in the practical organization of access and in the modulation of the technological environment itself. It protects not only a text or an abstract asset, but concretely interferes with the conditions of opening, closure, compatibility and adaptation of the ecosystem. Barbosa shows this clearly when dealing with reverse engineering: depending on how legislation admits or restricts it, the conditions of competition, compatibility between systems, access of new agents to the market and even the price structure are concretely altered. According to the author, if legislation facilitates this practice, it tends to promote imitative competition and lower prices; if it prohibits it, the tendency is toward artificial product differentiation, difficulty of compatibility and reinforcement of the market power of large international producers Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 1871-1872). This organizing dimension also appears when the author states that the structuring of property relations over software was conceived as an instrument to organize the market and correct effects of concentration and of the speed of technique in the field of informatics Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 1879). The point, therefore, is clear: the legal protection of software participates directly in the practical organization of access, interoperability, adaptation and distribution of power in the technological environment, helping to explain the governance infrastructure of IOCs. ↩
  12. Software does not function only as a neutral technical means, but as an object of legal protection directly linked to control over the execution, use, updating and exploitation of the program. It is along this line that Barbosa, dealing with the software license, states that the exercise of rights relating to the program “consists exactly in the use and enjoyment of the programs” and that their effective application necessarily implies reproduction susceptible to copyright protection, which is why what is at stake is an “exercise of the intellectual right, and not only use of the physical medium” Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 2015). The legal protection of software bears on the very practice of running, using and making the program work, which helps explain why, in IOCs, the computational infrastructure participates directly in the organization of enjoyment and in the asymmetry of contestation. This dimension of control is not exhausted in immediate execution. Barbosa also shows that the software regime engages with maintenance, technical support, development of improvements and introduction of modifications to the original program, observing that there are circumstances in which access to the source code becomes necessary not only for voluptuary alterations, but also for correcting errors and updating the software for the purposes for which it was licensed Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 1959). The legal regime of software, thus, helps to understand how positions of control over the execution, circulation and operational continuity of the program are structured, without it being possible to extract from this unlimited sovereignty over the entire experience consumed in the ecosystem. ↩
  13. The legal protection of software does not become, by itself, unrestricted sovereignty over the entire consumed experience, because the primary source of value of the program is not in the text itself, but in its behavior, and text and behavior are legally distinct. Barbosa underlines exactly this point when observing that one of the greatest discussions in the field of intellectual property applied to software stems from the fact that the primary source of value of the program is not in the text itself, but in its behavior, it being possible for different codes to lead to identical functional results Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 1899). The dogmatic consequence is crucial: protection of the code does not authorize, by itself, general appropriation of the functional result experienced by the user. This containment is reinforced when the author deals with the internal form of the program. Although he recognizes that copyright protection is not limited to the literal expression of the instructions, and may also reach the mode of organization of the elements that make up the software, Barbosa warns that, “to the extent that the internal form is directly related to functionality, it will not be protected by Copyright”, since the latter “does not protect the structure in itself”; in other words, “the copyright protection of the program does not extend to the method of operation” Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 1890). The legal regime of software helps to understand the infrastructure of control over the execution, access and modulation of the technological environment, but does not authorize deriving from it absolute power over functional equivalence, user experience or the entire enjoyment consumed in the ecosystem. ↩
  14. The distinctive function of the trademark cannot be reduced to a bureaucratic technicality of registration. It expresses, in legal language, the recognition that certain signs perform in the market a function of identification, differentiation and stabilization of expectations. In simple terms, the trademark is protected because it allows products and services to be presented, marked, known and distinguished in economic traffic, organizing social perception about what is offered, who offers it and how this offering should be recognized. The requirement that the sign be distinctive, special and unmistakable is not, therefore, a formal whim of the system, but the way in which the Law selects the signs apt to perform this function of orientation in the market Soares · Domingues(Soares, 2000, p. 16; p. 109; Domingues, 1984, p. 81). This means that trademark protection does not fall on the raw materiality of a word, design or figure, but on the semiotic aptitude of the sign to produce socially stable recognition. What is protected is not only the “name” as a graphic sequence, but its capacity to individualize, differentiate and refer the public to a certain business origin, a certain reputation or a certain bundle of expectations. For this very reason classical trademark doctrine does not exhaust the trademark in its strict visual form and recognizes that its raison d'être rests precisely on its force of identification before consumers, including when the sign projects beyond the traditional nominative format. The trademark has legal value because it functions socially as a vector of distinction. This dimension becomes even more evident when one observes the importance of the advertising function of the trademark and its growing autonomy in relation to the material medium of the product. In contemporary market experience, one promotes not only an anonymous object, but a sign capable of fixing perception, condensing identity and guiding the consumer's choice. In many contexts, the public chooses by the trademark and does not even know, immediately, the manufacturer or the distributor. The distinctive sign thus becomes a cognitive shortcut, a reference of origin and the symbolic center of gravity of the offer. It is precisely this aptitude to condense economic and social meaning that explains its legal protection Domingues(Domingues, 1984, p. 89). The phenomenon becomes even clearer in service marks. In them, the sign is not attached to a permanently marked corporeal thing, but designates an activity and informs the public of its origin. The trademark, in this case, practically detaches from the physical medium and comes to operate almost purely as a sign of identification, trust and differentiation. This reinforces the perception that trademark law already works, at its core, with a semiotic layer of the market: it protects not only a visual object, but a social mechanism of signification that guides recognition, association and economic choice Domingues(Domingues, 1984, p. 101). Seen from this angle, distinctiveness is the technical name the Law gives to the capacity of a sign to organize socially relevant difference in the market. The trademark is neither a mere ornament of the product nor a lateral detail of business activity. It is part of the very intelligibility of economic circulation, because it allows the public to perceive what that thing is, where it comes from, what it resembles, what it differs from and why it should be recognized as belonging to a certain origin. In other words, trademark dogmatics has long contained the legal recognition that signs produce real effects of orientation, trust and valuation in the economic world. ↩
  15. The legal protection of trademarks and business signs has always started from the premise that the sign layer of the market has concrete economic relevance. The Law protects it because it knows that confusion, undue association, strategic imitation and parasitic usurpation are not superficial accidents, but ways of capturing value through the appropriation of signs that organize public perception. When the legal order represses signs capable of misleading, diverting clientele or unduly exploiting the notoriety of others, it recognizes, quite explicitly, that signs are not neutral adornments. They concentrate trust, reputation, social memory, economic attractiveness and the capacity to guide market behavior Soares · Domingues(Soares, 2000, p. 15; p. 194; Domingues, 1984, p. 104-105). For this very reason, protection is not limited to the isolated sign in the narrow sense. Doctrine and forensic practice recognize that trade dress, the overall sign dressing and the organized appearance of the offer may also be the object of parasitic exploitation and unfair competition. This means that the legal order is concerned not only with the literal reproduction of a name or logo, but with the appropriation of the perceptual universe through which clientele identifies, associates and chooses. The repression of parasitism over trade dress reveals that what is protected is not only the formally registered graphic trait, but the overall sign arrangement that gives intelligibility and power of attraction to the supplier's economic presence Soares(Soares, 2000, p. 97; p. 194). This finding is decisive because it dismantles a recurring resistance when the problem shifts to consumption on digital platforms. If the Law already recognizes that signs, images, associations and globally organized perceptions have sufficient legal density to justify strong protection in favor of the supplier, there is no coherence in treating this same layer as irrelevant, ethereal or legally invisible when it structures the consumer's experience. The novelty of the thesis does not lie in stating that signs matter. Trademark and competition law have stated this for a long time. The novelty lies in refusing the selective blindness that makes this relevance appear only when it serves to protect origin, clientele and business investment, but disappear when the management of signs comes to govern belonging, expectation, recognition and perceived functional value on the user's side. In continuous digital ecosystems, the platform administers not only technical access to the service, but also the symbolic field within which enjoyment is understood. Signs of officiality, authenticity, rarity, prestige, lawfulness of circulation, community recognition and equivalence are not mere decorative accessories of the experience. They are part of the way in which the consumer understands what they own, what they lost, what they gained, what is worth more, what is worth less and what remains recognized as valid belonging within the environment. When this sign layer is governed opaquely, manipulated to induce behavior, or reconfigured so as to significantly degrade the economic and experiential meaning of what was enjoyed, what is at stake is not a “purely psychological” harm or an abstraction without legal grounding. What is at stake is the unilateral administration of signs that organize value, orientation and dependence within the supply itself. It is exactly here that the asymmetry of protection appears. The legal system shows enormous sophistication in protecting the supplier against the parasitic capture of signs, against public confusion and against the diversion of clientele. But it tends to be much less developed when the harm falls on the consumer inserted in a closed environment whose semiotic architecture is entirely controlled by the platform. The step proposed by this thesis does not consist in dissolving trademark law into consumer law, nor in denying the legitimacy of distinctive protection in favor of the holder. It consists, rather, in recognizing that the same layer of signs whose economic relevance the Law already admits may also operate as a technique of inducement, closure, opacity and capture in continuous digital consumption. In short, it is not a matter of creating legal relevance where it does not exist, but of redistributing dogmatic visibility to a phenomenon that the system itself already knows how to see, although traditionally it does so unilaterally. ↩
  16. “Art. 95. Industrial design is considered to be the ornamental plastic form of an object or the ornamental set of lines and colors that may be applied to a product, providing a new and original visual result in its external configuration and that may serve as a type for industrial manufacture.” Brasil(Brasil, [2024]c). ↩
  17. Industrial design constitutes, in the system of intellectual property, the specific form of protection of the economically relevant ornamental dimension of the product. Its protection does not fall on the technical utility of the good nor on its practical function, but on the aesthetic conformation that adds to it a competitive value of its own. It is precisely for this reason that doctrine identifies in design a dimension of “seductive power”, apt to make the product more aesthetically attractive and, with this, to add greater commercial value to it Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 21). The regime does not protect, therefore, the useful thing itself, but the visually relevant form in which it presents itself to the market. This fact helps explain the specific economic space in which industrial design operates. As Barbosa observes, its incidence lies not in the realm of utilitarian needs nor in the field of technology, proper to patents, but in the space of commerce, that is, in the sphere of the public's choice and market differentiation Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 68). Hence the relevance of the notion of “commercial novelty”, mobilized by the author to show that industrial design is not intended to protect the functioning of the product, but its capacity to produce attraction and preference through singular aesthetic conformation Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 68). In this key, the appearance of the good ceases to be a simple peripheral finish and becomes part of the economy of competition as a legally relevant asset. The delimitation of the exclusive right confirms this reasoning. The industrial design right does not monopolize the object in its materiality nor prevent the free reproduction of its utilitarian function. What it safeguards is the legally protected ornamental addition. Barbosa illustrates this didactically by observing that a plate ornamented with a certain industrial design cannot be manufactured and sold by third parties without the holder's authorization, whereas the same plate, identical in size, material and functionality, but without that ornament, remains freely reproducible Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 50). The boundary of protection, thus, is not in the utility of the good, but in the aesthetic investment that singularizes its external configuration and gives it a competitive differential. This is why industrial design proves apt to produce economic distinction even between functionally equivalent goods. As the author notes, ornamental differentiation transcends the utility of the objects to which it is applied, allowing two cups identical in material and practical aptitude to become completely distinct at the moment of acquisition by reason only of the sensory form that covers them Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 39). In a related formulation, Barbosa stresses that what singularizes industrial designs is the fact that this differentiation is part of the product itself, and does not merely signal it externally, as occurs with other institutes Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 42). Aesthetics thus appears not as an irrelevant ornament, but as a component of the very economic value captured in the market. The position of industrial design within the architecture of intellectual property also confirms the specificity of its function. According to Barbosa, the constitutional regulation of the cumulation of protections presupposes that each form of exclusive right fulfills a determined role, aimed at the remuneration and organization of a specific type of creative investment, so that the ornamental function is distinguished, in the system, both from the expressive function and from the technological function Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 45). This means that the protection of industrial design does not constitute a generic prize for creativity, but a functionally delimited instrument to protect the aesthetic conformation of the product as an economically operative dimension. The system, therefore, does not treat trademark, patent, copyright and industrial design as interchangeable boxes, but as different regimes aimed at distinct layers of immaterial value. This point matters directly for the present thesis because it shows that the legal order already admits, expressly and in an institutionalized way, the legal relevance of ornamentation. The Law protects not only what distinguishes by external signaling, as occurs with trademarks; it also protects what differentiates by the very appearance incorporated into the good. In both cases, it is recognized that non-utilitarian layers of the economic experience can concentrate value, guide preferences and justify legal protection. The difference lies in the mode of incidence: in the trademark, distinctiveness signals; in industrial design, differentiation is part of the very visual conformation of the product Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 42; p. 68). The common structural fact, however, remains the same: the legal system already knows that the form in which something appears to the public concretely interferes with economic circulation and with the formation of choice. From this perspective, industrial design reinforces the conclusion that the semiotic governance of the market is not a phenomenon foreign to the Law of intellectual property. On the contrary, it is already captured by the system when the aesthetic appearance of the good comes to function as a competitive asset, endowed with seductive power, commercial novelty and the capacity to guide the public's preference Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 21; p. 68). This rules out any attempt to reduce intellectual property to the protection of technique or of abstract ownership, revealing that the legal order also juridifies the ornamental layer of the economy. It is exactly for this reason that industrial design has special demonstrative value in the context of Immaterial Objects of Consumption. If the Law already recognizes, at the level of corporeal goods, that ornamental appearance can add commercial value, capture the public's choice and justify specific protection, there is no methodologically serious reason to treat as legally invisible the aesthetic layer that structures enjoyment in continuous digital ecosystems. On platforms, the user experience is also shaped by visuality, finish, iconographic coherence, rarity coded by appearance, graphic effects and aesthetic elements that are not technically necessary for the functioning of the environment, but are economically decisive for its attractiveness, its internal differentiation and its capacity to produce belonging, desire and permanence. The analogy, here, does not intend to equate distinct legal regimes nor to automatically convert skins, interfaces or digital visual arrangements into industrial designs. Its function is more precise: to show that the legal system itself already recognizes, in an institutional key, that ornament is value, that aesthetic seduction is economically operative and that the form in which the good presents itself to the public may form part of the legally relevant core of the market experience Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 21; p. 39; p. 45; p. 68). ↩
