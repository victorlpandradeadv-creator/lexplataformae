6.3.1 On the legal concept of open auditability in continuous digital services

See this section in the PDF

Concepts in this section

  • Open auditability

p. 639

Open auditability designates, in continuous digital services, a standard of technically qualified and institutionally usable transparency, enforceable whenever p. 640 the supplier carries out relevant alterations, sanctions or reconfigurations capable of reaching the core of enjoyment of the Immaterial Object of Consumption. This is not a requirement of total transparency, nor of unrestricted opening of the technical infrastructure, nor an obligation to expose source code, the integral architecture of the system or sensitive industrial secrets. The point is another. What is required is a verifiable minimum that allows one to reconstruct, in a legally useful key, what was altered, when it was altered, why it was altered and with what relevant effects on the consumer's enjoyment. Without this minimum, the continuous mutability of the service tends to become opaque power, and opacity, in turn, becomes a kind of informal shield against duties of information, good faith and control of abusiveness. Instead of real transparency, there is only a documentary staging of compliance, a situation incompatible with the standard of adequate and conspicuous information required by the CDC722.

This formulation is not born of conceptual whim nor of fascination with technological regulation. It results from the very structure of continuous supply. In governed digital services, performance is not exhausted in a fully consummated initial delivery. The bond is projected over time through updates, corrections, rebalancings, interface reconfigurations, rule changes, technical migrations, restrictions of use, compatibility changes and, not rarely, sanctions falling on access, visibility and permanence. In such an environment, treating execution as if it were a neutral, empty or legally invisible space would be far too crude. Objective good faith, in relationships of this nature, projects continuous duties of information, cooperation, fairness and protection of trust, so that the supplier cannot reserve for itself all the intelligibility of the process of mutation of the service and leave the consumer only with the final result already imposed723.

p. 641 It is for this reason that open auditability must be understood as an internal unfolding of the duty to inform and not as an institute foreign to the microsystem. Information, in the CDC, does not fulfill its function just because it exists somewhere, nor because it was deposited in extensive documents, cross-references, FAQs or update histories that the consumer will hardly locate, understand and connect to the effective impact suffered. What matters is its reconstructive and decisional utility. If the supplier alters obligations, parameters of use, compatibility conditions or central elements of enjoyment without providing a minimally intelligible basis for reconstructing what occurred, it does not merely communicate badly. It prevents control. And preventing control, in a context of strong technical asymmetry, is a material way of deteriorating the consumer's very position within the relationship724.

The critique of merely performative transparency gains its own density here. In many digital services, the company produces an expressive volume of information, but distributes it in a flow, form and hierarchy that make it difficult to perceive the essential. The change exists, the notice exists, the document exists, the history exists, but the useful reconstruction of the event does not exist. The consumer knows that “something changed”, but cannot discern precisely what changed, what criterion was adopted, what practical effects follow from it and how to demonstrate institutionally the impact suffered. Transparency, then, appears as a performance of regularity, not as a real condition of verifiability. It is precisely for this reason that open auditability becomes necessary. It does not require knowing everything. It requires knowing enough so that the mutation of the supply does not remain shielded by its own form of presentation725.

p. 642 This standard also has a decisive evidentiary presupposition. In the universe of the IOC, the platform concentrates records, histories, logs, application criteria, relevant versions, transactional data, communications effectively sent, alteration parameters and technical documentation sufficient to narrate the event unilaterally. If the legal system accepts that all this information remain entirely under the supplier's narrative monopoly, consumer protection dehydrates. Facilitating the defense and redistributing the burden of proof cease to be occasional instruments and become a condition of minimum effectiveness of the protective regime itself. Open auditability, at this point, functions as a technique for reducing informational and evidentiary asymmetry. It places the conflict back on reconstructible ground and prevents the lack of access to the elements of proof from becoming a practical impossibility of protection726.

The trigger of application, however, needs to be delimited rigorously so that the standard does not slip into maximalism. Open auditability is not enforceable in the face of any microscopic adjustment, any cosmetic change or any irrelevant oscillation of the service. Its proper field begins when the alteration, unilateral modulation, suppression, migration, nerf, discontinuation, reconfiguration of rule or interface, or even the sanction applied to the user, relevantly affects enjoyment. This relevance can appear in several ways. It can reach access and permanence, the functional integrity of the object, promised equivalences, the symbolic position of the IOC in the ecosystem, or even the consumer's conditions of proof and challenge. The central point is that the change must be capable of downgrading enjoyment, frustrating legitimate expectation or abusively concentrating the intelligibility of what occurred in the hands of the supplier.

p. 643 In such cases, the requirement of open auditability materializes in a minimum content far more modest than the caricature of “total openness”, but far more robust than corporate self-justification. The supplier must make available, in an institutionally useful way, an intelligible record of what was altered and of the moment of the alteration; a minimum technical justification, that is, the declared rationale and the objective assumed for the change; a description of the relevant impacts on enjoyment and on the parameters of the IOC; preservation of verifiable trails, such as histories of relevant versions, minimum logs of event, transaction or alteration; and, in cases of enforcement, minimally reconstructible criteria for the sanction applied. Furthermore, there must be a possibility of verification by qualified third parties, including protection bodies, expert examination or judicial review, without the regime of technical secrecy being used as an absolute screen against any scrutiny727.

This point is important because the corporate objection usually comes in predictable clothing: requiring auditability would be equivalent to requiring the opening of code, the surrender of industrial secrets, the exposure of know-how or the unprotection of software. That is not what this is about. The legal order already coexists, in various contexts, with limited forms of scrutiny compatible with the preservation of the informational asset. Judicial secrecy, restricted expert access, delimited functional analysis, technically necessary use for certain purposes and institutional control are not demolitions of exclusivity. They are legally known forms of reconciling protection of the asset with the need for verification. The protection of software does not turn it into a sacred black box. Nor does the discipline of the computer program convert the supplier into a sovereign over each and every legally relevant consequence of its operation728.

p. 644 Along the same lines, the protection of industrial secrets does not operate as an absolute veto on external control. Generically invoking “secrecy” or “technical sensitivity” without delimiting what in fact needs to remain reserved and without admitting any form of institutionally relevant reconstruction is not enough. For secrecy to be legally respected, it needs to be identified and carved out, not treated as a magic fog that dissolves every demand for accountability. The system admits protection of strategic business information, of course. What it should not admit is that this protection be instrumentalized to completely prevent the reconstruction of the legally relevant effects of the supply, above all when the very lawfulness of the conduct depends on this reconstruction. The company can still protect its secret. What it cannot do is want secrecy about the very impact of the change it imposed on the consumer729.

This is even more evident because technical exclusivity, in itself, does not eliminate the possibility of institutional examination of the operational effects of the system. What matters to Consumer Law is not, as a rule, the integral contemplation of the internal ingenuity of the program, but the possibility of verifying how a certain operating regime, a certain alteration or a certain criterion applied had repercussions on the enjoyment, continuity, equivalence or position of the consumer in the ecosystem. The legally relevant examination falls on the operation for purposes of control, not on the parasitic appropriation of the asset. For this reason, the existence of a secret or of protected software does not exclude limited forms of technical, expert and institutionally mediated analysis. Open auditability does not compete with exclusivity. It limits the abusive use of exclusivity as an excuse for irresponsibility730.

p. 645 The dogmatic function of the standard, then, becomes clearer. Open auditability is a legal technology of controllability. It reduces the informational and evidentiary asymmetry proper to the private governance of the IOC and brings continuous mutability back to a minimally verifiable field. This allows quality defect, inadequacy, abusiveness, violation of the duty to inform and breach of trust to be discussed on a reconstructible basis, and not in a crude confrontation between corporate narrative and the consumer's subjective feeling. Without this standard, the supplier can always claim that it changed for technical reasons, that it informed as best it could, that the impact was small, that the alteration was provided for in some document and that the consumer simply did not understand or did not like it. With it, it becomes possible to ask seriously what was changed, on what basis, with what effect and with what objective trails of reconstruction.

This gain is of enormous importance in long-term contracts. In relationships of continuous execution, the consumer places trust not only in an initial utility, but in the minimally intelligible permanence of the bond and in the possibility of understanding its relevant changes. The long-term relationship requires cooperation, adaptation and preservation of the legitimate expectations formed in the course of the contract. It is not enough, therefore, to keep the contract alive on the nominal plane while its execution becomes opaque and unilaterally reconfigurable in non-reconstructible terms. The consumer's trust is not protected with slogans of continuous evolution nor with a generic promise of “service improvements”. It requires a minimum regime of verifiable continuity of information about the execution of the bond731.

For that very reason, open auditability also connects to the mechanisms of specific relief. The CDC was not designed to allow the supplier to turn the injury into a mere accounting cost and move on. When the relevant mutation of the supply depends on records, versions, criteria and documentation concentrated in the hands of the company, the system already has instruments compatible with disclosure, early production of evidence, p. 646 specific relief and measures aimed at reconstructing the bond as concretely executed. This is especially important in continuous digital services, in which the abstract contractual instrument is almost never enough to reveal the effective content of the performance. Without access to the relevant versions, to the execution records and to the elements that demonstrate the supply carried out, the legal discussion runs the risk of being held hostage by the documentary fiction of the base contract, which often says little about the real life of the service732.

From this follows the legal consequence of non-auditability. When the supplier, in the face of a relevant alteration, does not provide the auditable minimum compatible with the consumer relationship, transparency must be treated as inadequate. And, depending on the intensity of the case, this inadequacy can function as evidence or direct manifestation of structural abusiveness of the supply regime. This is not a peripheral lack of informational politeness. It is the frustration of a duty essential to the controllability of the bond. In practical terms, this legitimizes the imposition of obligations to perform aimed at preserving and disclosing records, the dynamic redistribution of the burden of proof, the adoption of presumptions favorable to the consumer in the face of unjustified refusal to cooperate and the control of the validity or effectiveness of clauses and policies that seek to shield non-verifiable alterations. Where the relevant change cannot be minimally reconstructed, private governance has already entered a legally suspect zone.

This effect is even more important because the contemporary problem is not only the absence of information, but the strategic organization of information. The platform can flood the consumer with announcements, changelogs, banners, dialog boxes, policies, disclaimers and FAQs, and still preserve for itself the effective monopoly over the intelligibility of what matters. Open auditability appears precisely to break this false sufficiency. It is not enough to inform that something changed. The meaning of the change for the consumed enjoyment must be made institutionally reconstructible. Without this, the mutability of the service and the technical concentration of records produce, together, a kind of private evidentiary fortress.

p. 647 For this reason too, the standard prepares the next stage of the section. On its own, open auditability does not resolve the whole controversy, because demonstrating what changed and how it changed does not exhaust the question about the material legitimacy of the change. It will still be necessary to assess whether or not the alteration preserved the substance of the legitimately acquired enjoyment. But without open auditability this judgment becomes fragile, because there is no basis to reconstruct the mutation itself. The standard functions, then, as an institutional condition of intelligibility for the subsequent examination of functional value equivalence. First, the change is reconstructed. Then, its material legitimacy is measured. This order matters. Without it, the supplier will always have the advantage of the fog.

At the methodological level, open auditability plays a role similar to that which semiotic vulnerability played earlier. It transforms a structural diagnosis into a dogmatic operator. It is no longer said only that the platform controls the environment, the signs, the metrics and the proof. In the face of this concentration, a verifiable minimum of reconstruction of the supply comes to be required. It is no longer affirmed only that transparency can be performative. A criterion becomes available to separate useful transparency from scenographic transparency. The opacity of private governance is no longer merely denounced. It comes to be qualified as a controllable legal insufficiency.

For this reason, open auditability must be understood as a criterion of legitimacy of platform architectures, and not as abstract hostility toward them. Platforms can innovate, update, correct, rebalance, migrate and protect their technical assets. What they cannot do is do so in a regime of institutional non-reconstructibility, as if the very complexity of the system authorized them to decide alone what matters, what was altered and what can or cannot be known by those who enjoy the service and by those who control it legally. In relationships of continuous consumption, complexity does not revoke good faith. Technique does not suspend the duty to inform. And industrial secrecy, though legitimate, does not absolve the company of the obligation to allow the supply to be minimally reconstituted when its mutations relevantly reach the consumer's enjoyment733.

p. 648 In the end, this is the core of the legal concept of open auditability in continuous digital services. A standard of minimum verifiability, compatible with the protection of software and of business secrecy, but incompatible with the opaque shielding of execution; an unfolding of the duty to inform, of objective good faith and of facilitating the consumer's defense; a criterion for reducing evidentiary and informational asymmetry; and an institutional condition for relevant mutations of the IOC to cease being accepted as faits accomplis narrated only by the supplier itself. Without this standard, private governance comes too close to practical sovereignty. With it, the mutability of the service remains possible, but no longer hovers above legal control.

Notes

  1. Open auditability can be understood as an internal unfolding of the logic already present in the consumer-law microsystem, and not as an exotic regulatory graft. The starting point lies in the very standard of adequate information required by the CDC, which is not satisfied by the mere formal provision of data, but calls for content that is simultaneously complete, free and useful, with a prohibition on diluting relevant communication through loose, redundant or useless information for the consumer. The Manual synthesizes this standard by requiring “correct”, “clear”, “precise” and “conspicuous” information Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 128]). The same orientation is reinforced when the work records the “need to guarantee the consumer the possibility of a priori control of the contract”, the possibility of control only a posteriori not being enough, on pain of violation of the right to adequate information Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 129]). In addition, clear and adequate information governs the pre-contractual moment, the conclusion of the contract, the contract itself and the post-contractual moment, affecting “the essence of the deal”, since it is part of the contractual content and, if it fails, may represent a quality defect in the product or service offered Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 126]). ↩
  2. The notion of open auditability, in continuous digital services, can be legally traced back to the relational duties arising from objective good faith throughout the entire contractual execution. In bonds marked by functional mutability, successive updates and technical reconfigurations capable of affecting the consumer's enjoyment, it is not compatible with the CDC to treat execution as an opaque zone, immune to duties of intelligible reconstruction of what was altered, maintained, restricted or suppressed. The time lapse between the birth of the obligation and the satisfaction of the main performance “can no longer be seen as an ‘empty space’”, since the obligational relationship constitutes “a continuous whole”, within which the parties are bound by ancillary duties of conduct arising from objective good faith, among them “the duty to inform, to cooperate, duty of care, of secrecy, of advice, of fairness etc.” Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 110). Failing the duty of cooperation and fairness means defaulting, even if partially, including when the supplier hinders the consumer's access to their rights Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 236). ↩
  3. Protective information only fulfills its function when it allows the consumer to usefully reconstruct the supply and exercise effective control over its execution. This is not a technicist obsession with total openness. What matters, in a consumer-law key, is that the information provided be sufficient to allow monitoring, understanding of the relevant milestones of the performance and verification of its conformity. The very existence of information shifts the problem to the plane of implementation and control, insofar as it “places the burden of implementation on informed consumers”, as well as on the market forces that seek to avoid exposure of illegality Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 82). Not informing the execution deadline, or leaving its definition to a later moment under the exclusive control of the supplier, places the consumer in a situation of extreme vulnerability, since without intelligible reconstruction of the way the obligation is performed the consumer loses capacity for control and the supplier comes to dictate the rules of the game unilaterally Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 208). Prior information and express authorization fulfill a rebalancing function: they not only communicate, but condition the execution of the performance on a minimum form of verifiability by the recipient, compensating for the structural imbalance of the consumer relationship Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 210). ↩
  4. The critique of merely formal transparency leads directly to the requirement of minimum verifiability over relevant alterations and impacts of the supply. The simple existence of an initial document or of an abstract communication of the conditions of the deal is not enough to satisfy the protective standard of the CDC if the consumer cannot control substantial modifications of the performance. Wada makes this explicit in stating that there is abuse when there is an “alteration of the obligations contained in the estimate without the consumer's agreement” Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 55). Protection, therefore, requires more than formal exposure of content: it requires a real possibility of verifying relevant changes and their effects before they produce material subjection of the consumer. The same reasoning is reinforced when the author associates abusiveness with the element of surprise and the lack of understanding of patrimonial impacts, showing that an apparently transparent relationship can still be unlawful if it reserves unexpected conditions for the consumer, since surprise prevents effective control over the scope of the commitment assumed Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 120; p. 142). ↩
  5. Facilitating the consumer's defense and evidentiary protection, in the CDC, do not constitute mere procedural benevolence, but structural instruments of effectiveness of the microsystem itself. The Manual is clear in observing that the right provided for in Article 6, item VIII, although aimed at facilitating the consumer's procedural defense, is not of a merely procedural nature: it is a substantive right aimed at effective protection and reparation of the damages suffered Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 146]). The consequence is decisive: when the informational or technical structure of the supply hinders the reconstruction of the relevant facts, the system already has its own mechanisms to redistribute the evidentiary weight and prevent the supplier's opacity from turning into practical immunity. The Manual also highlights that the spirit of the CDC is precisely to facilitate the defense of the consumer's rights, and not to impose on the vulnerable and lay party the proof of elements that are part of the supplier's professional risk Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 147]). In reinforcement, the work recalls that the reversal of the burden of proof functions as an indispensable instrument of distributive justice and of redistribution of the risks inherent in consumer society Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 276]). ↩
  6. Open auditability can be converted into dogmatic language recognizable by the CDC from the articulation between a duty of real clarification, the duty of adequate information, the supplier's burden of proof and the control of abusiveness. The duty to inform represents a duty to enlighten the consumer, and not a mere formal transfer of content. Adequate information about the relevant elements of the performance serves as a criterion for assessing the regular exercise of the supplier's contractual freedom; its breach may reveal abusiveness, compromise the quality of consent and even attract Article 46 of the CDC. Demonstrating that this duty was effectively fulfilled constitutes an evidentiary burden of the supplier itself Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 412). Where there is greater risk without sufficiently conspicuous information, an informational defect is configured, and the absence of adequate clarification prevents the consumer from taking the necessary precautions. Even when the supplier intends to exclude its liability, it will be for it to demonstrate the non-existence of the defect, by express legal distribution of the burden of proof Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 488). ↩
  7. The requirement of open auditability is not confused with the integral opening of source code nor with the suppression of the technical secrecy of software. The legal order already knows forms of institutional scrutiny compatible with the preservation of the informational asset and of the secrets of the infrastructure. The legal discipline itself provides for judicial secrecy, which restricts third-party access to sensitive information, such as source code, and prevents its use by the opposing party for purposes foreign to the litigation Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 1940). The legal limitation allows the use of information and the translation of the program to the extent technically indispensable to the user's needs, for the exclusive use of whoever carried out the act, regardless of the right holder's authorization, precisely to enable integration between systems Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 1957). Likewise, administrative formalities cannot go beyond the conditions under which the object of the registration may remain secret Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 1963). ↩
  8. The protection of industrial secrets is not confused with an absolute veto on external scrutiny of legally relevant effects of the supply. The generic invocation of secrecy is not enough to block institutional control when the very claim for protection depends on minimally controllable verification of the protected object. For the case to be submitted to judicial control, “it is necessary to unveil and identify which secret would have been disclosed”, it not being enough to claim, broadly, that it is a secret related to the company's area of activity, because such a sweeping assertion does not allow effective judicial control of the controversy Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 392). The possibility of defense and judicial analysis are compromised if it is not known for certain what has already been made public and what would remain insusceptible of knowledge by third parties Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 392). Industrial secrecy prevents unrestricted exposure, but does not exclude minimum requirements of institutionally relevant reconstruction regarding alterations, criteria or impacts whose legal relevance demands external control. ↩
  9. The legal protection of the computer program does not turn it into a black box immune to any institutional scrutiny of the legally relevant effects of its operation. A program claimed in necessary connection with a specific system can be submitted to examination of novelty and inventive step as a product, provided a specific technical effect is identified, it being precisely the “mode and purpose of this operation” that is the object of the corresponding analysis Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 1999). The protection of software does not exclude institutional examination of its operational effects when this proves relevant for the application of the applicable legal regime. The Brazilian constitutional system leans toward the peculiarity of the computer program by reconciling the demands of social function with its value or purpose, precisely because what is protected is not only abstract communication, but “the practical action” of the program Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 1899). In dealing with registration, the competent agency, upon verifying breach of a rule whose enforcement is not its responsibility, must at least prompt the action of the competent body to restore the legal balance Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 1963). ↩
  10. In relationships of continuous execution, preserving the consumer's trust requires informational and relational conduct compatible with the very long-lasting structure of the bond. The initial performance is not enough, nor is the simple formal maintenance of the contract; the execution must unfold in a cooperative, adaptive and fair way, so as not to frustrate the legitimate expectations that justified the consumer's adhesion to and permanence in the relationship. The long-term contract “carries within it expectations other than those of contracts of immediate execution”, since it is based “more on trust, on repeated interaction, on the maintenance of economic potential and of the quality of services”, which is why “the capacity for adaptation, for cooperation between contracting parties, for continuation of the contractual relationship” is essential Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 106). The theory of the relational contract contributes to “a new understanding of the trust awakened by the activity of suppliers” and to the acceptance of constant readaptation of long-term relationships “so as not to frustrate the legitimate expectations of the parties” Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 101). ↩
  11. The CDC's mechanisms of effectiveness are already compatible with the control of relevant alterations of the performance, including through specific relief and instruments of disclosure and production of evidence. Specific performance of the obligation prevails precisely to prevent the supplier from turning default into an advantageous economic calculation, converting injuries into losses and damages as an ordinary cost of business. For this reason, conversion into compensation should only occur at the consumer's option or when specific relief or the equivalent practical result is impossible Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 898). This design is articulated with antecedent measures aimed at the early production of evidence, notably disclosure actions, especially in disputes in which the contractual instrument was not delivered to the consumer or in which the effective content of the bond needs to be reconstructed Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 903). There is no serious control of relevant mutations of the performance without access to the versions of the contract, to the execution records and to the elements that allow the concretely performed supply to be reconstructed. ↩
  12. The protection of strategic business information coexists with limited forms of technical reconstruction when this proves indispensable to legally recognized purposes. The protection of secrets, software or other strategic elements does not eliminate legitimate spaces of functionally justified verifiability. “Decompilation is free in Brazilian law”, whenever indispensable to the purposes of integration Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 172). Informational exclusivity does not function as an absolute block on the knowledge functionally necessary to allow compatibility, operational continuity or interaction between systems. Expert analysis in controversies over trade secrets includes investigation into possible independent development, reverse engineering or another distinct source as causes apt to explain the disputed elements Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 394). Various legal systems make exceptions to exclusivity to prevent it from being used as a barrier to interconnection, making the powers of the exclusive right unenforceable against specific uses necessary for the fitting and articulated operation of systems Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 173). ↩
