7.2 On the typical platform models and the incidence of the IOC

See this section in the PDF

This section in the layers of the site

  • Platforms · The sequence of each family

p. 755

The stabilization of the regime of the Immaterial Object of Consumption makes it possible to advance to a plane of typological application in which the usefulness of the dogmatic construction can be tested in distinct ecosystems, without this implying conceptual dilution, sectoral drift or rhetorical expansion of the concept. The point, here, is not to gather digital examples under a single heading by mere technological affinity, nor to suggest that every contemporary platform reproduces, with identical intensity, the same form of vulnerability, opacity or capture of enjoyment. What is sought is to verify whether the regime constructed retains a recognizable capacity for incidence when projected onto typical models of continuous digital supply whose organization, although varied in appearance, presents relevant functional equivalences as to the governance of the experience, the administration of intelligibility and the asymmetric distribution of the costs of evidence, permanence and contestation.

The application, therefore, does not rest on superficial similarity between sectors, nor on technological identity between services. The criterion of approximation is structural. What matters is to identify families of platform in which diverse arrangements of private governance administer, by their own means, analogous problems of enjoyment, opacity, verifiability and restoration. The unit of examination lies not in the economic branch nor in the marketing label of the service, but in the predominant mechanism by which the user's experience is organized, modulated, reconfigured or made contestable. It is this mechanism, and not the name of the sector, that provides the basis of comparability and allows the incidence of the IOC to operate as a transposable grammar of reading and decision.

This methodological option fulfills an important dogmatic function. It prevents the typology from turning into a catalog of digital markets, an impressionistic survey of cases or a sociological taxonomy of platforms. The legal interest does not lie in describing sectors out of classificatory curiosity, but in recognizing recurring regimes of continuous digital supply in which enjoyment is governed by architecture, by curation, by administered visibility, by functional opacity, by ordinary mutability or by selective p. 756 mechanisms of sanction and prioritization. By shifting the focus from the sector to the governance mechanism, the analysis preserves methodological unity and prevents the application from depending on the interpreter's cultural familiarity with the environment examined. What matters is not whether the platform presents itself as a cognitive tool, market infrastructure, productive environment or reputation system, but how it organizes access, modulates expectations, distributes risks and conditions the possibility of institutional reconstruction of the conflict.

To maintain this comparability, the exposition will follow a constant sequence. In each typical model, it starts from the structural governance mechanism that organizes enjoyment and produces the characteristic friction of the environment. Next, that mechanism is traced back to the central vulnerability it activates, as well as to the way in which promises, expectations and losses are manufactured, administered or degraded over the course of the relationship. In the following stage, the adequate evidence and the evidentiary requirements compatible with the specific form of the conflict are set, including the blind spots that usually hinder its institutional translation. Only then is the possible dogmatic consequence indicated, always by recognizable derivation from the operative grid previously consolidated.

With this, the applied typology ceases to be an exemplifying exercise and comes to function as a test of transposability. What one seeks to demonstrate is that the IOC regime maintains internal coherence when confronted with distinct ecosystems, precisely because its incidence does not depend on the appearance of the environment, but on the structure of private power that organizes it. If the construction is dogmatically consistent, it must be able to recognize, in diverse families of platform, equivalent problems of governed enjoyment, institutionally relevant opacity, asymmetry of meaning and evidentiary asymmetry, without losing precision and without turning into a totalizing metaphor. The value of the typology lies precisely there. It verifies whether the grammar is robust enough to operate outside the laboratory in which it was densified, preserving decisional usefulness and argumentative discipline.

This passage also makes it possible to show that the contemporary mutation of supply is not reduced to the digitization of media or the technical mediation of already known interactions. In many of these environments, the platform comes to govern not only the delivery of utilities, but the very conditions under which those utilities can be perceived, compared, contested, restored or socially stabilized as part of the consumer's experience. That is why the transposition of the regime should not be read as a classificatory adventure. It is, rather, a matter of gauging to what extent the logic of governed enjoyment, open auditability, functional equivalence, semiotic vulnerability and abusive p. 757 teleology remains apt to organize the institutional reading of conflicts that, although distinct in appearance, partake of the same structural rationality of supply.

What will follow, therefore, is not a sequence of sectors, but a succession of typical models of incidence. Each of them corresponds to a recurring form of governance of enjoyment on contemporary platforms. Each of them makes it possible to observe how the same grid of controllability behaves in the face of specific problems of promise, trust, opacity, mutability, sanction, reputation, intermediation or administration of expectation. And each of them contributes to showing that the Immaterial Object of Consumption is not a closed sectoral category, but a dogmatic instrument apt to translate, in a legally controllable key, the multiplication of private ecosystems that organize the user's experience as a continuous regime of supply.
