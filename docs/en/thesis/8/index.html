Epigraph

Pitty, Temporal (2003). What it announces · Read the epigraph in the PDF.

p. 791

The journey undertaken in this work began with the finding that there is a type of consumption in which the subject imagines being on his own ground, but moves only within a floor that is granted, revocable and reconfigurable. This was not, from the outset, an ornamental figure, but an institutional description. The access, the permanence and the meaning of what was acquired become dependent on continuous administration that does not present itself as management, but as technical normality. It is precisely this camouflage that makes the conflict hard to see, hard to say and, therefore, hard to protect against.

If Law is the language of the earthly gods, the central task does not lie in demonizing said deities, nor in pretending that they do not exist. The question is, from the outset, centered on the need to establish a translation. On the need to convert a power that sells itself as nature into a form that is controllable, contestable and attributable, without which the will of the strongest once again operates as normality by sheer capacity for imposition. The ethics of this study is not born of the desire to punish technology for being technology. It is born of the commitment to prevent a decision organized by architecture from being demoted to chance, the rules of the game or technical necessity.

That is why the image of Dante Alighieri's Inferno was of interest from the start. His work exposes the degradation of the human bond when intelligence ceases to be an instrument of understanding and comes to operate as a ruse, producing an appearance of legitimacy in order to violate trust. Frictions are neutralized as technique, opacity becomes an element of strategy and the consumer is pushed to negotiate with the virtual world created by the supplier itself to satisfy its own interests.

To point a way forward, however, does not mean seeking a heroic gesture of demolition, as if it were desirable to put out the sun to solve the problem of the shadow. To point a way forward means proposing criteria capable of taming the asymmetry without destroying the infrastructure.

Here, the image of the Egyptian deities Isis and Ra818 is able to compose the decisive ethical perspective. In the myth, the Sun-God does not cease to be the sun because someone hates him, nor does he lose p. 792 his centrality through a mere desire for confrontation. What happens is subtler and much more important. Ra reigns over all, but is not invulnerable. His vulnerability appears in a minimal detail, a remnant of himself, an almost negligible trace of his own body. At the same time, Isis does not wish to annihilate the sun. It is not in her interest to replace the order of the world with one centered on herself by mere whim. Her interest is to force the sun to recognize that it is not absolute. To share with her part of his power.

Isis produces the serpent from the substance of Ra himself and, with that, turns the excess of power into an instrument for manifesting its limit. The venom does not destroy the Sun as a principle of the world. In exchange for the cure, Isis demands only that Ra reveal to her the name he keeps for himself. The name that does not circulate, that is not confused with titles, images or apparent manifestations of his grandeur. It is here that the analogy takes concrete form. The true name, in the myth, is neither a verbal ornament nor an irrelevant mystical secret. It is a “magic word”, almost like a command password. It is the point at which power and vulnerability cease to be pure force and enter the field of intelligibility. Isis does not long to know his name in order to humiliate him. She demands his name in order to transmute his power, establishing the control that then allows her to offer the cure. Once named, power ceases to operate as pure natural presence and becomes invocable, delimitable and, therefore, obliged to answer for its acts.

What Consumer Law lacks in the face of the Lex Plataformae is not moral indignation, nor a rhetorical repertoire of denunciation. It lacks the vocabulary to render demonstrable the decisions that today pass for the mere functioning of the system. The language to reconstruct a pattern and not merely react to an episode. It lacks a method to separate legitimate alteration from abusive degradation, ordinary mutability from normalized extraction, management of the environment from an exploitative program inscribed in the architecture. That is why the category of the Immaterial Object of Consumption and the criteria of open auditability and functional value equivalence do not appear, in the end, as dogmatic props. The concepts operate as a civilized version of the snake of the myth. They are the minimal mechanism that obliges power and, consequently, whoever wields it, to assume its own responsibilities.

To name is not to poeticize. To name is to move power from a regime of self-evidence to a regime of contestability. As long as the platform can say that it merely functions, merely updates, merely executes, merely applies rules, the conflict will remain trapped in the idiom of the inevitable. When, however, the regime of supply is reconstructed, when it is shown that access, permanence, visibility, reputation, intelligibility and enjoyment are organized by p. 793 design and enforcement, what before seemed like weather once again seems like a decision. And what once again seems like a decision can be controlled. The name, here, is not the academic title of the thing. It is the condition of its limitation.

It is in this sense that the proposed protection is organized as an ethics of containment, not as an ethics of demolition. The aim is not to extinguish platforms, nor to romanticize a world without technique, something intellectually limiting and institutionally useless. The aim is to protect the functional integrity of the object acquired and the symbolic integrity of the consumer, recognizing that, under continuous consumption, value is not only technical utility, but place, sign, belonging and the possibility of social participation mediated by private infrastructure. When private power administers permanence, reputation, access and meaning, it already operates as practical normativity. The least the constitutional order can demand is that this power be translated into controllable criteria.

Law is not asked to put out the sun. It is asked to oblige it to say its name. It is asked that the platform cease to inhabit the comfortable zone of nature and accept the more demanding place of institutional responsibility. It is asked that what today presents itself as technical normality may once again be seen as choice, as program and, when necessary, as abuse. Only then will it be possible to restore degraded enjoyments, contest opaque decisions and rebuild trust without accepting that power organized by architecture present itself as a mere inevitable backdrop. To name, in the end, is not to adorn the world with words. It is to make even the sun obey, so that civilization may take back for itself the power to decide on the limits of its power.

Under such a horizon, the central legal question of this research consisted in verifying to what extent the normative structure of the Consumer Protection Code offers adequate protection to consumers inserted in continuous digital ecosystems, in which the enjoyment of what was acquired depends on stable private governance, ordinary mutability and opaque decisions incorporated into the very architecture of the supply.

Although historically read in light of the centrality of corporeal goods and discrete performances, Brazilian consumer law has settled into a robust instrument capable of offering adequate protection to consumers inserted in continuous digital ecosystems, even those in which value is less technical and more functional, economic, social or symbolic. These goods are concentrated in an immaterial ontology that, by its nature, depends on architecture, private curation, ordinary mutability and opaque regimes of decision.

The guiding hypothesis, confirmed in the end, was that the difficulty did not lie in an absence of normative basis, but in the persistence of an ontologically impoverished reading p. 794 of the CDC itself. The Brazilian legal order already contains enough openness to accommodate the protection of these goods, provided that Art. 3, §1, is reread with a dogmatic density compatible with the contemporary informational economy. What was lacking was not a new legislative microsystem for the digital, but a conceptual reconstruction capable of removing the immaterial good from the residual position in which it was kept and bringing it back to the center of the protective regime. It is at this point that the Immaterial Object of Consumption presents itself as a category apt to name, qualify and make attributable conflicts that today tend to appear as technical inevitability.

The IOC makes it possible to show that, in platform markets, the immaterial product is not exhausted in content, access or utility considered in the abstract. It is constituted as enjoyment that is functionally maintained, governed and reconfigurable, dependent on technical infrastructure, private rules of circulation, visibility criteria, sanction mechanisms and forms of permanence in the ecosystem. Thus, the axis of analysis shifts from a static ontology of the good to a functional ontology of enjoyment. Instead of asking only what the object is in the abstract, it is now possible to ask under what conditions it remains usable, intelligible, recognizable and substantially equivalent to what justified the acquisition and sustained the consumer's legitimate expectation.

The reconstruction requires recognizing that, in continuous digital ecosystems, the relevant asymmetry does not fall only on the quantity of information available or on an abstract technical difficulty of understanding. It falls on the private means of producing meaning, expectation, comparability, trust and evidence. It was in this context that semiotic vulnerability proved a more precise category than the broad and diffuse vocabulary of “digital vulnerability”. The consumer is not vulnerable only because he knows less about the technology. He is vulnerable because the platform governs the way the object appears, the way loss is perceived, the language by which frustration is explained and the very limits of what can be reconstructed as a legally cognizable conflict.

It is shown that the injury, in these environments, frequently ceases to take the form of an isolated event and comes to operate as a program. Hence the formulation of abusive teleology as a category of unlawful act of architecture. Its meaning does not lie in generically moralizing design, nor in turning all mutability into suspicion. Its meaning lies in identifying situations in which opacity, friction, manufactured urgency, selective reconfiguration, asymmetry of reversal and normalization of small losses cease to be episodic occurrences and come to compose a method of exploitation incorporated into the very regime of supply. When this occurs, technique can no longer be treated as a neutral background. It appears as legally attributable conduct.

p. 795 So that this attribution does not dissolve into generic criticism of the digital, the central standards of control are then proposed: open auditability and functional value equivalence. Open auditability is conceived to overcome the false alternative between absolute opacity and total transparency. Its core lies in asserting that Law need not demand full disclosure of code, proprietary databases or internal systems in order to exercise legitimate control over the supply. What it needs, rather, is sufficient institutional intelligibility about what relevantly interferes with enjoyment, sanction, visibility, access, the distribution of opportunities and the reconstruction of the conflict. Corporate opacity, however economically understandable it may be, cannot function as an immunity clause against the legal verifiability of what affects the consumer.

Functional value equivalence, in turn, is formulated to prevent the ordinary mutability of digital ecosystems from becoming a safe conduct for the unilateral degradation of the acquired experience. The point here is not to freeze the environment or to demand absolute stability in technically dynamic realities. The point is to assert that the lawfulness of the alteration cannot be gauged only by the nominal subsistence of the object, by the formal maintenance of access or by the abstract persistence of some residual utility. What matters is the substantial preservation of the function that legitimized the acquisition and structured the consumer's expectation. An object may continue to exist and, even so, no longer be, in a legally relevant sense, what was acquired. It is precisely to name this type of loss that functional value equivalence becomes decisive.

These two standards play a central role in the proposed regime because they supply a criterion for what would otherwise oscillate between technical reverence and intuitive indignation. It is they that make it possible to distinguish legitimate alteration from abusive degradation, a compatible update from an opportunistic reconfiguration, tolerable obscurity from immunizing opacity. Without open auditability, the critique of opacity risks becoming rhetoric without a knowable object. Without functional value equivalence, the critique of mutability risks becoming naive nostalgia for an impossible stability. With both, the thesis offers a yardstick of controllability capable of bringing the conflict back to the terrain of demonstration, attribution and institutional response.

The research also showed that this reconstruction does not require a break with intellectual property, but rather its reinscription in a constitutionally oriented key. In platform ecosystems, copyright, trademarks, software and other forms of exclusivity do not operate only as titles of appropriation over immaterial creations. They also function as legal technologies for organizing access, the sign, the interface and the very experience of enjoyment. For this reason, they cannot be read as a safe conduct for opportunistic p. 796 mutability, structural precarization of the experience or absolute blocking of institutional controllability. The social function of property and of intellectual property requires that the exercise of these private positions remain compatible with duties of loyalty, verifiable stability, sufficient transparency, restoration and limitation of abuse in continuous consumer relationships.

The consequence of this constitutional reinscription of intellectual property becomes even clearer when one observes cases of termination of activities, discontinuation of services, abandonment of product lines or definitive shutdown of servers. As central components of contemporary life migrate to private digital ecosystems, the formal precariousness of use licenses can no longer be read as automatic authorization for the complete sacrifice of immaterial objects of consumption already incorporated into the existential, economic, relational and symbolic trajectory of the consumer. Photographs, histories, libraries, contact networks, progressions, projected identities, reputational positions and long-term investments cannot be legally treated as residues without density merely because they are technically housed in someone else's infrastructure.

The social function of property and of intellectual property prevents private exclusivity from operating as a title sufficient to legitimize the unilateral erasure of entire digital worlds under the pretext of business freedom, economic convenience or strategic reorientation of the supplier. The right to exploit, terminate or reorganize an activity is not to be confused with a supposed right to annihilate, without duties of transition, preservation or transfer, the functional enjoyment already stabilized in the consumer's sphere.

In continuous consumer relationships, there is no intellectual property right that sustains, by itself, the complete extinction of the infrastructure with the consequent complete sacrifice of the IOCs. If the termination of the service is unavoidable, the constitutional order requires that it be reconciled, as far as technically and legally possible, with solutions of continuity, portability, functional restoration, assignment of the operation or other measures suitable to prevent private architecture from becoming a lawful instrument of patrimonial and existential devastation of the consumer.

The limit case, at this point, is not only the opportunistic platform that alters rules, degrades functionalities or intensifies value extraction while it remains operating. It is also the platform that fails. When the functional value equivalence, the biographical continuity, the patrimonial memory and the accumulated sociability of thousands or millions of consumers depend on the permanence of servers, accounts, libraries, databases, authentication systems and execution environments, the technical or economic failure of the p. 797 supplier may produce an even more radical form of loss. It is not only a matter of abusive alteration of the digital world, but of its possible erasure as an infrastructure of enjoyment. The question that emerges from this is whether Law will be able to keep treating business termination as a simple exercise of economic freedom when it results in the burial of collections, identities, reputations, histories and immaterial objects of consumption incorporated into consumers' practical lives.

It is on the remedial plane, however, that the proposed reconstruction reveals one of its most important consequences. If the injured bond is one of governed enjoyment, the legal response cannot be automatically reduced to the pricing of the harm. In IOC ecosystems, many injuries are not exhausted in the loss of a simple economic equivalent, because they fall on access, permanence, history, reputation, relational position, intelligibility of the experience and functional continuity of what was acquired. Treating every injury of this type as susceptible of merely pecuniary compensation means, in many cases, accepting that the supplier may unilaterally degrade, suppress or reconfigure enjoyment and then merely internalize the monetary cost of that conduct as an ordinary price of doing business. This is not adequate protection. It is automatic monetization of abuse.

For this reason, it is argued that the regime of remedies applicable to IOCs must be reorganized on the basis of a clear methodological priority. Whenever technically and legally possible, protection must aim at enjoyment, and not only at its later monetary translation. This means favoring functional restoration, reestablishment of access, reversal of relevant degradations, regime correction, cessation of opaque or exploitative practices and the imposition of minimum standards of verifiability on alterations that substantially affect the object acquired. Pecuniary compensation remains necessary in many cases, but it cannot occupy, by automatism, the position of sovereign remedy. When the injury is architectural, the response cannot be limited to pricing its effects; it must reach, as far as possible, the architecture that produced them.

This consequence is particularly important because it prevents Consumer Law from becoming a mere elegant administrator of normalized private losses. In platform markets, the automatic conversion of the conflict into money frequently keeps intact the structure that generated it. The consumer receives, at most, a late and imperfect equivalent, while the regime of supply keeps operating in the same way, ready to reproduce the same degradation at scale. By insisting on functional restoration and regime correction, the thesis seeks to put protection back in its proper place: not as a simple p. 798 later compensatory calculation, but as a mechanism of institutional containment of recurring forms of exploitation inscribed in the very architecture of the experience.

The illustrative references mobilized throughout the thesis were not used as exhaustive sociological proof, nor as the basis for large-scale empirical generalizations, but as sufficient anchoring to bring out recurring mechanisms of governance by architecture, structural opacity, administration of trust, ordinary mutability and private production of the conditions of perception of the conflict. The usefulness of this material lay in showing that, in connected interactive entertainment environments, the value consumed may reside not only in a virtual item taken in isolation, but in access, progression, visibility, belonging, time, continuity of trajectory and symbolic position in the ecosystem.

There is, however, an additional consequence of this model which, although it was not explored over the course of the work with the centrality its gravity would deserve, imposes itself at least as an inescapable theoretical disquiet at the end of the journey. In continuous digital ecosystems, above all in sectoral models analogous to the one studied, the platform not only governs the enjoyment of the object acquired, but may progressively learn to decipher the very subject who enjoys it. What makes the problem singular is not a mere quantitative accumulation of data, but the qualitative density of the inferences that become possible when the user's experience is entirely mediated by proprietary infrastructure, by constant tracking systems and by interaction regimes designed to convert conduct into economic intelligibility.

In environments composed of dozens or hundreds819 of diverse types and styles of risk and aggressiveness, of repeated patterns of choice, of progression routines, of responses to frustration and reward, of microdecisions made under pressure, of click cadences, of hesitations, insistences, deviations and recurrences, what is formed is not merely a database of declared preferences or registration identifiers. What is progressively formed is a private archive of dispositions, tendencies, propensities and behavioral structures whose depth comfortably exceeds the classical grammar of personal data traditionally addressed in the legal debate.

The point deserves special care because, in this scenario, the platform ceases to know the consumer only as an account holder, purchaser of content or recipient of p. 799 targeted advertising. It comes to know him as a pattern. As predictability. As susceptibility. As a probable architecture of response to specific stimuli, calibrated frictions, intermittent rewards, visibility regimes and designs of scarcity. The asymmetry, then, is no longer limited to the informational plane in the current sense, nor is it exhausted in the supplier's technical superiority as to the internal functioning of the system. It shifts to a more delicate plane, in which the supplier comes to hold means of psycho-functional reconstruction of the user that the user himself does not possess about himself with an equivalent degree of operative precision. In structural terms, the platform not only sells, administers, reconfigures, rewards and sanctions. It may also observe, model, test, learn and feed back into the experience on the basis of a progressively refined knowledge of how that subject tends to act, insist, give in, become frustrated, persevere, consume and remain.

It is at this point that the hypothesis becomes even more disquieting. The relevant legal result of continuous digital consumption may consist not only in the formation of lasting bonds of functional, symbolic and economic dependence, but also in the repeated extraction of traces dense enough to map, with growing accuracy, psychological and dispositional profiles of high strategic value. In such a context, the platform no longer appears only as a supplier of Immaterial Objects of Consumption, nor only as a private administrator of persistent digital worlds. It appears, potentially, as a business structure capable of converting attention, gesture, preference, repetition, performance, decision-making style and pattern of interaction into economic intelligence about the practical interiority of its users.

The gravity of the problem lies in the fact that this knowledge is not gathered at the margins of the consumed experience, but produced within it, as an effect of the very architecture that organizes enjoyment, distributes visibility, doses reward, imposes sanction and stabilizes permanence. Economic exploitation thus ceases to fall only on what the consumer buys or uses and comes to touch, in an increasingly sophisticated way, the manner in which he is constituted as a legible subject before the platform itself.

This concern reinforces the central conclusion reached so far. If the category of goods comprised under the Immaterial Object of Consumption required specific protection because enjoyment was subjected to structural opacity, ordinary mutability and continuous private governance, this requirement intensifies when one perceives that the same ecosystems function, at the same time, as permanent laboratories of behavioral deciphering.

Semiotic vulnerability, from this angle, reveals an even deeper reach than initially formulated. It is not only a matter of the consumer being exposed to p. 800 private regimes of production of meaning, expectation, trust and intelligibility of the conflict. It is also a matter of recognizing that these same regimes can operate as devices for extracting psychic knowledge about the consumer himself, expanding on an unprecedented scale the disproportion between who is read and who reads, between who is interpreted and who interprets, between who supplies traces of himself and who converts them into strategic power. The challenge that announces itself to Consumer Law, therefore, is not only that of limiting abusive mutations of enjoyment, but that of facing, in future development, the possibility that continuous platform consumption becomes a private technique for producing intensive subjective legibility, with consequences still insufficiently addressed for autonomy, for practical freedom and for the very integrity of personality in digitally governed environments.

This point helps explain why the conflict, in these environments, so often escapes the traditional categories of adjudication. The justice system frequently intuits that losses relating to accounts, digital goods, usage history and position in the environment exceed the immediate patrimonial dimension, but lacks a dogmatic grammar stable enough to turn this perception into rigorous reasoning. Hence the oscillation between two equally unsatisfactory reductions. Either the conflict is demoted to a low-density contractual annoyance, or it is perceived as intuitively serious without anyone being able to say precisely, in controllable legal language, what exactly was injured and why. One of the most relevant gains of this thesis lies in reducing this translation cost, providing categories apt to bring these conflicts back inside the CDC without inflating the system or abandoning it in favor of theories external to positive law.

To this extent, the main contribution of the research lies not in simply updating Consumer Law to keep up with technology, but in restoring institutional controllability to situations in which private power presents itself as nature, although it operates, in truth, as organized decision. The regime proposes to articulate coherently the qualification of the immaterial product by the Immaterial Object of Consumption, the location of the relevant asymmetry by semiotic vulnerability, the reading of governed expectation by hypercuratorial anticipation, the identification of the unlawful act by abusive teleology, the control of alterations by open auditability and functional value equivalence, the containment of exclusivity by the social function of property and of intellectual property and the reorganization of remedies around functional restoration and regime correction. With this, the aim is not to offer a total theory of platforms, but to prevent conflicts structurally produced by them p. 801 from continuing to be treated as the simple natural price of inhabiting private systems supposedly impregnable to legal language.

At the same time, the aim is not to offer a total theory of digital consumption, nor to map exhaustively the plurality of platform ecosystems that today organize work, cultural circulation, sociability, economic intermediation and everyday experience. The focus remains concentrated on the theoretical-dogmatic reconstruction of the regime applicable to continuous immaterial goods in consumer relationships, having as its privileged field of intelligibility the environment of connected interactive entertainment. Nor is the aim to replace broad empirical research with illustrative exemplification, or to exhaust the institutional operationalization of the proposed standards. Open auditability and functional value equivalence will require, in the future, evidentiary maturation, refinement of expert examination and the development of decision-making protocols more sensitive to the structural opacity of platform conflicts.

The challenge posed by digital platforms lies in recognizing that, at the center of the contemporary informational economy, there are immaterial objects of consumption whose protection requires taking seriously the governed experience of the consumer in its functional, semiotic and symbolic dimension. The regime of the Immaterial Object of Consumption, as formulated here, aims to be an internal, structural and constitutionally oriented reconstruction of the CDC, apt to bring back into the field of responsibility what today still hides under the indifferent appearance of the mere functioning of the system.

The images mobilized throughout the thesis help condense, in the end, its ethical core. On one side, the problem worsens when intelligence, architecture and language cease to serve understanding and come to operate as a ruse that stabilizes the violation of trust; on the other, the adequate legal response does not consist in destroying the infrastructure, but in stripping it of the pretension of becoming unreachable or absolute, obliging private power to let itself be named, proven and limited. In an institutional key, this is exactly what Consumer Law lacks in the face of the Lex Plataformae: not empty indignation, but vocabulary and criteria capable of converting naturalized force into contestable and accountable power.

Such is the ethical and dogmatic core proposed here. What presents itself as the simple functioning of the system can and must be reconstructed as choice, as program and, when necessary, as abuse. The consumer, in continuous digital ecosystems, cannot remain reduced to the condition of a tolerated guest in someone else's world, subjected to unilateral reconfigurations without a language of contestation.

p. 802 The Brazilian constitutional order does not require the elimination of the private infrastructures that organize contemporary experience; it requires, rather, that the power inscribed in them let itself be seen, proven, limited and, when necessary, corrected. In the end, the granted, revocable and reconfigurable floor that served as the starting point can no longer pretend to be nature. Once named, it comes to belong also to the field of responsibility, evidence, contestation, restoration and limit. For Law, force ceases to suffice unto itself and begins, at last, to answer to the name it bears.
