6.2.4.1.1 On the critical review of recent literature

p. 590

The critical review of recent literature on “digital vulnerability” performs, here, a strictly instrumental function. It is not intended to open an autonomous state of the art nor to dispute bibliographic prominence with the central argument. The aim is to show that the competing vocabulary, although relevant and academically serious, does not stabilize with sufficient sharpness the legally relevant mechanism of the asymmetry at issue, which is why it works better as a scenario label than as a decision criterion. In dogmatics, this difference matters. Concepts are worth not only for their capacity to describe a plausible environment, but for their aptitude to organize the passage from fact to normative consequence, cutting out the p. 591 legally relevant aspect of the conflict and making it treatable in a key of attribution, evidence and remedy Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 108; p. 247])666.

This is the first criterion for reading the selected corpus. The question is not whether recent literature correctly perceived that there is an aggravation of consumer vulnerability in digitally mediated environments. To a large extent, it did. The question is whether the category mobilized converts this diagnosis into a legal form stable enough to guide normative application. Concepts, in dogmatic discourse, need to function as operational theoretical abbreviations, linking factual hypothesis and legal effect. When they remain too broad, they manage to condense a legitimate concern, but lose precision for organizing the factual basis, the required evidence and the corresponding consequence Jr.(Ferraz Jr., 2003, [p. 92; p. 144; p. 246; p. 310])667.

The review was therefore conducted with a controlled cut. Recent Brazilian academic works were considered that employ “digital vulnerability” or very close expressions, in a context of consumption, platforms, continuous digital services or technologically mediated ecosystems, provided that the category was used as an explanatory element of the argument and not merely as a lateral mention. The cut does not aim at exhaustiveness. It aims at minimum verifiability. Only texts in which the expression triggers some normative consequence, however elementary, such as a duty of information, liability, p. 592 abusiveness, redistribution of the burden of proof or expanded protective relief, enter the corpus. The requirement is compatible with the very idea that legal research cannot stop at the descriptive understanding of the phenomenon, but needs to convert this diagnosis into normation, or at least into a legal form apt to guide conduct and decision Reale(Reale, 2001, p. 304; p. 173)668.

The reading matrix applied to the corpus was deliberately simple. In each work, it was tested whether “digital vulnerability” named only the scenario in which the problem arises or whether it effectively identified the legally relevant mechanism of the asymmetry. From there, four points were observed: whether there was a definition or only an invocation; what concrete asymmetry was described; whether the text indicated some minimally reconstructible evidentiary standard; and what normative structure or legal consequence was triggered. The criterion is important because it prevents impressionistic criticism. The objection is not that the term is new, attractive or too diffuse for the interpreter's taste. The objection is that, often, the label does not deliver enough normative form to convert the phenomenon into an operable category, that is, into a stable correspondence between apprehended social reality and a structure apt to guide the application of the CDC Reale(Reale, 2001, p. 174; p. 175; p. 187)669.

The first result of the survey is that recent literature correctly perceived the intensification of the consumer's fragility in platform environments. This appears sharply in more robust formulations, which link so-called digital vulnerability to the p. 593 monetization of data, the centrality of attention, behavioral advertising, dark patterns and decision-making opacity distributed along the consumer journey Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 230; p. 15; p. 143-144)670. It also appears in works that describe the consumer's vulnerabilization on the basis of lengthy terms of use, data transfer, the appearance of gratuitousness, opaque language and decision-making acceleration on digital platforms Pinto(Furtado; Pinto, 2024, p. 13; p. 12; p. 21)671. The problem, therefore, is not an invention of the thesis. The field already sees that something important has changed.

p. 594 The second result, however, is more decisive. Even when the literature is sophisticated, the category “digital vulnerability” usually operates as an aggregator of very diverse phenomena. Under the same label appear, all at once, data protection, technical opacity, behavioral manipulation, profiling, deficient consent, dark patterns, hyperconnectivity, the monetization of attention and informational asymmetry. The formulation is strong as an environmental synthesis, but less precise as a mechanistic cut. The concept comes to gather an ecosystem of risks instead of stabilizing the specific gear of the legally relevant asymmetry. This explains why it works better to describe the problem and worse to guide its fine juridification.

p. 595 This deficit also appears in close competing formulations. The notion of “cybervulnerability” records, with good sensitivity, that cyberspace aggravates already known vulnerabilities and widens the power gap between consumer and supplier, including through factors such as insufficient literacy, informational asymmetry, data collection and manipulation of the will Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 86; p. 93; p. 97; p. 120; p. 83-84)672. There is real merit in this. But the category itself remains very attached to the technological scenario as its organizing principle, which makes it less sharp for isolating the specific legal mechanism of vulnerabilization it seeks to explain. The author even seeks juridification by way of the CDC and the Constitution, which shows a serious effort at normativity, but still coexists with a strong dependence on “cyberspace” as the structural axis of the formulation Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 93; p. 113; p. 119)673.

p. 596 Something similar happens with the “situational digital hypervulnerability” worked out in the field of electronic games. The literature identifies, with relevant precision, real mechanisms of aggravation of vulnerability on platforms, such as continuous data collection, dynamic architecture, adaptation of design to user behavior, captivity, situational monopoly, irregular rewards and immersion strategies Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 8; p. 23; p. 15-16; p. 11; p. 21)674. There is even some normative density, with protective consequences drawn for cases involving children, family and p. 597 market practices in digital games Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 22-23)675. Even so, the category remains very dependent on the public affected and on the specific context of application. It explains a set of situations well. It explains less well the general mechanism that needs to be stabilized in order to recognize the contemporary form of structural risk that manifests itself in the key of the factual vulnerability already recognized by the CDC.

The analysis of the vocabulary of “electronic” or “technological vulnerability” reinforces the same diagnosis. Such expressions are useful for naming real barriers created by the compulsory digitalization of services, channels and procedures, especially in access to justice and in the technical exclusion of certain groups Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 3; p. 2)676. They also reveal the proliferation of internal subdivisions, such as technical, informative, algorithmic, behavioral and related vulnerabilities, which proves the field's sensitivity to new asymmetries Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 4)677. But this fertility comes with classificatory instability. The p. 598 term helps to describe technological obstacles and institutional accessibility responses. But for reconstructing, in a consumer-law key, the violated duty, the factual basis, the required evidence and the appropriate remedy, its usefulness remains limited.

There is, further, an important contrast with the field of access to justice. The expression “digital vulnerability” circulates there to designate phenomena of technical exclusion, lack of access, skills deficits and barriers to insertion in digitalized environments, especially for more socially exposed groups Simões(Simões, 2024, p. 79; p. 83; p. 86)678. In another formulation, it appears as a general layer of vulneration associated with broad sociotechnical transformation, privacy, informational self-determination, citizenship and the overlaps between income, gender and race Simões(Simões, 2024, p. 69; p. 79-80)679. This confirms that the vocabulary is serious, current and relevant. But it also confirms its polysemy. In many uses, “digital vulnerability” names a structural condition of exclusion or exposure in a p. 599 technological environment, and not a more delimited legal mechanism of relational asymmetry within the CDC.

The contrast with the work of Ricardo Morishita Wada helps to make this point cleaner. The legally relevant problem lies not in the relationship taking place in a sophisticated technological universe, but in knowing whether there is functional opacity capable of compromising the consumer's understanding, choice and practical self-determination. Informational failure does not stem only from the absence of data; it may also arise when the information exists, but its form of presentation neutralizes its practical usefulness, reduces transparency, hinders comparison or compromises understanding Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 168; p. 193-194; p. 213-214)680. This yardstick is decisive because it shows what the competing vocabulary lacks in many cases. It is not enough to point out that vulneration occurs in the “digital”. It is necessary to indicate how the environment concretely produces opacity, noise, decision asymmetry and evidentiary asymmetry.

The table confirms a simple point. The literature reviewed is relevant and, in several cases, normatively ambitious. But the label “digital vulnerability” or its close equivalents works, most of the time, better as a synthesis of scenario than as an isolator of the legally central mechanism. Sometimes it produces a concrete protective consequence, such as advocacy of a stronger reversal of the burden of proof or expanded contractual control. Even so, that consequence is usually extracted en bloc, from the global perception of a hostile environment, and not from the more precise stabilization of the specific gear of asymmetry.

It is precisely from this that the refining criterion adopted is drawn. To operate dogmatically in the CDC, any category of vulnerability needs to satisfy at least four cumulative requirements: identify a mechanism, and not merely a context; convert that mechanism into a duty, unlawfulness or criterion of control; offer a minimally reconstructible evidentiary standard; and point to a typical consequence compatible with the structure of the protective system. When these elements do not appear, the category may remain useful as a diagnosis of the environment, but remains weak as a decision concept.

The adoption of “semiotic vulnerability” responds to this problem. It does not replace “digital vulnerability” out of mere vocabulary whim. It replaces a predominantly environmental category with one aimed at describing the mechanism. The relevant asymmetry comes to be located in the production and governance of meaning, that is, in the way the platform administers expectation, perception of value, perception of risk, comparability, intelligibility of the offer and conditions of contestation. With this, greater operability is gained. p. 601 Information ceases to be thought of as volume and comes to be thought of as decision-making utility and verifiability. Advertising ceases to be a mere promotional backdrop and becomes controllable by ambiguity and relevant omission. Evidence ceases to gravitate only around the consumer's narrative and comes to require the preservation of trails, verifiable transparency and rational redistribution of the burden of proof. And abusiveness may, when necessary, be reconstructed at the level of the very architecture of the supply. It is this operational gain, and not a dispute over labels, that justifies the terminological substitution adopted here.

  5. The most sophisticated recent literature on consumption in a digital environment has already perceived, with considerable density, that the consumer's contemporary vulnerability can no longer be thought of only through the classic schemes. This becomes evident when one takes as a starting point the simultaneous condition of the consumer who holds personal data as the recipient of marketing practices, the raw material for their development and the product traded among the agents of the system, added to the growing informational asymmetry and the intensification of abusive practices based on prediction products inserted into the consumer journey Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 230). This robustness widens when the picture is linked to technology as a structuring factor of relationships, to big data and big analytics, to the monetization of data, to the growth of informational flow and to the centrality of attention as a capturable economic resource Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 15). The critical value of this construction, for the present review, lies in showing that the competing vocabulary is neither superficial nor accidental. On the contrary, it is strong, current and academically serious. But it is precisely for that reason that its limit matters more: even in its most elaborate version, the category tends to function as a synthesis of a broad ecosystem of risks and practices, more apt to condense the problematic environment than to isolate, with maximum dogmatic precision, the central mechanism producing the legally relevant asymmetry. The notion of digital vulnerability acquires special explanatory force when linked to the idea of a structure of stimuli and controls that imperceptibly permeates the consumer journey. In this picture, digital advertising, behavioral advertising, dark patterns and decision-making opacity do not appear as isolated phenomena, but as elements of a broader architecture of influence over the consumer's choice process, in a scenario in which the sufficiency of personal data protection is investigated and the existence of a new and universal digital vulnerability is asserted, generating an equally universal state of hyposufficiency Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 6). The formulation is especially relevant because it shows the category's capacity to gather central problems of the contemporary digital market. This aggregating vocation becomes even more explicit when dark patterns and digital advertising are inserted into a broader context represented by the consumer journey itself, going beyond what is immediately visible both to the user and to the legal community Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 143-144). The point is valuable for the critical review because it reveals that the competing category is not limited to naming a specific risk, but organizes a comprehensive explanatory superstructure. And it is precisely there that its limit appears for the purposes of this thesis: the category works very well as an environmental synthesis of an ecosystem of risks, but less as an analytical instrument aimed at cutting out, in a mechanistic and attributable way, the specific gear of asymmetry that matters to the dogmatic reconstruction proposed here. The dogmatic operability of the competing category appears clearly when a concrete procedural consequence is drawn from it: the universalization of the consumer's hyposufficiency in a digital environment and, by derivation, of the reversal of the burden of proof. The idea is that, in the face of ever more sophisticated and imperceptible deceptive practices, what used to function as an individual mark of procedural weakness becomes a general trait of digital consumer relationships, so that there would be no just legal protection as long as the reversal of the burden of proof did not operate as the rule in this field Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 98). This is important because it shows that the category does not stay still on the sociological plane; it tries to produce a verifiable legal effect. This consequence is reaffirmed when it is maintained that the protection offered today by the CDC and the LGPD is insufficient in the face of the new bases of relationship in a digital environment, which is why digital vulnerability and the corresponding hyposufficiency should be affirmed in a universal key, with reversal of the burden of proof ope legis, and not merely ope judicis Palmeira(Palmeira, 2022, p. 234). The gain of this formulation is clear: it offers a concrete protective answer, above all on the evidentiary plane. The limit, however, appears in the same movement. The central legal consequence drawn from the category tends to be organized en bloc, through one large general effect, which confirms its strength as a comprehensive diagnosis, but also its more global and aggregating character. Instead of selectively cutting out the specific mechanism producing the asymmetry, the category functions as the foundation for a protective regime expanded en masse. ↩
  6. The recent circulation of the expression “consumer's digital vulnerability” reveals that the literature already clearly perceives the aggravation of the user's fragility in platform-mediated consumer relationships, especially when the business model is organized through lengthy terms of use, data transfer and the appearance of gratuitousness. In this context, vulnerability is described as a factual situation of accentuated weakness in the face of transactional complexity and the dysfunctionalities proper to cyberspace, capable of reducing both the understanding of risks and the consumer's practical capacity to react Pinto(Furtado; Pinto, 2024, p. 13). The value of the formulation, here, lies in showing that the problem has already been clearly perceived in the contemporary Brazilian consumer-law field. At the same time, it is precisely this descriptive virtue that exposes the dogmatic limit of the label. The same category comes to gather, under a single name, digital medium, form of contracting, data processing, behavioral economics, decision-making inducement and crisis of consent, functioning as an aggregator of multiple risks and asymmetries Pinto(Furtado; Pinto, 2024, p. 12; p. 21). Consequently, the expression proves strong for naming the problematic scenario of platforms, but still less precise for isolating, with maximum sharpness, the legally relevant mechanism of vulnerabilization. It is precisely in this mismatch between good perception of the phenomenon and lesser refinement of the operative key that the critical review of the corpus finds usefulness. The aggravation of vulnerability in the digital environment is not presented merely as a generic abstraction about the internet, but as the result of concrete factors that reorganize the asymmetry between consumer and supplier. Among them appear the technical opacity of the tools, the complexity of the languages and codes used, the user's lack of control over the chain of processing of their data and ignorance of the functionalities actually operating in the service Pinto(Furtado; Pinto, 2024, p. 11-12). The relevance of this description lies in showing that the problem is not exhausted in the mere modernity of the medium, but involves concrete forms of inaccessibility, invisibility and loss of control. The same difficulty deepens when the speed, apparent ease and fluidity of the virtual environment reduce the user's caution and disorganize their perception of risk, favoring little-considered adhesions and little reading of the terms of use Pinto(Furtado; Pinto, 2024, p. 16). The point is important because it already makes it possible to see real mechanisms of vulnerabilization: decision-making acceleration, opaque language, technical invisibility and the weakening of informational control. Even so, all these elements remain gathered under a category strongly dependent on the digital scenario itself, which makes it a good contextual description of the problem, but a still less sharp tool for stabilizing, on its own, the dogmatically most precise form of the asymmetry in question. The critical usefulness of the expression “consumer's digital vulnerability” does not disappear because it presents low maximum refinement as an explanatory category. On the contrary, its recent circulation shows a real effort to extract concrete protective consequences in the consumer-law field, especially when the abusiveness of unrestricted authorization for data processing as a consideration imposed in zero-price models is maintained, even if formally provided for in terms of use Pinto(Furtado; Pinto, 2024, p. 23). The category, therefore, does not remain entirely on the plane of sociological setting: it already seeks to ground contractual control, a protective rereading of consent and the application of the CDC to the structure of the business model. This normative aspiration also appears when the relativization of the binding force of the vulnerable user's will is admitted in the face of the concrete fragility of the situation, with the prevalence of axiological parameters linked to the protection of the person and to the containment of abuses in predisposed clauses Pinto(Furtado; Pinto, 2024, p. 22). Added to this is the approximation with the idea of contracts linked to existential or functionally central needs, which reinforces the demand for a protective reading of the clauses and of the very design of the bond Pinto(Furtado; Pinto, 2024, p. 14-15). The critical balance, then, is not one of the expression's uselessness, but of partial usefulness. It already manages to produce relevant legal consequences, but continues to operate better as a broad aggregating label, stronger for gathering multiple practical problems under one name than for isolating, with maximum methodological precision, the dogmatically specific mechanism of the asymmetry that the present thesis seeks to capture. ↩
  7. Leonardo Machado Acosta's dissertation is useful, at this point, to denote the contrast internal to the recent literature itself that seeks to name the aggravation of consumer vulnerability in digital environments. His contribution is relevant because it shows that the field has already clearly perceived that the classic framework of consumer-law vulnerability proves strained in cyberspace, requiring additional formulations to capture the increased asymmetry between consumer and supplier. Along these lines, the author defines “cybervulnerability” as “the hypervulnerability of the consumer caused by the greater fragility of this subject in consumer relationships established in cyberspace in view of the existence of more uncertainties, risks and occurrences of damage in this environment, which increases the power gap between the consumer and the supplier” Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 86). The formulation has value because it records, without caricature, the contemporary perception that the digital environment intensifies already known fragilities and imposes more robust protective demands. At the same time, Acosta's own conceptual construction helps to highlight the critical point that justifies the present review. Acosta maintains that cybervulnerability stems from the “aggravation of the consumer's vulnerability(ies) caused by the insertion and action of this agent within cyberspace” Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 86), reinforcing that it is a form of hypervulnerability resulting from the sum of vulnerabilities beyond the usual, arising from the characteristics of this environment Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 93). The problem, therefore, was correctly perceived. What remains open is whether the chosen category delivers, with sufficient dogmatic precision, the legally operable mechanism that explains this aggravation. The aggravation of vulnerability in the digital environment need not be described only by broad labels of technological setting; it can be broken down into concrete factors of asymmetry produced by the platform's own design. This is what makes his work useful for identifying concretely which factors aggravate vulnerability, preventing the debate from remaining at a generic level. The author shows that this aggravation does not stem from vague abstraction about “cyberspace”, but from empirically perceptible elements that reorganize the asymmetry between consumer and supplier. Among them stands out the aggravated technical vulnerability associated with two central circumstances: one structural, linked to the very architecture of the virtual environment, and another situational, related to the cognitive and behavioral effects produced in the use of this medium, both responsible for further weakening the consumer in digitally established relationships Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 97). Moreover, he makes explicit other concrete factors that deepen this picture, such as the informational power gap, the lack of digital literacy, the indiscriminate collection and processing of personal data and the manipulation of individuals' will Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 120). In an especially elucidating passage, the author describes that the digital market tends to condition the provision of services on the collection of data, later stored and processed by algorithms aimed at modeling the will, whether through behavioral advertising or through broader forms of decision-making inducement in favor of companies or of other centers of power that control such informational bases Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 83-84). The merit of the formulation lies in demonstrating that recent literature has already precisely perceived several of the material factors that aggravate vulnerability in the digital environment. The critical point, however, remains whether the category chosen to name them manages to translate, with sufficient dogmatic density, the specific mechanism of vulnerabilization that emerges from them. ↩
  8. Leonardo Machado Acosta's reading makes it possible to assess, with some precision, to what extent the category of “cybervulnerability” advances toward the juridification of the problem and to what extent it still remains strongly tied to the technological scenario it seeks to describe. On the one hand, the proposal is not presented as a simple sociological label of the digital environment. The author insists on the need to fix a concept and parameters based on the principles of the Consumer Protection Code and the Federal Constitution, precisely to allow effective protection and guarantee material equality to consumers placed in cyberspace Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 93). There is, therefore, a genuine effort to juridify the category. On the other hand, the very structure of the formulation reveals that this juridification still coexists with a strong dependence on the technological scenario as its organizing axis. This appears when the author recognizes the precariousness, almost non-existence, of normative protection specifically aimed at the “cybervulnerable”, which hinders the direct subsumption of protective rules in the Brazilian legal order and makes the search for provisional interpretive solutions inevitable Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 113). The same tension reappears when he advocates, for the future, specific regulation that recognizes and combats the vulnerabilities aggravated in cyberspace, while, in the present, recourse is had to interpretation favorable to the consumer based on the CDC and the Constitution Acosta(Acosta, 2025, p. 119). The critical balance, thus, is not one of the category's uselessness, but of a formulation that correctly perceives the protective aggravation, although it still retains a marked adherence to the technological scenario as its organizing principle, without isolating with ideal sharpness the specific mechanism of vulnerabilization it seeks to explain. ↩
  9. The perception that the consumer's classic vulnerability proves insufficient to explain asymmetries produced in digital ecosystems already appears clearly in recent literature, including in the field of electronic games. The problem, therefore, is not artificially created by the present thesis, but has already been identified by competing formulations that seek to name the aggravation of the consumer's fragility in platform environments. It is in this key that Azevedo and Torres work with the notion of “situational digital vulnerability”, pointing out that it may assume, among others, a “by captivity” form, linked to relationships that extend over time, and a “neuropsychological” one, caused mainly by the application of behavioral economics and gamification Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 8). The article becomes especially relevant because it brings this diagnosis to the universe of games, showing that, in the very empirical object privileged here, the need for supplementary categories to explain the intensification of vulnerability is already perceived. When dealing with Fortnite, the authors observe that situational digital vulnerability by captivity manifests itself “mainly through the consumer's submission to the platform, which holds the items acquired for use in the development of the game”, to which are added the multiplayer character and the formation of teams as factors of permanence in the environment Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 23). The merit of the reference lies precisely in showing that the problem was well perceived. The critical point, however, remains whether categories of this nature, strongly dependent on the environment and on the public affected, manage to isolate with sufficient precision the dogmatic mechanism of vulnerabilization. The aggravation of vulnerability in the digital environment need not be described only by broad labels of technological setting; it can be broken down into concrete factors of asymmetry produced by the very design of the platform. This is what makes the article by Azevedo and Torres useful for the critical review of the corpus. The authors describe contemporary digital architectures as structures that are “customizable, dynamic and governed by the analysis and interpretation of data”, capable of continuously collecting data, learning from user interactions and changing the environment “at any moment” to secure better results for the supplier through the exploitation of the consumer's vulnerabilities Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 15-16). Here one is no longer merely facing a generic technological scenario, but an operational arrangement that monitors, adjusts and influences conduct. This mechanical dimension appears even more clearly when the authors explain that prolonged use of the service increases the capacity to identify and target vulnerabilities, because continuous data collection allows the design to be adjusted to influence behavior, while captivity makes leaving the platform difficult and builds a kind of “situational monopoly” Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 11). Added to this, in the case of electronic games, is the presence of irregular rewards, sounds, colors and greater graphic immersion as factors of behavioral conditioning Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 21). The article thus has the merit of pointing out real mechanisms of aggravation of vulnerability, even though the chosen category remains strongly anchored in the broad frame of the “situational digital”. ↩
  10. The dogmatic operability of competing categories can also be tested by their capacity to generate concrete legal consequences, and not only by their descriptive aptitude. In this dimension, the formulation of Azevedo and Torres does not remain entirely on the sociological plane, since the authors seek to link it to specific protective responses within the consumer-law and constitutional system. This appears when they argue for the “hypervulnerability of the family unit” through equivalence to the child on the basis of art. 29 of the CDC, treating the family as a collectivity exposed to commercial practices which, although they initially reach minors, radiate effects to the whole family unit and weaken the exercise of parental authority Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 22). The same movement is seen when they associate the platform's practices with concrete duties to contain economic power and to provide integral protection to children and adolescents, calling on the State, society and the family to act in the face of the harm to healthy development produced by such dynamics Torres(Azevedo; Torres, 2023, p. 22- 23). The article, therefore, demonstrates some normative density and capacity to generate real protective consequences. Even so, the way these consequences are triggered reveals that the category remains very dependent on the public affected and on the specific context of incidence, which reinforces its usefulness as a qualified object of critical review, but also exposes the limits of its dogmatic stability as a general category of explanation. ↩
  11. The practical usefulness of categories such as “electronic”, “digital” or “technological vulnerability” appears more clearly when they are mobilized to describe real barriers produced by the compulsory digitalization of services, channels and procedures. At this point, the vocabulary allows concrete exclusions to be identified, above all when technology becomes an obstacle to access to justice, to communication or to the exercise of legally relevant positions Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 3). There is, therefore, some minimal normative yield: the duty of institutional adaptation, inclusion and accessibility. This connection is reinforced when the problem is brought close to the so-called “platform effect”, that is, the concentration of economic growth in a few scalable digital organizations Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 2). The text no longer speaks only of individual technical ignorance, but of structural asymmetries produced by platformized markets. Even so, it is precisely in this test of operability that the limit of the category appears most clearly. The center of the formulation remains tied to technological obstacles, digital environment and access to justice, and not to the rigorous identification of a dogmatically specific mechanism producing asymmetry within the consumer relation. The vocabulary is good for describing a context of exclusion and for justifying institutional accessibility responses. But for reconstructing, with greater precision, the duty breached, the factual basis, the required evidence and the appropriate remedy in digitally mediated consumer conflicts, it remains too atmospheric. ↩
  12. The critical review of recent literature reveals that the field is not organized only around the expression “digital vulnerability”, but also circulates through neighboring formulations such as “electronic vulnerability” and “technological vulnerability”. This terminological multiplication is already, in itself, a symptom of conceptual instability: the problem is clearly perceived, but there is still no sufficiently stable delimitation as to the name of the category or as to its scope. In one of these distinctions, it is argued that technological vulnerability would cover those unskilled in or excluded from the digital world, while digital vulnerability would reach subjects already inserted, in some way, in this scenario Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 4). The critical consequence is clear: the recent competing vocabulary still presents mobile and poorly consolidated boundaries. The dispersion increases when several internal dimensions of this phenomenon are proposed, such as technological, technical, informational, algorithmic and neural or psychological-behavioral vulnerabilities Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 4). The field shows sensitivity to the problem, no doubt, but also reveals a certain classificatory inflation. The picture becomes even more revealing when specific labels appear, such as the so-called “vulnerability by manipulation”, associated with gamification and training on digital platforms, including in gig economy contexts Maia(Maia, 2023, p. 4). Here the material is valuable precisely because it shows the current state of the literature: fertile, intuitive, sensitive to new asymmetries, but semantically dispersed and oscillating between environment, technique, behavior, algorithm and exclusion from access. It is precisely this scenario that reinforces the need for the conceptual refinement undertaken by the present thesis. ↩
  13. The circulation of the expression “digital vulnerability” in fields other than consumption reinforces the perception that it is relevant, current and serious terminology, but semantically broad and dogmatically unstable. In the debate on access to justice, it appears associated with a multifaceted phenomenon ranging from the lack of physical access to the internet and to adequate devices to the absence of minimal digital skills for the use of technologies, with especially severe incidence on the elderly, people with low schooling and socially disadvantaged groups Simões(Simões, 2024, p. 79). The term is mobilized to name a structural condition of exclusion and difficulty of insertion into technologically mediated environments, and not a more delimited legal mechanism of relational asymmetry. This same key is reinforced when the problem is described in terms of barriers to access to justice: even with the gradual expansion of internet access, exclusions persist linked to the precariousness of the connection and to the insufficiency of technical skills, factors that become concrete obstacles to the use of institutionally digitalized channels Simões(Simões, 2024, p. 83). In this frame, so-called digital vulnerability appears as a new obstacle to access, adding to the classic barriers already identified Simões(Simões, 2024, p. 86). The vocabulary describes well a structural scenario of exclusion, but does so at a broad, environmental and transversal level, proving less precise for guiding attribution and evidence within the CDC. ↩
  14. The polysemy of the expression “digital vulnerability” appears even more clearly when it is presented as a new general layer of vulnerability linked to the growth of technological means and to the need for continuous adaptation to an entirely digitalized social life Simões(Simões, 2024, p. 69). The term already emerges, in this framing, less as a delimited dogmatic tool and more as the name of a general condition produced by a comprehensive sociotechnical transformation. This semantic openness expands even further when digital vulnerability is described as a state of predisposition to risk in cyberspaces, favoring inequities, power asymmetries, diminutions of citizenship and violations of privacy and informational self-determination, and is even brought close to the idea of socio-spatial vulnerability Simões(Simões, 2024, p. 79-80). Added to this is the recognition that the problem takes on diverse strands and frequently overlaps with other situations of vulnerability, in intersection with income, gender and race Simões(Simões, 2024, p. 80). The point is very useful here because it shows both the strength and the limit of the competing vocabulary: it sensitively captures a complex environment of exclusions, but tends to function as a broad category of context, less able to isolate the central legal mechanism of asymmetry that the thesis intends to delimit. ↩
  15. The critique of the literature on “digital vulnerability” cannot rest on the trivial fact that many consumer relations today unfold in technically complex contexts; that alone does not yet produce a dogmatic category. What matters, for consumer law, is to verify whether the formulation identifies a mechanism of functional opacity capable of compromising the consumer's understanding, choice and practical self-determination for the benefit of the supplier. It is exactly in this key that the passage mobilized by Wada (2016) offers a more solid criterion. In citing Benjamin, the text shows that, in mass and technically complex operations, the legally relevant datum lies not in the technology itself, but in the supplier's exploitation of the informational asymmetry of the “lay subject”. The problem arises when, faced with consumers not initiated in “complex technical, commercial, financial or tax transactions and operations”, the supplier takes advantage of the “weakness or ignorance of the consumer” to impose the opportunistic logic of “if it sticks, it sticks” Wada(Benjamin apud Wada, 2016, p. 193-194). This makes it possible to assess whether the literature on “digital vulnerability” really isolates an operable informational failure, marked by noise, opacity and the exploitation of ignorance, or whether it merely names, loosely, the technological scenario in which the relation takes place. The usefulness of Ricardo Morishita Wada's thesis, at this point, lies in allowing the consumer's informational vulnerabilization to be described through concrete mechanisms, and not through vague abstractions. Although the work does not fix “noise” or “functional opacity” as autonomous nominal categories, it precisely describes equivalent phenomena by showing that information can be formally provided and, at the same time, materially degraded in its function of clarification, comparison and decision. This can be seen, for example, when the structure of the offer reduces transparency and makes comparability unfeasible. In dealing with packages of banking products and services, Wada observes that the aggregation of multiple items “reduces price transparency”, hindering the comparison of offers, and that it was “very difficult for the consumer to compare the benefit packages offered by banks” Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 168). The information exists, but its form of presentation neutralizes its practical usefulness. The same logic appears when the author deals with the precariousness of the channel and the informational deficit as concrete instruments for exploiting vulnerability, noting that the “precariousness of verbal information may compromise the consumer's understanding” and proves incompatible with the regime of transparency, information and balance required by the CDC Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 213-214). In this key, noise, functional opacity and informational inadequacy cease to be convenient metaphors and come to designate concrete ways in which defective communication compromises understanding, comparability and practical freedom of choice. ↩
