7.1.1.8 On the parameters of evidence and dynamic distribution of the burden in an environment of structural opacity

See this section in the PDF

  • In practice · Parameters

p. 734

The effective application of the regime of the Immaterial Object of Consumption depends on an evidentiary discipline compatible with the contemporary way supply is organized. In platform-governed ecosystems, the asymmetry falls not only on information in the material sense or on abstract economic capacity. It reaches the very terrain of the institutional narrability of the conflict. Whoever controls logs, histories, decision criteria, relevant versions, interface records, moderation flows, alteration parameters and traces of sanction controls, to a large extent, the conditions for legally reconstructing what occurred. Evidence, in this context, is not an element external to the relationship. It is part of the way the relationship is governed. For this reason, the structural opacity of the environment cannot be treated as a p. 735 mere neutral fact of technology or as an evidentiary inconvenience to be borne entirely by the consumer.

This point shifts the discussion away from a procedural imaginary still excessively tied to the model of the observable event and the symmetrically accessible document. In many conflicts concerning immaterial objects of consumption, the disputed fact is born, transforms and remains within a technical infrastructure controlled by the supplier. The relevant alteration does not manifest itself as an easily graspable public occurrence. The sanction may be automated or partially automated. The reconfiguration of the object may occur without a sufficient public trail. The essential information may be distributed among internal systems, databases, version records and curation criteria inaccessible to the consumer. In these scenarios, demanding exhaustive evidence from the vulnerable party as if the conditions of production of the litigation were symmetrical means, in practice, rewarding private control of obscurity.

That is why the preservation and disclosure of minimal records take on a central legal status here. Interface trails, histories of relevant versions, essential alteration logs, sanction records, minimal data on automated or assisted decisions, contextual elements of the modulation of enjoyment and other documentary layers indispensable to reconstructing the conflict cannot be understood as business liberality or as a procedural favor granted to the consumer. In environments of governed enjoyment, verifiability forms part of the very duty to shape the relationship legitimately. When the platform administers the experience and at the same time concentrates the traces needed to prove it, the preservation of these elements ceases to be a peripheral question of cooperation and comes to constitute a condition of institutional controllability.

The consequence of this is that a dynamic distribution of the burden of proof is admissible whenever the relevant evidence is structurally under the supplier's technical control. It is not a matter of dramatizing exceptions or of abolishing criteria of procedural rationality. It is a matter of recognizing that formal equality of burdens may operate, in these cases, as a mechanism of material inequality. If the consumer was placed in a scenario of impossible or disproportionately difficult proof because the architecture of the environment concentrates, conceals or volatilizes the relevant elements of the conflict, the evidentiary discipline must react to this asymmetry. Redistributing the burden does not represent anti-technical benevolence. It represents fitting the proceedings to the real design of the consumer relationship.

This parameter thereby avoids one of the most corrosive defenses of structural opacity, which is turning the consumer's lack of access into an absence of proof of the very right claimed. The formula “you did not prove it” becomes institutionally empty when one does not p. 736 examine who controlled the conditions for producing, preserving and disclosing what was to be proven. In environments of this nature, the absence of evidence may be less an expression of the weakness of the claim than a result of the supplier's own governance arrangement. Law cannot accept that the platform should simultaneously administer enjoyment, the narrative of the event and the conditions of its verifiability, turning this triple concentration into a self-legitimating procedural advantage.

The evidentiary discipline proposed here is also important because it prevents standards such as open auditability and functional value equivalence from turning into mere conceptual rhetoric. Without a compatible evidentiary regime, these parameters would remain correct in theory, but ineffective in practice. It is not enough to assert that relevant alterations must be intelligible or that enjoyment must remain functionally equivalent. There must be instruments to demonstrate, contest and reconstruct the occurrence of modulations, losses, reconfigurations and sanctions. Evidence, therefore, is not a later and accessory stage of the reasoning. It is a means of efficacy of the regime itself. For this reason, its organization must be conceived from within the dogmatics of the IOC, and not as a secondary problem to be resolved only at the abstract procedural level.

The dynamic distribution of burdens must, in this context, respond to the concrete architecture of the case. The more intense the private concentration of records, the greater the requirement of preservation, delimited disclosure and verifiable justification. The more the reconstruction of the conflict depends on internal governance, the less reasonable it will be to demand from the consumer a detailed demonstration of facts whose documentary and technical support was never within their reach. This does not eliminate the need for plausibility, a chain of argument or delimitation of the disputed object. What it eliminates is the fiction of symmetry in environments whose very structure operates through the asymmetry of meaning and of evidence.

This parameter also has a preventive function. By recognizing that essential records form part of the controllability of the relationship, it encourages the institutional organization of minimal reconstruction trails, discouraging governance models based on the convenient volatilization of the system's memory or on selectively insufficient documentation. The platform that profits from the continuous administration of enjoyment cannot organize this administration in a way that makes unrecoverable, indeterminable or institutionally opaque what will later condition protection. The unjustified failure to preserve or disclose relevant records must not, for this reason, be treated as a mere neutral evidentiary inconvenience. It may authorize presumptions favorable to the consumer and reinforce the attribution of inadequacy to the supply regime.

p. 737 At the dogmatic level, the result is clear. In environments of structural opacity, evidence must be read as an internal dimension of the governance of the immaterial object of consumption. The platform does not only supply, alter and sanction. It also conditions the ways in which these operations can be seen, narrated and verified. Recognizing this is a condition for the CDC to retain its efficacy in ecosystems of high technical complexity. Without this recognition, consumer protection runs the risk of remaining formally proclaimed but materially neutralized by architectures that concentrate power not only over enjoyment, but over the very possibility of proving that it was degraded, obscured or abusively governed.
