6.3.1 On the legal concept of open auditability in continuous digital services

See this section in the PDF

Concepts in this section

  • Open auditability

p. 639

In continuous digital services, open auditability designates a standard of technically qualified and institutionally usable transparency, enforceable whenever p. 640 the supplier makes relevant changes, imposes sanctions or carries out reconfigurations capable of reaching the core of enjoyment of the Immaterial Object of Consumption. This is not a demand for total transparency, nor for unrestricted opening of the technical infrastructure, nor an obligation to expose source code, the entire architecture of the system or sensitive trade secrets. The point lies elsewhere. What is required is a verifiable minimum that makes it possible to reconstruct, in legally useful terms, what was changed, when it was changed, why it was changed and with what relevant effects on the consumer's enjoyment. Without this minimum, the continuous mutability of the service tends to turn into opaque power, and opacity, in turn, becomes a kind of informal shield against duties of information, good faith and control of abusiveness. Instead of real transparency, there is only a documentary staging of compliance, a situation incompatible with the standard of adequate and conspicuous information required by the CDC722.

This formulation springs neither from conceptual whim nor from fascination with technological regulation. It follows from the very structure of continuous supply. In governed digital services, performance is not exhausted by a fully completed initial delivery. The bond extends over time through updates, fixes, rebalancings, interface reconfigurations, rule changes, technical migrations, restrictions of use, compatibility changes and, not infrequently, sanctions affecting access, visibility and permanence. In such an environment, treating performance as if it were a neutral, empty or legally invisible space would be far too crude. In relationships of this kind, objective good faith projects continuous duties of information, cooperation, fairness and protection of trust, so that the supplier cannot keep for itself all the intelligibility of the process by which the service changes and leave the consumer with only the final result already imposed723.

p. 641 For this reason, open auditability must be understood as an internal development of the duty to inform and not as an institution foreign to the microsystem. Under the CDC, information does not fulfil its function just because it exists somewhere, nor because it was deposited in lengthy documents, cross-references, FAQs or update logs that the consumer is unlikely to find, understand and connect to the actual impact suffered. What matters is its usefulness for reconstruction and decision. If the supplier changes obligations, parameters of use, compatibility conditions or central elements of enjoyment without providing a minimally intelligible basis for reconstructing what happened, it does not merely communicate badly. It prevents control. And preventing control, in a context of strong technical asymmetry, is a material way of worsening the consumer's very position within the relationship724.

The critique of merely performative transparency takes on its own weight here. In many digital services, the company produces a significant volume of information, but distributes it in a flow, form and hierarchy that make it hard to perceive what is essential. The change exists, the notice exists, the document exists, the log exists, but a useful reconstruction of the event does not. Consumers know that “something changed”, yet cannot discern precisely what changed, what criterion was adopted, what practical effects follow and how to demonstrate the impact suffered before an institution. Transparency then appears as a performance of regularity, not as a real condition of verifiability. This is precisely why open auditability becomes necessary. It does not require knowing everything. It requires knowing enough that the change in supply does not remain shielded by the very way it is presented725.

p. 642 This standard also rests on a decisive evidentiary premise. In the universe of the IOC, the platform concentrates records, histories, logs, enforcement criteria, relevant versions, transactional data, communications actually sent, change parameters and enough technical documentation to narrate the event unilaterally. If the legal system accepts that all this information remain entirely under the supplier's narrative monopoly, consumer protection withers. Facilitating the defense and reallocating the burden of proof cease to be occasional instruments and become a condition for the minimum effectiveness of the protective regime itself. At this point, open auditability works as a technique for reducing informational and evidentiary asymmetry. It puts the conflict back on reconstructible ground and prevents the lack of access to evidence from turning into a practical impossibility of protection726.

The trigger for application, however, must be rigorously delimited so that the standard does not slide into maximalism. Open auditability cannot be demanded in the face of every microscopic adjustment, cosmetic change or irrelevant fluctuation of the service. Its proper field begins when the change, unilateral modulation, suppression, migration, nerf, discontinuation, reconfiguration of a rule or interface, or even a sanction applied to the user, significantly affects enjoyment. This relevance can appear in several ways. It can affect access and permanence, the functional integrity of the object, promised equivalences, the symbolic position of the IOC in the ecosystem, or even the consumer's conditions for proof and challenge. The central point is that the change must be capable of downgrading enjoyment, frustrating a legitimate expectation or abusively concentrating the intelligibility of what happened in the supplier's hands.

p. 643 In such cases, the requirement of open auditability takes the form of a minimum content far more modest than the caricature of “total openness”, but far more robust than corporate self-justification. The supplier must make available, in an institutionally useful way, an intelligible record of what was changed and when; a minimum technical justification, that is, the declared rationale and stated objective of the change; a description of the relevant impacts on enjoyment and on the parameters of the IOC; preservation of verifiable trails, such as histories of relevant versions and minimum event, transaction or change logs; and, in cases of enforcement, minimally reconstructible criteria for the sanction applied. In addition, verification by qualified third parties must be possible, including by protection bodies, expert examination or judicial review, without the regime of technical secrecy being used as an absolute screen against any scrutiny727.

This point matters because the corporate objection usually comes in predictable dress: requiring auditability would amount to requiring the opening of code, the surrender of trade secrets, the exposure of know-how or the loss of software protection. That is not what is at stake. The legal order already lives, in various contexts, with limited forms of scrutiny compatible with preserving the informational asset. Judicial secrecy, restricted expert access, delimited functional analysis, technically necessary use for certain purposes and institutional control do not demolish exclusivity. They are legally established ways of reconciling protection of the asset with the need for verification. Software protection does not turn software into a sacred black box. Nor does the legal regime of computer programs make the supplier sovereign over each and every legally relevant consequence of its operation728.

p. 644 Along the same lines, the protection of trade secrets does not operate as an absolute veto on external control. Generically invoking “secrecy” or “technical sensitivity” without delimiting what actually needs to remain confidential and without admitting any form of institutionally relevant reconstruction is not enough. For secrecy to be legally respected, it must be identified and carved out, not treated as a magic fog that dissolves every demand for accountability. The system does, of course, allow strategic business information to be protected. What it should not allow is for that protection to be used to prevent entirely the reconstruction of the legally relevant effects of the supply, above all when the very lawfulness of the conduct depends on that reconstruction. The company may still protect its secret. What it cannot do is claim secrecy over the very impact of the change it imposed on the consumer729.

This is all the more evident because technical exclusivity, in itself, does not rule out institutional examination of the system's operational effects. What matters to Consumer Law is not, as a rule, a full contemplation of the program's internal ingenuity, but the possibility of verifying how a given operating regime, a given change or a given criterion applied affected the enjoyment, continuity, equivalence or position of the consumer in the ecosystem. The legally relevant examination concerns operation for purposes of control, not parasitic appropriation of the asset. For this reason, the existence of a secret or of protected software does not exclude limited forms of technical, expert and institutionally mediated analysis. Open auditability does not compete with exclusivity. It limits the abusive use of exclusivity as an excuse for irresponsibility730.

p. 645 The dogmatic function of the standard thus becomes clearer. Open auditability is a legal technology of controllability. It reduces the informational and evidentiary asymmetry inherent in the private governance of the IOC and brings continuous mutability back into a minimally verifiable field. This allows quality defect, inadequacy, abusiveness, breach of the duty to inform and breach of trust to be discussed on a reconstructible basis, and not in a crude clash between the corporate narrative and the consumer's subjective impression. Without this standard, the supplier can always claim that it made the change for technical reasons, that it informed as best it could, that the impact was small, that the change was provided for in some document and that the consumer simply did not understand or did not like it. With it, it becomes possible to ask seriously what was changed, on what basis, with what effect and with what objective trails for reconstruction.

This gain is of enormous importance in long-term contracts. In relationships of continuous performance, the consumer places trust not only in an initial utility, but in the minimally intelligible permanence of the bond and in the possibility of understanding its relevant changes. A long-term relationship requires cooperation, adaptation and preservation of the legitimate expectations formed over the course of the contract. It is therefore not enough to keep the contract alive in name while its performance becomes opaque and unilaterally reconfigurable in non-reconstructible terms. Consumer trust is not protected by slogans of continuous evolution or by a generic promise of “service improvements”. It requires a minimum regime of verifiable continuity of information about the performance of the bond731.

For that very reason, open auditability is also connected to the mechanisms of specific relief. The CDC was not designed to let the supplier turn the injury into a mere accounting cost and move on. When the relevant change in supply depends on records, versions, criteria and documentation concentrated in the company's hands, the system already has instruments suited to disclosure, early production of evidence, p. 646 specific relief and measures aimed at reconstructing the bond as actually performed. This is especially important in continuous digital services, in which the abstract contractual instrument is almost never enough to reveal the actual content of the performance. Without access to the relevant versions, the performance records and the elements showing the supply actually provided, the legal discussion risks being held hostage by the documentary fiction of the base contract, which often says little about the real life of the service732.

Hence the legal consequence of non-auditability. When, in the face of a relevant change, the supplier does not provide the auditable minimum compatible with the consumer relationship, transparency must be treated as inadequate. And, depending on the intensity of the case, this inadequacy may function as evidence or as a direct manifestation of structural abusiveness of the supply regime. This is not a peripheral lack of informational courtesy. It is the frustration of a duty essential to the controllability of the bond. In practical terms, this legitimizes the imposition of obligations to act aimed at preserving and disclosing records, the dynamic reallocation of the burden of proof, the adoption of presumptions in the consumer's favor in the face of an unjustified refusal to cooperate and review of the validity or effectiveness of clauses and policies that seek to shield non-verifiable changes. Where a relevant change cannot be minimally reconstructed, private governance has already entered a legally suspect zone.

This effect is all the more important because the contemporary problem is not only the absence of information, but its strategic organization. The platform can flood the consumer with announcements, changelogs, banners, dialog boxes, policies, disclaimers and FAQs, and still keep for itself an effective monopoly over the intelligibility of what matters. Open auditability appears precisely to break this false sufficiency. It is not enough to announce that something changed. The meaning of the change for the enjoyment consumed must be made institutionally reconstructible. Without this, the mutability of the service and the technical concentration of records together produce a kind of private evidentiary fortress.

p. 647 This is also why the standard prepares the next stage of the section. On its own, open auditability does not settle the whole controversy, because demonstrating what changed and how does not exhaust the question of the change's material legitimacy. It will still be necessary to assess whether or not the change preserved the substance of the legitimately acquired enjoyment. But without open auditability this judgment becomes fragile, because there is no basis for reconstructing the change itself. The standard thus functions as an institutional condition of intelligibility for the subsequent examination of functional value equivalence. First, the change is reconstructed. Then its material legitimacy is measured. This order matters. Without it, the supplier will always have the advantage of the fog.

At the methodological level, open auditability plays a role similar to the one semiotic vulnerability played earlier. It turns a structural diagnosis into a dogmatic operator. It is no longer said only that the platform controls the environment, the signs, the metrics and the proof. In the face of this concentration, a verifiable minimum of reconstruction of the supply comes to be required. It is no longer merely asserted that transparency can be performative. A criterion becomes available for separating useful transparency from scenographic transparency. The opacity of private governance is no longer merely denounced. It comes to be qualified as a controllable legal insufficiency.

For this reason, open auditability must be understood as a criterion of legitimacy for platform architectures, and not as abstract hostility toward them. Platforms may innovate, update, fix, rebalance, migrate and protect their technical assets. What they cannot do is so act under a regime of institutional non-reconstructibility, as if the very complexity of the system entitled them to decide alone what matters, what was changed and what may or may not be known by those who enjoy the service and by those who exercise legal control over it. In relationships of continuous consumption, complexity does not revoke good faith. Technique does not suspend the duty to inform. And trade secrecy, though legitimate, does not release the company from the obligation to allow the supply to be minimally reconstituted when its changes significantly affect the consumer's enjoyment733.

p. 648 In the end, this is the core of the legal concept of open auditability in continuous digital services. A standard of minimum verifiability, compatible with the protection of software and business secrecy, but incompatible with the opaque shielding of performance; a development of the duty to inform, of objective good faith and of the facilitation of the consumer's defense; a criterion for reducing evidentiary and informational asymmetry; and an institutional condition for relevant changes to the IOC to stop being accepted as faits accomplis narrated only by the supplier itself. Without this standard, private governance comes too close to practical sovereignty. With it, the mutability of the service remains possible, but no longer hovers above legal control.

Notes

  1. Open auditability can be understood as an internal development of a logic already present in the consumer-law microsystem, and not as an exotic regulatory graft. The starting point lies in the very standard of adequate information required by the CDC, which is not satisfied by the mere formal provision of data, but calls for content that is at once complete, free of charge and useful, and forbids diluting relevant communication with scattered, redundant or useless information for the consumer. The Manual sums up this standard by requiring “correct”, “clear”, “precise” and “conspicuous” information Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 128]). The same orientation is reinforced where the work notes the “need to guarantee the consumer the possibility of a priori control of the contract”, since the possibility of control only a posteriori is not enough, on pain of violating the right to adequate information Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 129]). In addition, clear and adequate information governs the pre-contractual moment, the conclusion of the contract, the contract itself and the post-contractual moment, affecting “the essence of the deal”, since it forms part of the contractual content and, if it fails, may amount to a quality defect in the product or service offered Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 126]). ↩
  2. In continuous digital services, the notion of open auditability can be legally traced back to the relational duties arising from objective good faith throughout the performance of the contract. In bonds marked by functional mutability, successive updates and technical reconfigurations capable of affecting the consumer's enjoyment, it is incompatible with the CDC to treat performance as an opaque zone, immune to duties of intelligible reconstruction of what was changed, kept, restricted or removed. The time lapse between the birth of the obligation and the satisfaction of the main performance “can no longer be seen as an ‘empty space’”, since the obligational relationship constitutes “a continuous whole”, within which the parties are bound by ancillary duties of conduct arising from objective good faith, among them “the duty to inform, to cooperate, duty of care, of secrecy, of advice, of fairness etc.” Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 110). Breaching the duty of cooperation and fairness means defaulting, even if partially, including when the supplier hinders the consumer's access to their rights Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 236). ↩
  3. Protective information only fulfils its function when it enables the consumer to reconstruct the supply usefully and exercise effective control over its performance. This is not a technicist obsession with total openness. What matters, from a consumer-law standpoint, is that the information provided be sufficient to allow monitoring, understanding of the relevant milestones of the performance and verification of its conformity. The very existence of information shifts the problem to the level of implementation and control, insofar as it “places the burden of implementation on informed consumers”, as well as on the market forces that seek to avoid exposure of illegality Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 82). Failing to state the deadline for performance, or leaving it to be defined later under the supplier's exclusive control, places the consumer in a situation of extreme vulnerability, since without an intelligible reconstruction of how the obligation is performed the consumer loses the capacity for control and the supplier comes to dictate the rules of the game unilaterally Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 208). Prior information and express authorization fulfil a rebalancing function: they do not merely communicate, but make the performance conditional on a minimum form of verifiability by the recipient, offsetting the structural imbalance of the consumer relationship Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 210). ↩
  4. The critique of merely formal transparency leads directly to the requirement of minimum verifiability over relevant changes in, and impacts of, the supply. The mere existence of an initial document or an abstract statement of the terms of the deal is not enough to satisfy the protective standard of the CDC if the consumer cannot control substantial modifications of the performance. Wada makes this explicit in stating that there is abuse when there is an “alteration of the obligations contained in the estimate without the consumer's agreement” Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 55). Protection therefore requires more than the formal display of content: it requires a real possibility of verifying relevant changes and their effects before they produce the consumer's material subjection. The same reasoning is reinforced when the author associates abusiveness with the element of surprise and the lack of understanding of financial impacts, showing that an apparently transparent relationship can still be unlawful if it holds unexpected conditions in store for the consumer, since surprise prevents effective control over the scope of the commitment undertaken Wada(Wada, 2016, p. 120; p. 142). ↩
  5. Under the CDC, facilitation of the consumer's defense and evidentiary protection are not mere procedural benevolence, but structural instruments for the effectiveness of the microsystem itself. The Manual clearly observes that the right provided for in Article 6, item VIII, although aimed at facilitating the consumer's defense in court, is not merely procedural in nature: it is a substantive right aimed at effective protection and compensation for the damage suffered Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 146]). The consequence is decisive: when the informational or technical structure of the supply hinders the reconstruction of the relevant facts, the system already has its own mechanisms to reallocate the evidentiary burden and prevent the supplier's opacity from turning into practical immunity. The Manual also stresses that the spirit of the CDC is precisely to facilitate the defense of consumer rights, and not to require the vulnerable layperson to prove elements that belong to the supplier's professional risk Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 147]). Further, the work recalls that the reversal of the burden of proof works as an indispensable instrument of distributive justice and of redistribution of the risks inherent in consumer society Bessa(Benjamin; Marques; Bessa, 2021, [p. 276]). ↩
  6. Open auditability can be translated into dogmatic language recognizable by the CDC by articulating the duty of real clarification, the duty of adequate information, the supplier's burden of proof and the control of abusiveness. The duty to inform is a duty to clarify matters for the consumer, and not a mere formal transfer of content. Adequate information about the relevant elements of the performance serves as a criterion for assessing whether the supplier exercised its contractual freedom properly; its breach may reveal abusiveness, impair the quality of consent and even bring Article 46 of the CDC into play. The burden of proving that this duty was actually fulfilled lies with the supplier itself Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 412). Where there is greater risk without sufficiently conspicuous information, an informational defect arises, and the absence of adequate clarification prevents the consumer from taking the necessary precautions. Even when the supplier seeks to exclude its liability, it will be for the supplier to prove that there was no defect, by express statutory allocation of the burden of proof Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 488). ↩
  7. The requirement of open auditability is not to be confused with full disclosure of source code or with the suppression of software's technical secrecy. The legal order already recognizes forms of institutional scrutiny compatible with preserving the informational asset and the secrets of the infrastructure. The legal framework itself provides for judicial secrecy, which restricts third-party access to sensitive information, such as source code, and prevents the opposing party from using it for purposes unrelated to the litigation Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 1940). The statutory limitation allows the use of information and the translation of the program to the extent technically indispensable to the user's needs, for the exclusive use of whoever performed the act, regardless of the right holder's authorization, precisely to enable integration between systems Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 1957). Likewise, administrative formalities cannot go beyond the conditions under which the object of the registration may remain secret Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 1963). ↩
  8. The protection of trade secrets is not to be confused with an absolute veto on external scrutiny of the legally relevant effects of the supply. A generic invocation of secrecy is not enough to block institutional control when the very claim for protection depends on a minimally controllable verification of the protected object. For the case to be submitted to judicial review, “it is necessary to unveil and identify which secret would have been disclosed”, since it is not enough to claim broadly that it is a secret related to the company's field of activity, because such a sweeping assertion does not allow effective judicial control of the dispute Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 392). The possibility of defense and judicial analysis are compromised if it is not known for certain what has already been made public and what would remain beyond the knowledge of third parties Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 392). Trade secrecy prevents unrestricted exposure, but does not exclude minimum requirements of institutionally relevant reconstruction regarding changes, criteria or impacts whose legal relevance calls for external control. ↩
  9. The legal protection of computer programs does not turn them into a black box immune to any institutional scrutiny of the legally relevant effects of their operation. A program claimed in necessary connection with a specific system can be examined for novelty and inventive step as a product, provided a specific technical effect is identified, and it is precisely the “mode and purpose of this operation” that is the object of the corresponding analysis Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 1999). Software protection does not exclude institutional examination of its operational effects when this proves relevant to the application of the governing legal regime. The Brazilian constitutional system attends to the peculiarity of computer programs by reconciling the demands of social function with their value or purpose, precisely because what is protected is not only abstract communication, but “the practical action” of the program Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 1899). With regard to registration, the competent agency, upon finding a breach of a rule whose enforcement is not its responsibility, must at least prompt action by the competent body to restore the legal balance Barbosa(Barbosa, 2017, p. 1963). ↩
  10. In relationships of continuing performance, preserving the consumer's trust requires informational and relational conduct consistent with the long-lasting structure of the bond itself. Neither the initial performance nor the mere formal maintenance of the contract is enough; performance must unfold cooperatively, adaptively and loyally, so as not to frustrate the legitimate expectations that justified the consumer's joining and remaining in the relationship. The long-term contract “carries within it expectations other than those of contracts of immediate performance”, since it rests “more on trust, on repeated interaction, on the maintenance of economic potential and of the quality of services”, which is why “the capacity for adaptation, for cooperation between contracting parties, for continuation of the contractual relationship” is essential Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 106). The theory of the relational contract contributes to “a new understanding of the trust awakened by the activity of suppliers” and to the acceptance of constant readjustment of long-term relationships “so as not to frustrate the legitimate expectations of the parties” Marques(Marques, 2016, p. 101). ↩
  11. The CDC's enforcement mechanisms are already compatible with the control of relevant alterations of the performance, including through specific relief and instruments for the disclosure and production of evidence. Specific performance of the obligation takes precedence precisely to prevent the supplier from turning non-performance into an advantageous economic calculation, converting injuries into damages as an ordinary cost of doing business. For this reason, conversion into compensation should occur only at the consumer's option or when specific relief or an equivalent practical result is impossible Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 898). This design ties in with preliminary measures for the early production of evidence, notably disclosure actions, especially in disputes in which the contract document was not delivered to the consumer or in which the actual content of the bond must be reconstructed Miragem(Miragem, 2020, p. 903). There is no serious control of relevant changes in the performance without access to the versions of the contract, to the records of performance and to the elements that allow the supply actually provided to be reconstructed. ↩
  12. The protection of strategic business information coexists with limited forms of technical reconstruction when this proves indispensable to legally recognized purposes. The protection of trade secrets, software or other strategic elements does not eliminate legitimate room for functionally justified verifiability. “Decompilation is free in Brazilian law”, whenever indispensable for the purposes of integration Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 172). Informational exclusivity does not operate as an absolute bar to the knowledge functionally necessary for compatibility, operational continuity or interaction between systems. Expert analysis in trade secret disputes includes inquiry into possible independent development, reverse engineering or another distinct source as causes capable of explaining the disputed elements Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 394). Several legal systems carve out exceptions to exclusivity to prevent it from being used as a barrier to interconnection, rendering the powers of the exclusive right unenforceable against specific uses necessary for systems to fit together and operate jointly Barbosa(Barbosa, 2018, p. 173). ↩
