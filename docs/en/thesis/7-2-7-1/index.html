7.2.7.1 On governance over rumors in hypercuratorial anticipation

See this section in the PDF

This section in the layers of the site

  • Platforms · Subcase of trust administration

p. 783

The recurring circulation of rumors, leaks and unofficial anticipations about skins, champions, events and reconfigurations of the game environment makes it possible to identify, in the Riot Games ecosystem, an especially relevant zone of private administration of expectation that is not exhausted by the platform's formal communication. The legally p. 784 relevant point does not lie in asserting, simplistically, that all unofficial information must be attributed directly to the company, nor in presuming absolute control over any parallel circulation of signs within the community. The problem is a different one. It is a matter of recognizing that, in ecosystems of continuous enjoyment, the consumer's expectation can also be organized in a functionally effective way through informal channels, provided that these channels stabilize in the environment as predictable circuits of anticipation, desire and preparation of economic conduct. In that case, the formal announcement ceases to be the only relevant center for shaping trust.

Hypercuratorial anticipation, already delimited as a mechanism for governing the present and the future of enjoyment, takes on a specific form here. It does not operate only through roadmaps, teasers, trailers, dev blogs, seasons or express announcements. It can also operate through unofficial circuits of curated rumor, in which partial images, technical references, fragmentary leaks, presumed calendars and speculations reputed plausible are organized by leakers, influencers and channels specialized in narratives of a consumable future. The rumor, in this context, does not remain mere noise external to the supply. It comes to perform an ordering function, because it introduces a temporality of pre-enjoyment in which the user ranks expectations, postpones present decisions, reserves resources and reorganizes their economy of desire on the basis of unconfirmed signals that are nonetheless socially stabilized as relevant.

The essential aspect does not lie in the abstract existence of anticipation, but in the economic productivity of its unofficial operationalization. When the content circulates without official confirmation, the platform preserves a margin of plausible deniability and strategic flexibility to reformulate schedules, alter products, recalibrate offers or simply harvest reactions without fully assuming, in communicational terms, what was anticipated. At the same time, the expectation stays in circulation, engagement intensifies and the community remains in a state of interpretive readiness. An asymmetric arrangement is thus produced in which the semiotic and economic dividends of anticipation can be captured without proportional absorption of the legal and communicational burdens of the formal announcement. Ambiguity then ceases to be a mere transparency deficit and comes to function as a potential technique for governing expectation.

This dynamic is especially relevant in Immaterial Objects of Consumption because, in them, the will to consume is not formed only at the classical instant of the express offer. It is built in a prolonged ambience of signs, promises, updates and reconfigurations, in which the user learns to wait, to interpret, to bet and to position themselves in the face of possible futures of enjoyment. In such environments, consumption is preceded by a temporal p. 785 economy of expectation. One does not buy only the available object. One also buys on account of the anticipated object, the sensed event, the awaited skin, the imagined update. The future comes to organize the present of consumption. That is why stabilized and functionally exploitable rumors can form part, even if obliquely, of the effective governance of the ecosystem.

Influencers and specialized intermediaries perform a particularly relevant function in this process. They are a mass of agents responsible for converting scattered technical data into an emotionally intelligible narrative, stabilizing leaks as plausible promise, transforming fragmentary material into a horizon of expectation and thereby keeping the community in continuous mobilization around what has not yet been officially made available by the developer. Their role is not merely informative. It is also semiotic and economic. They produce interpretive cohesion around the rumor and, with that, allow ambiguous signs to begin influencing spending plans, purchase priority, willingness to wait and the ranking of interest within the ecosystem. The rumor then ceases to be mere community talk and comes to function as an informal infrastructure for administering desire.

The dogmatic usefulness of the point lies in showing that, in contexts of hypercuratorial anticipation, the consumer's trust can be materially shaped outside the traditional limits of the formally delimited offer. This aggravates semiotic vulnerability, because the user comes to decide on the basis of signs that are at once effective and displaced. Effective, because they guide real economic conduct. Displaced, because they remain formally unassumed, hindering direct attribution of liability and making it harder to reconstruct the link between the administration of expectation and the practical impact on enjoyment. The environment thus produces a sophisticated form of strategic opacity. The sign circulates with enough social force to govern decisions, but with enough legal ambiguity to protect, at least in part, whoever benefits from its effectiveness.

The hypothesis upheld here does not depend on proving the platform's absolute dominion over all informal circulation. That objection, although frequent, misses the target. The legal problem does not lie in demanding full control of the origin of each leak, each channel or each speculation. It lies in recognizing that, once a specific, repeated expectation materially apt to guide consumption decisions has settled in the ecosystem, the platform no longer remains in a neutral position. It can administer that expectation through strategic silence, calculated evasion, categorical denial, later incorporation of the anticipated content or simple functional tolerance of the circulation that keeps the environment in a state of engagement and readiness. What matters, therefore, is not absolute sovereignty over the birth of the sign, but p. 786 responsibility for managing the expectation once it has come to form, in a relevant way, part of the consumer's decision-making economy.

From this perspective, it becomes possible to consider a normative consequence proportional to the real limits of the platform's governance. It is not a matter of imposing an impracticable duty to prevent every rumor, every leak or every community speculation. Nor is it a matter of turning the company into a universal certifier of hearsay. What can be demanded, in a key compatible with the IOC regime, is something more delimited and institutionally defensible: a duty of clarification, non-contradiction and justification when ecosystemically relevant rumors, endowed with sufficient social credibility and functionally apt to influence spending plans, purchase priority or the wait for enjoyment, are the object of specific prompting by users or become persistently integrated into the consumption environment. Omission, in this framework, ceases to be a simple neutral communicational choice and may become a relevant informational insufficiency in a context of operative expectation.

This framing makes it possible to distinguish dogmatically different situations. The first is that of silence or an evasive answer in the face of an expectation already stabilized and economically influential. In it, the problem may lie in the strategic refusal to administer information materially relevant to the consumer's planning. The second is that of a specific denial later contradicted by a substantially equivalent practice of the platform itself. In that case, the contradiction between communication and conduct may acquire its own weight for the examination of deceptiveness and of legitimately formed trust. The third is that of the confirmation or positive inducement of an expectation later frustrated without proportional clarification. Here, the question is no longer limited to the absence of delivery, but reaches a reinforced duty of transparency, justification and possible restoration compatible with the consolidated trust. In all these situations, the point is not to punish the mutability proper to continuous digital services, but to prevent their structural flexibility from being converted into communicational immunity.

The theoretical gain of this shift is significant. It shows that, in IOCs, consumer protection cannot remain hostage to the classical model of an offer that is formally delimited, instantly verifiable and externally separable from the community ambience that precedes it. In ecosystems of continuous enjoyment, trust begins to be produced before the express announcement and outside the traditional limits of the isolated advertising piece. What is governed is not only the final purchase, but the temporality of waiting, the reserve of desire, the willingness to postpone, the affective economy of the future. When the platform exploits, tolerates or administers gray zones of socially curated expectation, liability tends to p. 787 fall less on the isolated origin of each sign and more on the way private governance responds to its practical effectiveness in organizing the consumer's conduct.

Thus, in ecosystems of continuous enjoyment, strategic opacity is not only a passive transparency deficit. It may be an active technique for the economic exploitation of ambiguity. Therefore, the control of the IOC must also reach the informalized zones of administration of expectation, whenever they cease to be spontaneous community noise and come to form, functionally, part of the governance of desire, waiting and consumption planning. The rumor, when it stabilizes as a social infrastructure of pre-enjoyment, is no longer outside the legal problem. It becomes one of its subtlest and most dogmatically demanding forms.
